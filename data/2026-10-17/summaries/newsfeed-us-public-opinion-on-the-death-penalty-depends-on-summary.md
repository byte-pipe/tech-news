# U.S. public opinion on the death penalty depends on details

## Overview
- Public sentiment about capital punishment has resurfaced after President Trump’s brief endorsement, then reversal, of a livestreamed firing‑squad execution.  
- The issue is highlighted by recent botched lethal‑injection attempts in Tennessee and the planned execution of former Army psychiatrist Nidal Hasan.

## Poll findings
- **General support:** About two‑thirds of Americans back the death penalty for murder (Pew Research, summer 2026).  
- **Nuanced views:**  
  - Support drops to ~60 % when the alternative is life without parole.  
  - Support falls further when monetary compensation for victims’ families is offered.  
- **Trend:** Gallup’s 2025 poll shows 52 % support for the death penalty for murder, the lowest level in more than 50 years.  
- **Deterrence belief:** 59 % say the death penalty does not deter serious crimes; 39 % say it does.  
- **Specific case:** 52 % support execution of Nidal Hasan, but 80 % oppose livestreaming it (ABC News/Washington Post/Ipsos, Oct. 2026).

## Factors shaping opinion
- **Wrongful convictions:** 203 exonerations since 1972 (Death Penalty Information Center) raise doubts about system accuracy.  
- **Moral and cost concerns:** Perceived fallibility and high expense of capital punishment fuel skepticism.  
- **Demographics:** Supporters tend to be Christian, white, and Republican (PRRI).  
- **Political influence:** Executions are concentrated in a few states; most states that retain the death penalty are issuing fewer sentences, suggesting public disenchantment.

## Recent execution cases
- **Tennessee lethal‑injection failures:** Inability to locate veins for Christa Pike and Tony Carruthers sparked criticism from Governor Bill Lee.  
- **Planned firing‑squad execution:** Nidal Hasan’s execution scheduled for December 2026; appeals may delay it.  
- **State activity:** Six states carried out 31 executions in 2026; Florida accounted for over half. Texas remains the state with the most executions since the 1970s.

## Historical context
- The last public execution in the United States occurred in the 1930s; the proposed livestream would have been the first in 90 years.  
- Historically, quasi‑public executions attracted thousands of spectators, but the introduction of anesthesia and concerns about public pain shifted attitudes toward viewing such events as barbaric.  
- Some immigrant communities historically turned executed individuals into symbolic heroes.

## Conclusions
- While a majority still endorse the death penalty for murder, public opinion is highly contingent on question framing, alternatives offered, and concerns about wrongful convictions and moral legitimacy.  
- The concentration of executions in a handful of states and the decline in overall support suggest growing public ambivalence toward capital punishment as a solution to violent crime.
