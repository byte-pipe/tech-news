# The Waymo effect: how AI is quietly making research less collaborative

## Main observations
- I rode in a Waymo driverless car in San Francisco and found the experience “wonderful” because it eliminated the need for any human interaction.  
- My colleague Susan Winslow and I, both introverts, appreciated the quiet, undemanding space the car provided, even though we had traveled across continents to meet in person.  
- This personal pleasure illustrates a broader phenomenon I call **the Waymo effect**: when technology removes the friction of dealing with other people, we perceive a net gain because the hidden benefits of that friction are invisible.

## Definition of the Waymo effect
- **Waymo effect** – the loss of valuable, often unseen, social frictions when a frictionless alternative (e.g., driverless cars, AI tools) is adopted.  
- Echoes Tim Wu’s “tyranny of convenience” and Albert Borgmann’s “device paradigm”: convenience hides the practices that once produced the commodity, so we stop noticing their disappearance.  

## Parallel in research: the frictionless colleague
- Large language models (LLMs) act as “driverless” collaborators: they are available 24/7, have no agenda, and respond only to prompts.  
- Human collaborators bring unpredictable challenges, alternative framings, and serendipitous insights that LLMs do not provide unless explicitly asked.  
- The value of a colleague lies precisely in the ways they *refuse* to be an extension of our own thinking.  

## Consequences for the research community
- Research is a “community of practice” sustained by argument, apprenticeship, informal conversations, and shared struggles (e.g., referee reports).  
- Each interaction replaced by a chatbot removes a thread from the social fabric; individually minor, but collectively leading to **de‑collaboration**.  

## Incentive trap driving de‑collaboration
1. **Funding pressure** – budget cuts first eliminate unmeasurable but essential activities (travel, workshops, sabbaticals), eroding the physical infrastructure of serendipity.  
2. **Velocity worship** – evaluation systems still prize rapid output; LLMs promise faster literature reviews and drafts, tempting researchers to prioritize speed over depth.  
3. **Credit arbitrage** – LLMs demand no authorship, ego management, or compromise, making them an attractive “collaborator” in a system where credit is survival currency.  

## Why research leaders should worry
- The shift toward frictionless tools is not merely a matter of personal discipline; it is reinforced by institutional incentives that make de‑collaboration the rational choice.  
- If unchecked, the loss of spontaneous, challenging, and serendipitous human interaction will weaken the very social fabric that makes research innovative and robust.  

## Call to action
- Recognize the hidden value of collaborative friction.  
- Re‑evaluate incentive structures to reward genuine collaboration, not just rapid output.  
- Preserve and invest in spaces (physical and virtual) that foster unscripted human exchange.
