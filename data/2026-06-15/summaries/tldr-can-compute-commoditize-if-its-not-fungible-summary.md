# Can Compute Commoditize if it's Not Fungible?

## Main Argument
- Fungibility is not a strict prerequisite for a commodity market; markets rely on a standardized reference plus priced deviations (basis).
- Historical commodity markets (natural gas, power, agriculture) succeed despite non‑fungibility by using indices and basis swaps.

## Why Compute Appears Non‑Fungible
- Different cloud providers deliver varying goodput, model‑flop utilization, and operational realities for the same GPU class (e.g., H100‑hour).
- Lack of established “plumbing” (clusters, queues, failure domains, data gravity, security, workload‑specific performance) to converge on a single price.

## Potential Market Structure for Compute
- First tradable product would likely be a cash‑settled reference exposure (e.g., DGX spec) with explicit spreads for:
  - Operator differences
  - Topology and location
  - Scale and duration
  - Service‑level agreements (SLA)
- Physical/spec fungibility (GPU class, DGX spec, cooling) is largely present; operational and contractual fungibility are still developing.

## Strategic Implications for CoreWeave
- Positioning compute as a non‑commodity supports higher valuation: long GPU lifespans, operator moat, contracted cash flows, and lower cost of capital.
- If compute became a pure commodity, depreciation would accelerate, margins would compress, and valuation would shift toward utility‑style multiples.

## Conclusion
- McBee’s caution reflects real gaps in operational and contractual fungibility, not a fundamental impossibility of a compute commodity market.
- The market can price the existing basis differentials, and a structured index plus spread framework could enable commoditization over time.
