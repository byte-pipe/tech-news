# Almost the whole of Japan moved eastward after 2011 earthquake

## Overview
- About 15 minutes after the magnitude‑9 Tohoku earthquake (11 March 2011), GPS stations across Japan recorded a simultaneous eastward shift of roughly 5 mm.
- The displacement spanned ~3000 km, far exceeding the length of the main rupture and any previously recorded slip.

## Key Observations
- The shift was uniform across the entire Japanese archipelago and likely extended into the surrounding ocean.
- No separate surface earthquake occurred at the time of the movement; the motion was attributed to a deep‑travelling seismic wave.
- The phenomenon was identified through extensive analysis of GPS and seismic data collected during the disaster.

## Proposed Mechanism
- The initial Tohoku quake generated a powerful seismic wave that traveled to Earth’s core and reflected back toward the surface.
- Although weakened, the reflected wave retained enough energy to trigger a coordinated motion of the four adjoining tectonic plates.
- Prior vigorous shaking from the main shock may have weakened plate boundaries, making them more susceptible to the returning wave.

## Implications
- Demonstrates a previously unrecognised hazard: deep‑core‑reflected waves can induce delayed, widespread fault motion minutes after a major earthquake.
- Suggests the need to consider such mechanisms when assessing seismic risk in regions with similar fault systems.
- Calls for further research to determine how far offshore the motion extends and its relevance to other parts of the world.
