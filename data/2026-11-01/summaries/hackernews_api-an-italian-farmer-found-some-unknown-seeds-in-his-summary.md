## Discovery of the seeds  
- Early 1990s, 40‑year‑old Claudio Grossi (Parma, Italy) found an old sack of unidentified seeds in his attic, likely left by his great‑grandfather (farm founded c. 1915).  
- He scattered the dry, cracked kernels onto manure and largely forgot about them.  

## Unexpected growth  
- The seeds sprouted several wheat types; one grew to about six feet, twice the height of commercial wheat.  
- This tall wheat produced **seven ears per stalk** in a fixed pattern: one long ear surrounded by six short “fingers.”  
- Kernels were larger than normal and displayed a spectrum of colors (yellow, black, red, white, green), sometimes all on the same plant.  

## Biblical resonance  
- While reading Genesis, Grossi recognized the description of a stalk with seven ears (Joseph’s dream to Pharaoh).  
- He linked the ancient‑looking wheat to the biblical passage, reinforcing his sense that the grain was a relic from antiquity.  

## Baking the “miracle grain”  
- Grossi harvested, milled the grain, and baked a loaf.  
- The dough rose poorly, but the bread emitted a nutty aroma and offered a sweet, rich flavor absent from typical commercial flour.  

## Agricultural advantages  
- The wheat proved hardier, drought‑tolerant, and self‑sufficient; it outcompeted weeds by its height and required no fertilizer or herbicides.  
- Grossi named it **grano del miracolo** (“miracle grain”).  

## Historical backdrop of wheat breeding  
- Wheat domestication began ~10,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent, later spreading to Europe and Asia.  
- 1920s Italy’s “Battle of Wheat” under Mussolini promoted high‑yield “elite” varieties to reduce reliance on imports.  
- The 1930s Dust Bowl in the U.S. spurred search for drought‑resistant strains.  
- The mid‑20th‑century Green Revolution, led by Norman Borlaug, increased yields but produced short, disease‑susceptible plants that depended on chemicals, degrading soil health and resilience.
