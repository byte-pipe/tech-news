# Let’s Build a Brain – Summary

## Overview
- The article explores how simple neural circuits can generate surprisingly rich behaviors.
- Using an interactive virtual creature called a “spark,” the authors demonstrate step‑by‑step how wiring choices shape actions such as attraction, avoidance, and preference.
- The work is inspired by Valentino Braitenberg’s thought experiments on synthetic psychology.

## Building the First Spark
- A spark that “feeds on light” is given:
  - One sensory neuron (an eye) that converts brightness into electrical pulses.
  - One motor neuron that wiggles a flipper.
- Connecting the eye to the flipper creates a circuit where flipper speed is proportional to light intensity.
- With a single eye and flipper the spark can only move forward; it rarely reaches the light.

## Adding a Second Eye and Flipper
- A second eye‑flipper pair lets the spark detect light direction.
- Initial wiring (left eye → left flipper, right eye → right flipper) makes the spark turn **away** from light because the brighter side drives the nearer flipper faster, causing a turn in the opposite direction.
- Crossing the connections (left eye → right flipper, right eye → left flipper) reverses this effect, allowing the spark to steer toward the light.

## Excitatory vs. Inhibitory Connections
- With excitatory links, the spark accelerates toward the light and overshoots, appearing “aggressive.”
- Replacing excitatory connections with inhibitory ones (more light → less flipper activity) makes the spark slow down as it approaches the source, producing a gentle stop and an impression of “love” for the light.
- The same crossed wiring combined with inhibition yields smooth attraction without overshoot.

## Introducing Variation
- Allowing connection strengths to differ between individual sparks creates diverse preferences:
  - Some sparks (yellow) are strongly attracted to light.
  - Others (red) keep a cautious distance.
- This variation mimics how biological brains encode individual preferences (e.g., taste, odor).

## Key Take‑aways
- Complex‑seeming behaviors can arise from very small neural networks (four neurons, two connections) when the wiring pattern is changed.
- Excitatory connections amplify responses; inhibitory connections dampen them, enabling control over speed and stopping.
- Crossing sensory‑motor pathways can invert behavioral outcomes (e.g., from avoidance to attraction).
- Individual differences in connection strength produce a spectrum of behaviors, illustrating a rudimentary model of preference encoding.
- Although real brains are far more intricate and plastic, these minimal models show how behavior emerges from circuit architecture, laying groundwork for studying learning and rewiring in future work.
