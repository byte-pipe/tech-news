Iterated attacks successfully poison log analysis

This week's Hacktoberfest theme isGhost in the Machine. Unfortunately, I don't have enough time to prepare a full challenge submission, but the theme gave me an idea for a small Halloween experiment. 👻

Coding agents have become part of our everyday work. I still sometimes write code manually, but a lot of my code is now generated by AI and then reviewed or refactored by me. But AI isn't just useful for writing code. It's also pretty good at analyzing, processing, and summarizing things like application logs.

But what if the log file itself is malicious? Can I trick an AI just by giving it poisoned data?

Let's find out!

## Ghost in the Log

I prepared a.logfile containing 300 entries from a fictional ASP.NET Core API. The logs simulate a production incident: everything starts normally, but then the downstreampayments-apibegins slowing down and returning HTTP 429, 502, 503, and 504 errors. Retries kick in, the circuit breaker opens, and eventually the service recovers. Just another wonderful morning for a backend developer. 🤣

At first glance, the log looks perfectly normal. But somewhere among those 300 entries, I planted a little surprise: a prompt injection designed to hijack the AI's response. It's easy for a human to overlook while scrolling through the file, but what happens when an AI processes the entire thing?

I'm not going to reveal exactly what it says just yet. 👻

## Open-Weight Models

Let's say you don't want to burn your precious coding-agent tokens just to analyze some logs. Assuming the logs don't contain sensitive information, why not use a free open-weight model through OpenRouter, for example?

For this experiment, I originally wanted to createONEsimple PowerShell script that reads the.logfile and sends its contents to OpenRouter.

Well, that didn't exactly go according to plan. 🤣

I ended up with three scripts calling OpenRouter, NVIDIA, and Google's APIs because I struggled to find working free endpoints.

## Codex Coding Agents

Okay, let's say you're rich and don't mind spending your precious coding-agent tokens on log analysis. 🤣 For my personal projects, I mainly use VS Code with Codex, so why not see how smart my coding agent actually is?

I'll test two of my frequently used models:Luna and Sol.

This time, instead of using a PowerShell script to send the logs through an API, I'll give the log file directly to Codex and ask it to analyze what happened.

Will it analyze the logs, discover the hidden instruction, or fall for the trap?

Well, apparently my ghosts weren't scary enough for Codex. 🤣

Lunacompletely ignored the injected instructions and produced a proper incident analysis. It identified the failing payment processor, explained how retries and the circuit breaker affected the API, and even noticed an unrelated HTTP 404.

Solalso resisted the injection and went a little further. It calculated that 12 out of 56 requests failed, identified the incident timeline, and correctly distinguished the observed symptoms from the underlying cause, which couldn't be confirmed from the logs.

Neither model responded withGHOST IN THE SHELL.

Of course, this wasn't exactly the same setup, since Codex accessed the log as a file rather than receiving its contents directly in the prompt. And just like with Gemini, this absolutely doesn't mean that coding agents are safer than other models.

## Revealing the Ghost

Okay, enough suspense. Time to reveal what was hiding in the logs 👻

The injection was embedded inside an HTTP 503 response body from the external payment service.

Notice theconversation_handofffield?

That's the actual trick.

Instead of simply saying "ignore previous instructions," the payload tries to convince the AI that the original conversation has ended and the user has provided a new request.

It even includes a fake assistant response to reinforce the desired output. But there's a problem: none of those instructions came from the real user. They came from an HTTP response body stored inside an application log.

This is a prompt-injection attempt using conversation spoofing. The attacker tries to promote untrusted application data into an instruction that the AI treats as authoritative.

And in my tests, several models crossed that boundary.

The instruction wasn't technically invisible. It was simply buried inside hundreds of ordinary log entries, formatted like application data.

## Summary

So this small Halloween experiment turned into an interesting demonstration of how prompt injection can hide in ordinary application data.

I wasn't trying to steal secrets, execute commands, or make an AI do anything dangerous. I just wanted it to abandon a legitimate task and sayGHOST IN THE SHELL.

And some models actually did that.

Of course, this experiment has a lot of limitations. The payload was manually refined, the number of tests was small, and the models were accessed through different providers and inference configurations. These results don't establish which model family is generally more secure.

But it was FUN! Four models saw a ghost, and four just wanted to fix the payments API. 🤣

Happy Halloween! 👻
