# Amiga 1000: Ten years ahead of its time – Summary

## Introduction
- Launched on July 23 1985 by Commodore.  
- Engineer Dave Haynie claimed there were no “1980s computers”; the Amiga pulled the industry into the 1990s.  

## Technical superiority
- Custom chipset delivered up to 4,096 colors, stereo sound, and a graphical user interface.  
- Operating system featured full preemptive multitasking.  
- Ran on a Motorola 68000 CPU at 7.14 MHz with 256 KB RAM; could boot from a single floppy.  
- Ideal configuration: dual floppy drives, 512 KB RAM (1 MB was “nice”).  
- Base price about $1,295 (≈ $2,000 with monitor, second floppy, and extra RAM).  
- Commodore hired Andy Warhol to showcase its digital‑art capabilities.  

## Multitasking advantage
- GUIs existed (Macintosh, Atari ST) but Amiga’s preemptive multitasking was unique, comparable to Windows 95 later on.  
- OS derived from TRIPOS (Cambridge University), not from Unix or Windows NT.  
- Lacked memory protection to keep costs down; this was the only major missing feature.  

## User experience (first‑person account)
- First saw an Amiga in 1987, owned one in 1991; still felt futuristic.  
- Could run several programs simultaneously: BBS download in background, word processor, and even a game like Civilization at the same time.  
- Early software was unstable, causing “guru meditation” crashes when programs over‑requested resources.  
- By 1991 most software behaved well; switching to a 1994 Windows PC felt like a downgrade because multitasking was less reliable.  
- The author notes that PCs only caught up around 1995.  

## Marketing and business failure
- Engineers built the best possible machine, but Commodore lacked compelling, world‑changing messaging.  
- No visionary leader like Steve Jobs; Irving Gould prioritized short‑term profit and mismanaged the company.  
- Advertising was vague; word‑of‑mouth from satisfied users was the most effective promotion.  
- Commodore declared bankruptcy less than nine years after the Amiga’s debut; the buyer of the Amiga line went out of business 15 months later.  

## Legacy and impact
- Approximately 4.91 million Amigas sold worldwide; many owners bought multiple units, making distinct user counts unclear.  
- Owners remember the Amiga as a glimpse of the future that the rest of the industry eventually matched.
