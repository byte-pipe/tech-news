# Agentic Engineering Is Just Everything We Haven’t Been Doing

## Main argument
- The author is not promoting agentic engineering; they assume readers have already chosen to try it.
- Excitement around “agentic engineering” is really excitement about standard software‑engineering practices that are often skipped due to time pressure.
- The author is frustrated that bots are being held to higher standards than the engineers who create them.

## Normal engineering practices highlighted
- Using an issue tracker to capture change details, requirements, and enable agents to ask questions and update status.  
- Adding documentation strings to methods.  
- Writing descriptive pull‑request titles and bodies.  
- Keeping documentation up to date, or creating it from scratch.  
- Writing meaningful tests (or at least having tests).  
- Running automated linting and agreeing on code conventions.  
- Planning, architecting, and seeking feedback on designs before implementation.  
- Recording meeting notes and confirming shared decisions.  
- Conducting conversations in open, searchable channels instead of private DMs.

## Critique of the “agentic” label
- These practices are already part of good software engineering; the author is merely relieved they are being revisited.  
- Holding agents (bots) to stricter standards than human engineers is unfair.  
- A coworker’s quote illustrates the belief that formal documentation will make agents follow principles, whereas in reality it often requires team discussion and shared understanding.

## The role of mētis
- Cites James C. Scott’s concept of *mētis*: practical, situational knowledge gained through long‑term practice.  
- Code syntax is well‑defined, but many engineering principles are free‑form and need local, adaptable judgment.  
- Written rules are less flexible than human intuition; they are useful but cannot replace nuanced decision‑making.

## Practical advice
- If agentic engineering feels difficult, examine whether ordinary software engineering is also problematic in the organization.  
- Adding AI to a dysfunctional process (people or code) will not solve underlying issues; it may repeat the same mistakes.  
- “More people on a late project makes it later”; similarly, adding a “fake person” (an agent) does not improve outcomes.
