# AI Arms Race Puts Sovereign Supply Chains in the Spotlight

## Overview
- The AI arms race mirrors the Cold‑War nuclear buildup: nations first seek capability, then sovereignty.
- South Korea prioritises domestically sourced AI accelerators over simply importing the most powerful GPUs from the United States.
- Reliance on U.S. hardware is viewed as risky because American policy can change abruptly.

## South Korea’s Strategic Advantages
- Home to the world’s two largest memory producers, **SK Hynix** and **Samsung**, securing a steady supply of high‑bandwidth memory (HBM).
- **Samsung Electronics** ranks as the second‑largest leading‑edge wafer fab globally.
- Geographic proximity to **TSMC** (Taiwan) and **Rapidus** (Japan) further strengthens the supply chain.

## Domestic Chip Start‑ups
- **FuriosaAI** and **Rebellions** are the primary Korean AI‑accelerator startups; former Sapeon talent merged into Rebellions in late 2024.
- Both have shipped silicon, a milestone many startups have not achieved.
- Current products are modest:
  - **Rebellions Rebel100** (launched late 2025) performs roughly on par with Nvidia’s three‑year‑old H200.
  - **Furiosa RNGD** delivers about 2.25 × the performance‑per‑watt of Nvidia’s six‑year‑old A100 in tested workloads.

## Recent Funding and Roadmap
- **Furiosa** secured a **$620 million Series D** round to:
  - Scale RNGD production (≈ 20 000 units in 2024, double by 2027).
  - Develop a third‑generation accelerator in partnership with **Broadcom**.
- Anticipated next‑gen chip details:
  - Fabricated on a **2 nm** process (likely TSMC).
  - Multi‑die chiplet architecture with **HBM4 or HBM4e** memory.
  - Broadcom‑driven rack‑scale design using **Tomahawk 6/Ultra** and **3.5D XDSiP** packaging, similar to tech adopted by Meta and OpenAI.

## Global Sovereign‑AI Momentum
- The push for AI hardware that can be owned and operated within national borders extends beyond Korea.
- Past U.S. actions (e.g., the brief ban on Nvidia H2O accelerators for China) underscored the fragility of relying on foreign chip designers.
- Regional examples:
  - Japan’s **neocloud ai&** plans to deploy up to 100 **Rebellions RebelRack** systems (≈ 3 200 Rebel100 accelerators) to augment its GPU‑based compute offering.
  - **Furiosa** signed a deal with **Equinix** to install RNGD accelerators at the LS2 colo in Lisbon, addressing Europe’s shortage of home‑grown AI chips.

## Implications
- Even less‑performant, non‑American accelerators become attractive when they reduce exposure to supply‑chain disruptions.
- South Korea’s combination of memory leadership, advanced wafer fabrication, and emerging accelerator startups positions it to become a key player in the sovereign AI hardware ecosystem.
