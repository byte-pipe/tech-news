# 4 Behaviors That Make You Look Junior (No Matter Your Title)

### Four tells that paint you as "not ready" without saying it out loud.

A few years ago, during my time at Amazon, I had to endure a meeting in which a mid-level engineer spent sixty minutes radiating frustration. This man kept his arms crossed, and he sighed audibly through most of the meeting. At one point, I heard him mutter, “This is ridiculous!” loud enough that those of us sitting next to him had to pretend we hadn’t heard him.

The roadmap decision, or whatever it was we were debating, was probably wrong, and he was probably right to disagree with it. But the way he manifested his disagreement betrayed him.

The company had plans for him to become a senior engineer, but less than a year after that meeting it was clear he stalled out at his level. Two years later, he left for another company.

That frustrated engineer may have never connected the dots between his behavior in that meeting and the years that followed. I did, though. This is because, as a Principal Engineer, I came into contact with hundreds of engineers, and that gave me insights into the effects of common patterns of behavior.

In my view, the most underrated career skill in tech is so underrated that nobody even calls it a skill. In fact, it’s defined by its opposite: looking junior.

Let me explain.

Someone “looks junior” when they act or respond in an immature manner in a variety of situations, from high-pressure moments to mundane ones.

It is possible to do great work and yet still pick up a reputation for being immature.

In tech, “maturity” has less to do with age than with how you cope when things get hard. The strength of your contributions will be undermined if you handle yourself poorly in the moments around the work.

Put another way, your temperament has a huge impact on your seniority.

The following four behaviors are the most common reasons strong engineers get read as junior. All four are failures of professionalism. Most strong engineers are doing at least one of them right now.

## 1. Visible irritation with process

Every company has meetings that engineers privately hate. Roadmap reviews, prioritization sessions, and grooming meetings are common candidates. Such meetings are detested because they’re so often messy, the requirements are vague or nonexistent, and they drag on forever. There’s an absurdity to talking endlessly about work instead of just doing it.

The professional question is how you conduct yourself during these grueling, overly bureaucratic meetings.

I witnessed that engineer tank his reputation in a single quarterly planning meeting by being the loudest sigher in the room. He wasn’t the only one. The sighers quietly stopped getting invited back.

It rarely takes much. A jaw set in response to a PM’s aggressive timeline. A short exhale right after leadership repeats a priority your team thought was settled.

People notice the body language before they even process what has been said. Each response (sigh, jaw, exhale) is a small data point that says,This person is not steady enough yet to operate with more scope.

What makes this hard is that the irritation usually comes from a real place. You’re sitting through a meeting being run badly, watching decisions being made that don’t make sense. The body language may be honest, but it’s also damaging your reputation in real time.

A better use of that energy is to push the meeting itself toward something more useful.

What to do:Process is a necessary part of working on a team or on big projects. The meetings you privately hate aren’t going away, and visibly resisting them will only signal that you don’t yet understand how big work gets done. The best approach is to recognize that working through processes is part of the job. When you notice yourself getting visibly irritated, treat that as a sign that there’s something worth contributing. Challenge yourself to bring it into the discussion in a way that makes the meeting more productive. A line as simple as“Can we pause and name what we’re trying to decide here?“can redirect a meeting that has lost its way. This way, the instinct that was about to leak out as a sigh becomes the thing that gets the room back on track.

## 2. Emotional weather clouding (or tornadoing) your work

Everybody has bad days.

The professional question is whether anybody can tell that you’re having a bad day on the day that you’re having it.

Are you letting your emotional weather cloud (or rain, hail, or tornado) your work?

I have watched people lose months of accumulated reputation because of a single bad incident. Picture an engineer who has just come out of a meeting where they received feedback that landed wrong. The feedback wasn’t unreasonable, but it felt personal in the moment. Back at their desk, they fire off a sharp reply in the doc thread, and they vent in a side DM with a teammate. Then they make a too-pointed comment at the next stand-up.

None of those moments would ever surface in a performance review. They show up instead in the small decisions other people make over the following months. For example, who gets assigned to the next high-visibility project, and who gets the benefit of the doubt when something else goes sideways.

Professionalism is showing up and doing the job well, including on the days when you don’t feel like it. The job is hard enough on its own. Projects shift, priorities change, and things go sideways pretty much every day. The people around you are managing their own version of that while experiencing their own bad days.

Pouring your emotional weather onto shared work helps nobody.

Maintaining a steady baseline on a hard day is one of the most underrated forms of professionalism. What you bring with you, weather settled or stormy, will have a significant effect on your colleagues’ working conditions.

What to do:Nobody ever writes a professional message when their emotions are high. Before you respond to anything that’s hit you hard, ask yourself whether you’re in a steady enough state to be putting words in front of other people. If you’re not, the most professional move is to step away from the work entirely. Excusing yourself for an hour, an afternoon, or the rest of the day to get your bearings is far more professional than saying something in the heat of the moment that you’ll regret. If you genuinely have to be at work, slow your communication down: draft your replies, sit on them for an hour, and re-read them with fresh eyes before sending.

## 3. Treating disagreement as a personal attack

Walk into any design review at any company, and you will be able to spot the people who treat critical feedback as a verdict onthem. They are the ones whose first instinct is to defend themselves rather than understand the feedback.

I have watched many people gradually lose their collaborators this way. A colleague might leave a tough comment on a design doc and get a defensive three-paragraph response. The next time, they soften the feedback before sending. By the third or fourth round, they’ve decided that saying anything isn’t worth the energy. After a few of these incidents, the person’s collaborators will have decided that working with them on anything difficult isn’t worth it.

The person doesn’t see the shift directly. Rather, they’ll see fewer and fewer comments on their docs, and easier sign-offs in reviews, and they’ll interpret that as confirmation that their work is getting stronger.

But the opposite is happening. They’ve simply stopped getting the input that helps their work to improve.

Professionalism is treating critiques as information and not an attack. The professional posture is curiosity. Everyone in a collaborative environment wants the work to be good. They’re just looking at the same problem from a different angle and seeing something you may have missed. When feedback lands hard, the question that turns it into something useful is,What do they see that I don’t?People who default to that question will get steadily better. Those who default toWhy are they coming after me?will stop getting better at all.

What to do:When a piece of critical feedback lands or a disagreement occurs, lead with a question instead of a counterargument. Ask the person to walk you through how they got there. Queries such as“Help me understand what you’re seeing,” or “What’s the alternative you’d suggest?” do something that a defensive posture will never achieve: they give you a clearer picture of the disagreement itself. Often, you’ll find that you and the other side were already aligned but had just framed the point differently. The rest of the time, you’ll learn something you need to know. Either outcome guarantees progress, whereas defensiveness shuts it all down.

## 4. Defaulting to pessimism

The most damaging version of all this happens inside your own head. And you’re the only one who can catch it early.

Picture someone who hears unwelcome news on a Monday. For example, a project they’ve been leading is being cancelled. Over the next few days, the cancellation starts coloring everything else at work. Conversations and decisions that would have felt routine a week earlier start looking like signs of something bigger going wrong. When they finally ask their manager about it, the cancellation turns out to have been a routine resourcing call that had nothing to do with them.

Most of the things people catastrophize about are due to routine variance. Launches slip, people get reorged, and projects get cancelled on every team. None of those things mean what they might feel like they mean. The professional discipline is to read variance as variance and resist sliding into a bleaker interpretation than the data supports.

In the long run, most setbacks turn out to be noise. The real cost is letting one of them poison your outlook on everything else.

The reason this matters professionally is that pessimism is contagious. When you walk into a meeting convinced things are going badly, the people you work with will pick up that read whether you intend it or not. They will start hedging their own takes, and the next problem will become harder for everyone to engage with.

The good news is that optimism spreads the same way. You get to decide which direction to lean.

What to do:Pessimism feels like sophistication, and optimism feels naive. In a collaborative environment, however, that take is backwards. Defaulting to optimism is the professional move because the people around you are reading your tone and forming their own. You can be honest about problems while still believing they’re solvable. That’s the version of optimism that holds up at work. Engineers who walk into a hard situation looking for a way through will usually find one, partly because their posture will make the team around them more confident and, thus, capable.

## Closing

Looking junior at work has less to do with your level and more to do with your professional steadiness. You want your presence to be the same on a hard day as on a good one. This way, you gain a reputation for reliability and professional maturity.

Strong work earns you the first chance. Steady professionalism will earn you every one after that.
