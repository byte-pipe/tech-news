### The Regulator Lag

TheCambridge Centre for Alternative Finance’s (CCAF) 2026 Global AI in Financial Services Report— 140 pages published 29 April with the BIS, IMF, World Bank, and World Economic Forum, drawing on 800+ respondents across financial institutions, vendors, and regulators — produced four findings that stick out to us:

* 4 in 5 firms now deploy AI at some level
* 76% of industry uses OpenAI as their primary foundation model
* The top three cloud providers serve more than 80% of the industry
* 43% of financial regulators are not collecting AI adoption data and have no near-term plans to start

Industry is moving roughly twice as fast as the supervisors meant to oversee it, and the supervisors don’t have the data, infrastructure, or training to catch up: 46% of surveyed regulators use no cloud infrastructure at all, 24% use no foundation models. Among regulators flagging constraints to their own AI adoption, 48% cite a lack of training and capacity building, 47% a lack of talent, and 45% a lack of technology and infrastructure.

This is not a stress-test problem. No capital adequacy ratio, liquidity coverage requirement, or operational resilience framework captures it. You can see the data below in the chart tracking active AI adoption by stakeholder.

Source

The firms that regulators oversee are deploying AI in credit decisioning, fraud detection, customer communications, trading strategies, and compliance infrastructure. These oversight institutions are operating without the tooling, data, or capabilities to monitor and interrogate these companies.

We highlight several of the additional conclusions below and recommend you check out the whole report.

### An Information Problem

Regulation often lags technology by design, so some delay is a reasonable trade-off for preserving innovation incentives for private companies.

But that logic rests on regulators at least observing what is happening, even if not yet acting on it. When 43% of the world’s financial regulators are not collecting AI adoption data and have no near-term plans to start, it becomes an information problem which won’t self-correct.

##### Regulator Collection of Data on AI Adoption and Related Risks

Source

TheFinancial Stability Board (FSB) flagged this in October 2025, explicitly acknowledging the early-stage monitoring efforts and data gaps which are preventing authorities from adequately quantifying the risks they are responsible for managing. Building on its November 2024 work, the FSB acknowledged that most authorities remain at an early stage of monitoring, citing data gaps including the lack of agreed definitions for AI, difficulty assessing the criticality of AI services, and no consistent way to track third-party dependencies. The FSB identified four categories of AI-related systemic vulnerability:

1. Third-party dependencies and service provider concentration
2. Market correlations from common models
3. Cyber risks
4. Model risk, data quality, and governance failures

### Three Jurisdictions, Three Ideas for Regulation

Source

The advanced economies are ahead in exploring solutions to the AI transformation relative to the rest of the world, but unfortunately, the three major regulatory blocs have reached materially different conclusions about how to respond.

* The EU has focused on prescriptive measures. TheAI Act’s obligations for financial services take effect on 2 August 2026, covering AI systems used in credit scoring, insurance pricing, and AML profiling. Non-compliance carries fines of up to 7% of global annual turnover or €35 million, whichever is greater. Institutions using third-party AI models are classified as deployers, not providers, carrying different regulatory obligations.
* The UK is trying to be more flexible in its regulations.The FCA has been explicitthat it will not introduce AI-specific rules. FCA chief executive Nikhil Rathi reaffirmed this in December 2025, citing the technology’s pace of change as justification for resisting prescriptive rule making. TheSupercharged Sandbox,AI Live Testing programme (its second cohort commencing April 2026), andAI LABrepresent the frameworks for supervised experimentation rather than codified prohibition.The problem is that theUK Parliament’s Treasury Select Committee’sJanuary report concluded that the FCA, the Bank of England, and HM Treasury are exposing the public and the financial system to potentially serious harm. It called for the FCA to publish practical guidance on AI by the end of 2026.
* The US is using examination as a way to enforce its ideas of AI use in financial services. TheSEC’s 2026 Examination Priorities, released in November 2025, made AI a primary focus across fraud detection, AML, trading, portfolio management, and customer services. Currently,only 40% of S&P 500 companies provide AI-related disclosures. Just 15% disclose board oversight of AI. There is still a gap.

### Risks and Model Concentration

Source

There are many risks to consider, but among the CCAF’s most striking findings is the degree to which the global financial system’s AI dependency has concentrated around a single provider.

The 76% OpenAI dependency among industry respondents is the single most exposed concentration in the system. Google sits at 57%, Anthropic at 35%, DeepSeek at 15%. Among regulators, OpenAI usage is 48% — meaning supervisors are partly dependent on the same provider their firms are using. On cloud, AWS leads industry at 46% and vendors at 55%; among regulators that do use cloud, Azure leads at 39%.

Source

This can easily expose the financial system to resilience vulnerabilities, pricing shocks, or supply disruptions.

What breaks if OpenAI or Anthropic have a sustained outage? Credit decisioning pipelines stall. AML alert generation pauses. Customer-facing chatbots stop. Trading systems that depend on GPT-based signal extraction lose inputs. Compliance functions that auto-summarise filings or transcripts go dark.

TheEuropean Systemic Risk Board’s (ESRB) Advisory Scientific CommitteepublishedArtificial Intelligence and Systemic Riskin December 2025. The report analysed 11 AI features and identified five as potential amplifiers of systemic risk:

* Concentration and entry barriers
* Model uniformity
* Monitoring challenges
* Overreliance and excessive trust
* Speed

The ESRB's proposed policy response covers three categories: capital and liquidity regulation adjustments; "skin-in-the-game" and "level of sophistication" requirements; and enhanced supervision combined with transparency labelling on financial products that use AI. The skin-in-the-game proposal is arguably the sharpest policy idea in the current regulatory landscape, addressing a fundamental misalignment that firms deploy AI systems they did not build, cannot fully explain, and are not financially liable for when they fail.

TheUK’s Critical Third Parties Regimewas designed to address exactly this class of systemic dependency, giving the FCA and the Bank of England powers of investigation and enforcement over firms providing critical services such as AI and cloud providers. However,despite being operational for more than a year, no organisation has yet been designated under it.

The financial system’s 76% dependence on a single AI provider does not appear on any stress test. No regulator has formally quantified what a disruption to that provider would mean for the firms it supervises.

### Intent to Execution Misalignment

Different surveys put agentic AI deployment in different places, but they point in the same direction.

TheNeurons Lab synthesisof recent agentic AI research finds that 99% of companies plan to put autonomous AI agents into production. Only 11% have actually done so. Among those that have started, 57% report lacking the internal capabilities to take advantage of agentic AI.

Source

This matters for the regulatory lag argument.

Gartner projectsthat 40% of business software will include AI capable of completing end-to-end tasks independently by the end of 2026.Finastra’s researchputs the operational efficiency gain from agentic AI at 20%, with banks leveraging it earning a 15% greater market share. These are the commercial incentives that will accelerate deployment beyond the current 11%.

The buffer between where firms are and where they are going is the policy window for regulation changes.

### What This Means in Practice

For investors, a portfolio of financial services firms with 76% dependence on a single AI provider is carrying a correlated operational risk that is neither well-priced nor well-governed.

For CEOs, the FCA has been clear that personal accountability for AI failures sits with named senior managers, specifically SMF24 (Chief Operations) and SMF4 (Chief Risk), not with systems or vendors.

In the EU, the provider-vs-deployer distinction established by the AI Act creates an additional board-level decision on how your institution sources its AI and what determines your regulatory classification and liability exposure.

Some regulatory lag is unavoidable. But the absence of supervisory data, capability, and legal clarity is leaving firms inside a thin oversight structure. The commercial incentives to accelerate AI adoption are substantial. The safety structures around that acceleration are not.
