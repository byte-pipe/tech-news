# 4 Behaviors That Make You Look Junior (No Matter Your Title)

## Overview
- The author, a Principal Engineer, observed that engineers can produce strong technical work yet still be perceived as junior because of certain unprofessional behaviors.  
- Perceived “juniority” is less about age or title and more about how one handles pressure, processes, and interpersonal interactions.  
- Four common behaviors undermine seniority; the first three are detailed below.

## 1. Visible irritation with process
- **What it looks like:** sighing, jaw‑clenching, rolling eyes, or vocal complaints during meetings that are perceived as bureaucratic (roadmap reviews, grooming sessions, etc.).  
- **Why it hurts:** Body language is noticed instantly and signals that the person cannot stay steady under broader scope, leading peers to doubt their readiness for larger responsibilities.  
- **How to change it:**  
  - Recognize that the meeting’s process is unavoidable and view irritation as a cue that something can be improved.  
  - Convert the impulse to a constructive contribution, e.g., ask “Can we pause and clarify the decision we’re trying to make?” to steer the discussion back on track.  

## 2. Emotional weather clouding (or tornadoing) your work
- **What it looks like:** letting a bad day affect written communication, venting in chats, or making sharp remarks after receiving feedback.  
- **Why it hurts:** These moments rarely appear in formal reviews but shape colleagues’ future decisions about project assignments and trust, eroding reputation over time.  
- **How to change it:**  
  - Before responding while emotions are high, pause and assess whether you are in a steady state.  
  - If not, step away from the work for a short period or the rest of the day.  
  - When you must reply, draft the message, wait (e.g., an hour), then reread with a clear mind before sending.  

## 3. Treating disagreement as a personal attack
- **What it looks like:** reacting defensively to critical feedback in design reviews, replying with lengthy justifications, or withdrawing from future feedback.  
- **Why it hurts:** Defensive responses discourage collaborators from offering input, leading to fewer comments, easier sign‑offs, and ultimately slower growth for the engineer.  
- **How to change it:**  
  - View feedback as data about the work, not a verdict on personal ability.  
  - Respond with curiosity (“Can you elaborate on the concern?”) rather than self‑defense.  
  - Encourage ongoing dialogue to keep the feedback loop active and collaborative.
