# Activity-Focused Design – Summary

## Overview
- Activity‑focused design centers on the actions users take to achieve a goal (e.g., steps to connect or control lights in a lighting app).  
- It complements, not replaces, human‑centered design and includes approaches such as task analysis, jobs‑to‑be‑done, activity theory, and activity‑based design.  
- The core unit of analysis is **activity** – what people do and how they do it.

## Key Definitions
- **Goal**: The end state a person intends to reach (e.g., “lightbulb is connected to the app”).  
- **Activity**: A series of tasks that help achieve a goal (e.g., “connect the lightbulb to the app”).  
- **Task**: A single unit of action or work (e.g., “open the app”, “click the lightbulb icon”).

## Task Analysis – Process
1. **Identify Goals** – Determine the most important goal(s) users have with the product (e.g., turn lights on/off, schedule automation).  
2. **List Tasks** – Enumerate the tasks required to reach each goal, choosing an appropriate level of specificity and deciding whether to describe tasks in an interface‑independent way (for new designs) or include UI details (for existing systems).  
3. **Document** – Record goals and tasks using diagrams, hierarchical task trees, flowcharts, or any format that helps the team spot gaps and opportunities.  
4. **Analyze & Improve** – Look for unnecessary, inefficient, or counter‑productive tasks and consider removing or redesigning them (e.g., eliminating a Bluetooth‑on step if devices are always connected).  
- Methods to gather data include think‑aloud interviews, contextual inquiry, or personal walkthroughs when time is limited.  
- Additional insights may be gathered through surveys or semi‑structured interviews if task analysis reveals issues such as user anxiety.

## Strengths of Task Analysis
- Reveals gaps and optimization opportunities when a design depends on a sequence of tasks.  
- Translates naturally to digital design; identified tasks map directly to interface elements and interactions.

## Weaknesses of Task Analysis
- Does not automatically capture non‑task aspects such as emotions, social pressures, or cultural norms.  
- May encourage backward‑looking design, limiting imagination of novel interaction methods beyond current user practices.

## Takeaway
- Activity‑focused design treats **activity** as the primary analytical unit.  
- Task analysis, one activity‑focused method, follows four steps: determine goals, list tasks, document them, and analyze for improvements.  
- Its strengths lie in exposing task patterns and aligning directly with UI design; its weaknesses involve limited insight into emotional and social dimensions and potential bias toward existing workflows.
