# Summary of “A Bipartisan Amendment Would End Police License Plate Tracking Nationwide | WIRED”

## Key Proposal
- An amendment to be introduced at a House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee markup would bar any recipient of federal highway funding from using automated license plate readers (ALPR) for purposes other than toll collection.
- The amendment consists of a single sentence restricting Title 23 assistance recipients from non‑toll ALPR use.

## Sponsors and Political Context
- Sponsored by Rep. Scott Perry (R‑PA, Freedom Caucus) and Rep. Jesús “Chuy” García (D‑IL, progressive).
- The two lawmakers represent opposite ideological wings but share concerns over surveillance and privacy.
- Neither office responded to WIRED’s request for comment.

## Expected Impact
- Title 23 funds cover roughly 25 % of U.S. public road mileage, including most state, county, and many city streets.
- Conditioning this funding on the amendment would force virtually all states, counties, and municipalities that accept federal highway money to either dismantle existing ALPR networks or limit them to tolling functions.
- Historically, jurisdictions rarely decline federal highway assistance, making the amendment’s effect nationwide.

## Background on ALPR Use and Controversies
- ALPR cameras are mounted on poles, overpasses, traffic signals, and police vehicles, capturing every passing plate, time, and location, and feeding data into searchable, inter‑agency databases.
- In Illinois, an audit found the Flock Group’s ALPR network violated state law by providing U.S. Customs and Border Protection access to data; the state ordered the federal access cut.
- Flock paused federal pilots after the audit, though the company previously denied such arrangements existed.

## Legal and Privacy Concerns
- Privacy advocates argue that aggregated ALPR data creates a de‑facto warrantless tracking system.
- The Brennan Center for Justice documented integration of ALPR feeds into broader police data‑fusion systems that combine plate data with surveillance and social‑media monitoring.
- The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) reported misuse cases, including targeting of mosques, disproportionate deployment in low‑income neighborhoods, and a Texas deputy querying the network to track a woman who had an abortion.
- Litigation: The Institute for Justice filed a class‑action suit against San Jose, alleging its 474‑camera network violates Fourth Amendment rights; the city has not yet responded substantively.

## Responses from Stakeholders
- Flock spokesperson Josh Thomas warned that the amendment could deprive first responders of essential tools, citing recent support for ALPR use by Austin, Texas police.
- Demand Progress senior policy adviser Hajar Hammado called the amendment “commonsense,” describing the current situation as a “mass surveillance dystopia.”
- San Jose Mayor Matt Mahan, a defendant in a separate ALPR challenge, remains a vocal proponent of the city’s camera program.

## Legislative Process and Outlook
- The amendment will be debated during the Thursday markup of the five‑year, $580 billion reauthorization of federal surface‑transportation programs.
- By using Congress’s spending power—similar to past conditions on the drinking age and DUI standards—the amendment sidesteps direct constitutional rulings on Fourth Amendment searches.
- While states could theoretically refuse the funding, historical precedent suggests they will accept the conditions, making the amendment’s nationwide impact likely if passed.
