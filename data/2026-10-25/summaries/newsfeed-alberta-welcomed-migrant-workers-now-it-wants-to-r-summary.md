# Alberta welcomed migrant workers. Now, it wants to restrict their rights

## Context
- Alberta held a non‑binding referendum on its relationship with Canada and on limiting services for some newcomers.
- Unofficial results show the sovereignty question will fail, while immigration‑related proposals are likely to pass.
- The province relies heavily on temporary foreign workers in agriculture, food services, tourism and other sectors.

## Referendum proposals
- Only Canadian citizens, permanent residents and “Alberta‑approved” immigration status holders would be guaranteed access to healthcare and education.
- Newcomers would need to reside in Alberta for 12 months before qualifying for certain social‑support programmes.
- The province could charge temporary residents fees for healthcare and education.

## Government rationale
- United Conservative Party (UCP) attributes overcrowded hospitals and schools to rapid population growth.
- Premier Danielle Smith claimed each dollar earned by a guest worker generates ten dollars in public service costs, describing the situation as unsustainable.
- The government argues temporary residents (≈6 % of the population) contribute about $150 million in taxes but cost over $1 billion in services.

## Criticisms and concerns
- Critics say the proposals do not address the root causes of service strain and would make migrant workers more vulnerable to exploitation.
- Economists note temporary residents also pay federal taxes that fund Alberta’s healthcare, and they tend to use fewer services than average Albertans.
- Lack of reliable data to support the claim that migrants cost more than they contribute.
- The referendum received little public scrutiny on immigration questions, with many voters focusing only on the separation issue.

## Voices from the community
- Vanesa Ortiz, a Mexican‑Canadian teacher, recalls being welcomed in Calgary a decade ago and worries the new rules would deny her daughter’s education and family’s healthcare.
- Ortiz warns that scapegoating non‑permanent residents will not lower living costs and could push workers into the shadows.
- Writer Ximena González describes the shift as moving from “exploiting” workers to “punishing” them, feeling unwelcome as a temporary resident.
- Economist Jim Stanford calls the vote a symbolic ideological exercise, noting the province lacks unilateral power to enforce the proposals.

## Potential impacts
- Restricting access could leave thousands of farm workers without healthcare.
- The policy may deepen anti‑immigrant sentiment and undermine Alberta’s labor market that depends on foreign workers.
- Workers could face increased exploitation, reduced dignity, and uncertainty about building a stable life in the province.
