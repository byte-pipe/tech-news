# A worm that lived half a billion years ago preferred turning right

## Background
- The fossil species *Spriggina floundersi* lived about 555 million years ago in shallow seas of the Ediacaran Period, before the Cambrian explosion.
- The organism was a small, flatworm‑like animal that moved by wriggling along the seafloor.

## Research and Findings
- Scott Evans (American Museum of Natural History) and colleagues examined roughly 100 fossil specimens collected in South Australia.
- About 50 specimens show a clear bend; twice as many are bent to the left as to the right.
- Because the fossils are mirror‑image impressions formed when storms buried the worms in sand, a left‑bent impression indicates the animal itself bent to the right.
- The statistical excess of right‑bending individuals is interpreted as evidence of “handedness” – a preference for turning one way.
- Some specimens display multiple bends in both directions, suggesting the worms could change direction to avoid moving in circles.

## Evolutionary Significance
- The presence of handedness, bilateral symmetry and active locomotion in these Ediacaran animals indicates that such traits evolved well before the Cambrian.
- Evans argues that Cambrian organisms built on these earlier foundations, adding features like legs to become more familiar animal groups.
- Russell Bicknell (Flinders University) notes that functional asymmetry this deep in the fossil record provides insight into how such behaviours originated.

## Publication
- The study appears in *Scientific Reports* (DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-53857-x).
