# Summary of “I Thought Coding Was The Job”

## Freelancing Experience
- Got first freelance client while in final college semester; built an app for gym equipment.
- Expected the hardest part to be coding (React Native, backend, deployment, bugs).
- Project ran smoothly despite personal challenges (heartbreak, final semester, career uncertainty).
- Client was professional: paid on time, kept scope reasonable, communicated respectfully, even covered Mac rental costs.
- Initial perception of freelancing was simple: client needs → build → get paid.
- Reality revealed many non‑technical tasks:
  - Understanding client needs
  - Discussing timelines and pricing
  - Handling revisions, feature changes, scope creep
  - Managing awkward conversations and waiting for replies
- First project had only verbal agreement; luck prevented scope expansion or disappearance.
- Second project included proper planning and written agreement but ended abruptly due to client’s internal alignment issues.
- Learned the importance of upfront agreements: “You don’t get lucky twice with clients.”

## Lessons Learned from Freelancing
- Freelancing involves managing expectations, communication, trust, and uncertainty.
- Technical work is only one component of the overall system.
- Realized that people skills are crucial for successful outcomes.

## Transition to Corporate
- Entered corporate environment with a simplified view: focus on coding, solving technical problems, building systems, learning architecture.
- Discovered an invisible layer of non‑technical responsibilities:
  - Hierarchy and communication styles
  - Meetings, visibility, and asking questions correctly
  - Team dynamics, knowing when to speak or stay silent
- Recognized that the same pattern appears across different professional contexts.

## Broader Insight Across Fields
- Language learning: moved from vocabulary+grammar to timing, confidence, hierarchy, context, listening, and atmosphere.
- Marketing: beyond posting content to positioning, audience psychology, negotiations, attention dynamics, and virality.
- General observation: most careers appear simple from the outside (visible output) but hide extensive invisible work:
  - Uncertainty, failed attempts, coordination, emotional pressure, relationships, trust, adaptation.

## Reflection and Call to Action
- Initially believed careers were primarily about technical skills; now sees them as heavily reliant on people skills (technical, social, communication, emotional systems).
- The realization, once overwhelming, now feels interesting and adds depth to any field.
- Concludes that coding was just the most visible part of the job, a pattern likely true for many areas of life.
- Invites others to share similar experiences where the perceived “main skill” turned out to be only a small part of the actual work.
