# Almost 500,000 Russian soldiers killed in Ukraine war, GCHQ says

## Main findings
- GCHQ director Anne Keast‑Butler announced that almost 500,000 Russian soldiers have been killed since Russia’s full‑scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.  
- The figure is based on data compiled by BBC Russian, Mediazona and volunteers, which have confirmed the names of 223,539 Russian soldiers; experts estimate this represents 45‑65 % of total Russian war deaths.  
- Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky reported 55,000 Ukrainian military casualties since 2022.

## GCHQ threat assessment
- Russia is described as “relentlessly targeting” UK critical infrastructure, democratic processes, supply chains and public trust.  
- The Kremlin is blamed for a series of espionage plots, a hybrid war against the UK and NATO, and past assassinations on British soil (e.g., Alexander Litvinenko, Sergei Skripal).  
- Russia and China are heavily investing in space for both civilian and military purposes.  
- Hundreds of Russian “shadow fleet” vessels have entered UK waters despite the prime minister’s threat to intercept them.

## Cyber‑security emphasis
- GCHQ is working to fend off cyber attacks, organised‑crime phishing, ransomware and sabotage attempts.  
- The agency urges businesses and individuals to act immediately: switch to passkeys, embed security in new technologies, protect supply chains and treat cyber‑security as a national defence priority.  
- Collaboration with the tech industry, academia and the public is presented as essential to stay ahead of rivals, especially in AI development.

## Context on GCHQ
- GCHQ (Government Communications Headquarters) is the UK’s largest spy agency, based in Cheltenham (“the Doughnut”), focusing on signals intelligence and cyber security and receiving the bulk of the national intelligence budget.  
- The director’s speech was delivered at Bletchley Park, the historic World War II code‑breaking site.

## Additional notes
- The Kremlin denies all allegations made by GCHQ.  
- The speech highlighted a “narrowing window” for the UK and its allies to maintain a technological edge over adversaries.
