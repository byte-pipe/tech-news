# How Private Equity Bought America’s Essential Services

## Overview
- A fatal fire‑truck malfunction in Chicago (June 26 2025) illustrates how private‑equity ownership can turn essential public infrastructure into profit‑driven enterprises.
- The article argues that the private‑equity (PE) model, while effective for restructuring underperforming firms, becomes dangerous when applied to services with inelastic demand such as fire trucks, ambulances, and other emergency‑response assets.

## Private‑Equity Business Model
- PE firms raise funds from pension plans, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, and wealthy individuals.
- Companies are acquired mainly through leveraged buyouts (LBOs), loading 50‑90 % of the purchase price as debt onto the target’s balance sheet.
- PE firms earn a 2 % annual management fee and 20 % carried interest on profits, taxed at the long‑term capital‑gains rate (20 %) rather than ordinary income rates (37 %).
- The model works when applied to genuinely distressed businesses that need operational discipline and growth capital.
- Problems arise in essential‑service sectors where customers cannot switch providers, quality degradation is hard to detect, and the typical 3‑to‑7‑year exit horizon incentivizes cost‑cutting and asset stripping.

## Fire‑Truck Industry: A Case Study
- Two decades ago, more than two dozen independent fire‑apparatus manufacturers existed; today three firms (REV Group, Pierce Manufacturing/Oshkosh, Rosenbauer) control ~80 % of the market.
- REV Group, owned by American Industrial Partners (a PE firm), consolidated former independents (E‑One, Ferrara, KME, Ladder Tower, Spartan) under a single corporate umbrella.
- Consequences:
  - Backlog of $4.5 billion in orders, with wait times of up to four years for custom trucks.
  - Prices have doubled in ten years (pumper ≈ $1 million; ladder ≈ $2 million).
  - Profit margins rose from 4‑5 % to over 13 %.
  - Production facilities in Pennsylvania and Virginia were closed despite the growing backlog.
  - $530 million spent on stock buybacks and dividends, including a $180 million special dividend to PE owners before the company went public.
  - CEO compensation reached $6 million annually.
- Senate testimony (Sept 10 2025) labeled the practice a “heist,” and antitrust lawsuits have been filed by several cities and a Texas price‑fixing investigation opened.

## Pattern Across Other Essential Services
- **Ambulances:** KKR’s ownership of Envision Healthcare and Global Medical Response led to a 60 % rise in median transport charges (2012‑2017); Envision filed Chapter 11 in 2023.
- Similar consolidation and profit‑extraction tactics are reported in sectors such as waste management, water utilities, and school transportation, though the fire‑truck example remains the most documented.

## Implications
- Public‑sector customers are locked into contracts funded by tax receipts, giving PE‑owned firms a captive revenue stream while they prioritize backlog maintenance and margin growth over service quality.
- The “buy, strip, and flip” approach increases bankruptcy risk; public companies acquired by PE are about ten times more likely to fail than comparable firms.
- Ongoing legislative debate about the carried‑interest tax loophole and potential antitrust actions reflect growing concern over the societal costs of PE dominance in essential services.
