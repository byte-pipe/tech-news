# Anthropic illegally trained Claude with Stanford professors’ books

## Overview
- A federal judge ruled in May 2026 that Anthropic scraped and pirated works of over 10 Stanford professors to train its Claude models.  
- The case, **Bartz v. Anthropic**, is described by the Authors Alliance as the largest copyright settlement involving an AI company.

## Legal Findings
- Judge William Alsup prohibited Anthropic from using pirated books to create a permanent central library for AI training.  
- He deemed the acquisition of books for training under **fair use**, likening it to a human reader internalizing themes and style.  
- The settlement amount was **$1.5 billion**, to be distributed to authors and publishers of roughly 500,000 registered works.

## Authors’ Responses
- Professor Tobias Wolff and 27 other authors argue the settlement is insufficient.  
- Wolff called the unauthorized use “slave labor” and warned it could jeopardize writers’ livelihoods.  
- Ten of Wolff’s publications were used without permission.  
- Wolff and professor Chang‑Rae Lee opted out of the class settlement and filed a separate suit, **Cruz v. Anthropic**, seeking statutory damages up to $150,000 per infringed work.

## Claims and Allegations
- Plaintiffs allege Anthropic downloaded, reproduced, and stored copyrighted books from “shadow libraries” such as LibGen and PiLiMi.  
- They seek a permanent injunction preventing Claude from being trained on their works.

## Broader Legal Landscape
- Over 70 infringement lawsuits have been filed against AI firms by authors, visual artists, and news outlets (e.g., The New York Times, Getty Images).  
- The Authors Guild is pursuing additional actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, advocating for mandatory licensing of source material.  
- The Guild is also lobbying Congress for national legislation to enforce licensing requirements.

## Commentary from Experts
- Stanford IP scholar Paul Goldstein highlighted the “transaction cost” problem: licensing millions of works is impractical for AI platforms.  
- He noted the U.S. lacks a moral right of attribution, unlike most industrialized nations, limiting authors’ ability to demand credit.  
- Mary Rasenberger of the Authors Guild emphasized licensing as a way to ensure compensation and control over output inclusion.

## Implications for Writers
- Wolff expressed concern for emerging writers facing technological challenges that threaten fair compensation and recognition.  
- Authors view legal action as a means to secure credit and protect creative rights amid rapid AI development.
