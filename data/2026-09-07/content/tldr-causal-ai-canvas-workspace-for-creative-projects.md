## Never lose a reference.

# Spaced repetition

The gap between reviews matters more than the hours you put in.

Six weeks of notes and almost none of it stuck, which is the opposite of how I had assumed revision was supposed to work. The point was never to log more time at the desk. It was to be asked the question again just as the answer started to slip, which is a different thing entirely and much harder to schedule by feel.

## What the studies agree on

* Retrieval beats re-reading, even when it feels harder in the moment
* The optimal gap widens after each successful recall of the same item
* Spacing related items apart cuts the interference between them
* Testing yourself is not just measurement, it is itself the study

Every scheduler still guesses at the timing.SM-2quietly underpins most of them, andit was tuned back in 1987 onone person'sflashcards. Nothing since has really replaced it, so the defaults every app ships with today are still, in effect, that same person's, carried forward because they were there, not because anyone re-ran the experiment at scale.

Conditions that slow acquisition often improve long-term retention (Bjork, 1994).

So I am rebuilding the deck around the gaps rather than the totals: shorter sessions, wider spacing, and no more re-reading the same page the night before.

## Where it breaks down in practice

None of this is an argument against the algorithm, only against trusting it past the point where it is measuring anything real. A scheduler knows when a card was last seen and whether the answer came back right. It does not know that the answer came back right because the card sat directly underneath one that gave it away, or that two cards in the same deck are quietly teaching the same fact in slightly different words.

The failure mode is always the same, and it is not the maths. A deck grows past the point where its intervals mean anything, the daily queue turns into a backlog, and the backlog gets cleared by pressing the middle button on everything in it, which the scheduler reads as evidence that the intervals were correct, and duly widens them. The numbers stay healthy the whole way down.

* Leeches: the few cards that fail over and over and eat a third of the session
* Interference: near-identical cards that trade places in memory week to week
* Backlog: a queue long enough that the grading stops being honest

What I have settled on is duller than a better algorithm and works far better than one. Delete the leeches instead of nursing them. Write cards that ask for one thing, so a wrong answer means something specific rather than that the card was too broad to answer. Keep the deck small enough that a bad week can be caught up in a day, because the moment it cannot be, every interval in it is a guess resting on a guess.

## Common questions.

### What is this app for?

It's a spatial workspace. Instead of filing things into folders, you put your notes, files, images and links onto an infinite canvas and arrange them in a way that matches how you think conceptually. The layout is the organisation - similar ideas sit near each other, and connections represent relationships. It preserves causal relationships between ideas this way, so your brain can focus on what it does best: generating new ones.

### How is this different from Notion or Obsidian?

Causal is the step before opening any creative tool. It's for creatives who see the benefit in planning before execution. Notion organises structure into pages and databases; Obsidian is plain markdown files. Causal's canvas stores rich text, files and images without restraining you to the structure of a page, so you have complete freedom in planning.

### Is there a free plan?

Yes, and it isn't a trial - the free plan doesn't expire and you don't need a card to start. You get 200 nodes, 50 AI requests a month, and every feature the canvas has: nothing is locked behind Pro. Pro raises those limits for when you outgrow them.

### Can I share my canvas with someone?

Yes. Any canvas can be turned into a read-only link in one click, and whoever you send it to can open it without an account. Sharing grants access to that canvas and any nested canvases within it. You can copy the link again or stop sharing at any point.

### How do I stop a canvas turning into a mess?

Three tools provide structure. Create a collection to pack nodes into tidy columns. Create a nested canvas inside a canvas when a topic needs its own space. And when you have something you don't want to place yet, drag it to the right edge and park it in the unsorted tray.

### What file types can I upload, and can I open them without downloading?

You can drop in anything up to 20 MB per file. PDFs, Word documents, Adobe files, markdown, spreadsheets, images and video all open fullscreen inside the app with real previews. Markdown files and CSVs can even be edited in place, saving straight back to the file. Anything else is stored safely and shows up as a card you can download whenever you need it.

### Can I write proper documents, or is it just short notes?

Both. Notes sit directly on the canvas for fast, flexible thoughts, while longer pieces open into a dedicated full-page editor. Quick notes and full-page text files share the exact same rich formatting - headings, lists, checkboxes, quotes, code blocks, highlights, images and links.

### Is there a desktop app, and does it work on my phone?

Yes - there's a free Mac app, and it keeps itself up to date, so you only ever install it once. Open this page on a Mac and the download sits in the footer, under Product. There's no phone app: Causal needs a desktop-sized screen to be worth using, so that one is on the longer horizon.

### Do I have to start from a blank canvas?

No. Empty canvases come with a set of templates for different kinds of creative work, whatever your workflow - we have templates for app development, website design, branding, storyboarding, research and more.
