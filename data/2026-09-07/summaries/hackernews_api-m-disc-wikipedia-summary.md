# M‑DISC – Summary

## Overview
- Write‑once optical disc technology introduced in 2009 by Millenniata, Inc.  
- Available in DVD and Blu‑ray formats (DVD+R, BD‑R, BD‑R DL, BDXL).  
- Designed for archival longevity; manufacturer claims up to 1 000 years of data retention when properly stored.  
- Physical appearance: almost transparent DVD; Blu‑ray discs have standard or ink‑jet printable labels.  
- Data layer uses a glassy carbon (MABL) material that is inert to oxidation and tolerates 200–1000 °C.  
- Readable by most DVD players (post‑2005) and Blu‑ray/BDXL drives; writable by drives supporting the higher power mode (typically 2× speed).  
- Standard capacities: 4.7 GB (DVD+R), 25 GB (BD‑R), 50 GB (BD‑R DL), 100 GB (BDXL).

## History
- Millenniata founded by BYU professors Barry Lunt, Matthew Linford, CEO Henry O’Connell, and CTO Doug Hansen; incorporated 13 May 2010 in American Fork, Utah.  
- Company declared bankruptcy in December 2016; assets acquired by debt holders who formed Yours.co to continue sales.  
- Current licensed manufacturers (2020s): Ritek (Ritek and RiDATA brands) and Verbatim (co‑branded “Verbatim M‑DISC”).  
- Planned 128 GB BDXL disc never reached market due to bankruptcy.  
- 2022 Verbatim updated its Blu‑ray M‑DISC formulation: faster write speed (6× vs 4×), different coloration; company asserts unchanged longevity.

## Durability Claims
- Original DVD+R M‑DISC tested to ISO/IEC 10995:2011 and ECMA‑379, projected lifespan of several hundred years.  
- Theoretically, glassy carbon layer could survive >10 000 years in ideal conditions (e.g., salt mine), though polycarbonate substrate limits overall lifespan to ~1 000 years.  
- Verbatim Japan states use of a titanium layer for moisture protection; claims ≥100 years based on internal ISO/IEC 16963 testing.  
- Other regional sites cite “several hundred years” based on the same ISO testing.

## Durability Testing
- 2009 U.S. Department of Defense (China Lake Report) found M‑DISC outperformed organic‑dye competitors in accelerated aging tests.  
- 2010‑2012 French National Laboratory (LNE) high‑temperature/humidity tests: M‑DISC survived <250 hours, compared with 250–1000 hours for other inorganic discs; methodology and comparability questioned.  
- 2016 independent stress test (Mol Smith) showed 25 GB BD‑R M‑DISC remained readable after 60 days outdoor exposure, while a standard TDK BD‑R failed.  
- 2022 NIST Interagency Report (IR 8387) listed M‑DISC as an acceptable archival format rated for 100+ years, referencing the 2009 DoD and 2012 LNE studies.

## Commercial Support
- Writing requires drives with firmware supporting the higher power mode; typical write speed 2× for DVD, up to 6× for newer Blu‑ray discs.  
- Most standard Blu‑ray drives can read and write M‑DISC media; certified by Blu‑ray Disc Association (as of 2019).  
- Price premium: 1.5–3× the cost of standard Blu‑ray discs; DVD‑M‑DISCs have limited availability.  
- Early DVD‑M‑DISCs were nearly translucent, making the writable side hard to identify; later versions added coloration/labels.  
- Drive manufacturers supporting M‑DISC recording include Asus, LG, Lite‑On, Pioneer, Buffalo, and Hitachi‑LG.

## Adoption
- Utah state government has used M‑DISC for archival purposes since 2011.  
- Some consumers and data‑hoarding enthusiasts adopt M‑DISC for long‑term cold storage.

## Alternative Technologies
- **Optical:**  
  - Syylex Glass Master Disc – etched glass substrate, resistant to ageing but vulnerable to physical/chemical damage.  
  - Standard BD‑R/BD‑R DL/BDXL (25 GB, 50 GB, 100 GB) and Sony BDXL 128 GB discs – generally rated for up to 50 years in archival conditions.
