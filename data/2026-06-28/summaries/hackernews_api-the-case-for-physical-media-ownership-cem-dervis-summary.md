# The Case for Physical Media Ownership – Summary

## Digital purchases are licenses, not ownership
- Digital storefronts typically sell revocable licenses, keeping substantial control over the content.  
- DRM can remotely remove or block access; if a service shuts down or changes policy, purchased items may disappear from the user’s account.  
- Notable incidents:  
  - 2013 Xbox One plan for 24‑hour online check‑ins and blocking used game sales (reversed after backlash).  
  - 2011 ReDigi attempt to resell iTunes tracks; the 2018 appellate ruling held that the first‑sale doctrine does not apply to digital files.  
  - Multiple lawsuits (2022 Amazon class action, 2020 California case, 2025 Lisa Reingold case) alleging that “Buy” buttons misrepresent revocable licenses as ownership.

## Physical media provides true ownership
- Discs, cartridges, books, and vinyl records can be owned, resold, lent, archived, and used offline without reliance on an account or provider.  
- Offline media require no passwords, two‑factor authentication, or ongoing Terms of Service updates; access is independent of the provider’s status.  
- Physical items retain secondary‑market value, allowing owners to recover part of the purchase price through resale.

## Consumer‑protection and legal implications
- Lawsuits argue that companies violate consumer protection laws by misrepresenting digital licenses as purchases.  
- Courts have consistently distinguished digital licenses from physical copies, denying first‑sale rights for digital files.

## Economic considerations
- Physical media’s resale potential offers a tangible financial benefit absent from most digital licenses.  
- When a digital license is revoked, the consumer loses the entire investment, as the license holds no resale value.
