# Summary of “A senator tried to ban gambling on prediction markets—now she’s a Kalshi lobbyist”

## Background
- In July 2010, Senator Blanche Lincoln (D‑Ark.) warned that prediction markets could be used for gambling if “event contracts” were allowed for sports events such as the Super Bowl or the Kentucky Derby.  
- During the Senate debate on the Dodd‑Frank Act, Lincoln argued that such contracts would serve no commercial purpose and should be prohibited.

## Lincoln’s 2010 Legislative Action
- As chair of the Senate Agriculture Committee, Lincoln was a primary author of Title VII of Dodd‑Frank, giving the CFTC authority over swaps and event contracts.  
- The law was written to let the CFTC ban contracts that existed mainly for gambling, reflecting Lincoln’s intent to prevent sports‑betting‑type prediction markets.

## Shift to Lobbying for Kalshi
- After leaving the Senate, Lincoln became a lobbyist for prediction‑market platform Kalshi, receiving $480 000 from the firm since 2024.  
- She has urged the CFTC to permit sports‑event contracts, a reversal of her earlier stance.

## Regulatory Landscape Under Different Administrations
- **Biden administration:** Interpreted Dodd‑Frank as prohibiting sports‑related event contracts; proposed expanding the ban to include election bets. Lincoln’s August 2024 letter to the CFTC opposed this expansion, arguing sports outcomes lack “significant economic consequences.”
- **Trump administration:** Used Dodd‑Frank authority to allow sports‑event contracts, refusing to enforce the 2011 CFTC Rule 40.11 that barred “gaming” contracts. This shift enabled nationwide sports betting on prediction markets, even in states that restrict such gambling.

## Legal Battles Between States and Prediction‑Market Platforms
- About 20 states have sued Kalshi, Polymarket, and the CFTC to determine whether Congress intended to preempt state gambling laws.  
- Courts are split; the Supreme Court may eventually decide if the CFTC can override state regulations.

## Market Developments
- Kalshi launched sports betting on 23 January 2025, three days after Trump’s second inauguration, branding it “The Golden Age of markets.”  
- Polymarket, a rival, offers contracts on potential Supreme Court cases, illustrating the broader scope of prediction‑market wagering.

## Commentary and Industry Position
- Legal scholar Ilya Beylin noted that Kalshi timed its launch to coincide with the Trump administration’s relaxed enforcement of Rule 40.11.  
- Kalshi claims it is governed by federal CFTC rules, not state gambling laws, and portrays opposition as a “gaming cartel” protecting a monopoly.  
- Donald Trump Jr. serves as an advisor to both Kalshi and Polymarket, and his venture‑capital firm 1789 Capital is a major investor in Polymarket.

## Key Takeaways
- Lincoln helped craft the law that was meant to ban sports gambling on prediction markets, yet now lobbies to lift those restrictions.  
- The regulatory approach to event contracts has swung dramatically between the Biden and Trump administrations, creating a patchwork of state lawsuits and potential Supreme Court involvement.  
- Prediction‑market platforms are positioning themselves as federally regulated financial markets, challenging traditional state gambling authority.
