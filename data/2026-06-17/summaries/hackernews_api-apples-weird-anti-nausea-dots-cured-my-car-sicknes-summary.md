# Apple’s weird anti-nausea dots cured my car sickness

## Overview
- The article recounts how Apple’s **Vehicle Motion Cues** (introduced in 2024) eliminated the author’s motion‑sickness while working from a moving vehicle.  
- The feature adds subtle moving dots around the screen edges that sync with the car’s acceleration, braking, and turning, reducing the conflict between visual and vestibular cues.

## How Vehicle Motion Cues Work
- Uses the device’s accelerometer and gyroscope to detect vehicle motion.  
- Dots move opposite to the car’s direction (e.g., turn right → dots sweep left).  
- When the car brakes, dots slide forward; on straight stretches they may stay stationary.  
- The system can also invert text color for better contrast when dots cover characters.

## User Experience
- Enabled the feature on iPhone, iPad, and MacBook; could read Kindle books for hours and write a 1,000‑word review without nausea.  
- The author’s wife also adopted the feature for her own road‑trip work.  
- Dots are generally unobtrusive but can interfere with maps or text on long straight roads; the author suggests Apple should dim them in such cases.  
- Default dot size, color, and density work well, but they are customizable in Accessibility settings.

## Activation & Settings
- Accessible via **Settings → Accessibility** on iOS, iPadOS, and macOS.  
- Options: manually toggle, automatic activation when vehicle motion is detected, or set to appear only when needed.  
- The author created a quick toggle by assigning **Back Tap → Double Tap** to “Vehicle Motion Cues” (iOS 18+).  
- Prefers manual toggling while driving to avoid visual distraction.

## Practical Takeaway
- The feature proved valuable during a two‑month European road trip and is recommended for anyone who needs to read, write, or work on screens while traveling by car.  
- It exemplifies an accessibility tool that can benefit a broader audience beyond its original intent.
