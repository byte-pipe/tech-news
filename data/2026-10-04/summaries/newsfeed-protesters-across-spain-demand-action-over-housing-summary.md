# Protesters across Spain demand action over housing crisis

## Background
- The protests were sparked by the eviction of 87‑year‑old Maricarmen Abascal after a sharp rent increase.
- The eviction triggered nationwide anger, with demands for lower rents, an end to evictions, and a ban on “vulture funds” buying residential property.
- Spain faces a shortage of about 700,000 homes, according to the Bank of Spain.
- House prices rose 12.2 % in the past year; rental costs increased 8.5 % in 2025, with Madrid rents up 9.7 %.

## Scale of the Demonstrations
- Around 50 marches were scheduled for Saturday in cities such as Madrid, Valencia, and Barcelona.
- Estimates of participants in Madrid ranged from 70,000 (official) to 500,000 (Tenants’ Union).
- Protesters set up a long‑term encampment at Puerta del Sol in Madrid.
- In Valencia, clashes with police near the Palau de les Arts resulted in rubber‑bullet use, nine officer injuries, and five arrests.

## Protesters’ Demands
- Immediate suspension of evictions for vulnerable tenants until 2030.
- Extension of existing rental leases by two years for contracts expiring before 2028.
- Automatic renewal of rental contracts.
- Higher taxes on tourist apartments.
- Prohibition of residential property purchases by investment (“vulture”) funds.
- A general strike over the housing crisis.

## Government Proposal and Parliamentary Vote
- The Sánchez coalition (Socialist Party + Sumar) presented two separate decrees covering the above measures.
- Both decrees were rejected:
  - First decree: 178 – 172 against.
  - Second decree: 184 – 166 against.
- Opposition parties (People’s Party, Vox) and the pro‑independence Together for Catalonia (JxCat) voted against the measures, leaving the minority government without the needed support from Catalan and Basque nationalist parties.

## Political Consequences
- The defeat deepens speculation that Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez may call an early general election, originally scheduled for next summer.
- The coalition’s inability to pass emergency housing legislation highlights its fragile parliamentary position.

## Current Situation
- Despite the failed legislation, protests continue across Spain.
- The housing shortage and rising costs remain a central issue for the working class, with activists urging stronger regulation and protection for tenants.
