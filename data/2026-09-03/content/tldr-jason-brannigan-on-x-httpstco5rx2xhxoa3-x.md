# AI labs are the new crypto L1s

OpenAI and Anthropic are each valued as if they will categorically win the AI market, yet they cannot both win the intelligence race. A near-$1tn valuation for each assumes today's technical and distribution lead can be turned into durable control of the customer relationship and its economics, even as alternatives improve around it.

Crypto's L1s last cycle are the reason I think that assumption is too aggressive. The leading networks had real technical advantages and network effects, yet once enough competing chains became reliable and performant, further marginal improvement in throughput mattered less and less. Users became chain-agnostic, routing improved, and value moved towards applications and distribution.

AI is moving in the same direction. Model capability will keep improving, but the returns to each marginal gain will diminish across most work as ‘good-enough’ alternatives multiply and become easier to swap between. Once several models clear the quality bar for a task, buyers can optimise for price, speed or availability. Intelligence becomes increasingly commoditised and substitutable, just as L1 performance did.

At their latest rounds, OpenAI announced $122bn of committed capital at an $852bn post-money valuation, alongside more than 900m weekly active ChatGPT users, over 50m subscribers and $2bn in monthly revenue. Anthropic then raised $65bn at a $965bn post-money valuation, with an annualised revenue run rate above $47bn [1]. Those revenue figures are already stale: by August, Anthropic's annualised revenue run rate had reportedly passed $65bn and OpenAI's had reached $40bn [1]. OpenAI’s operating losses, however, widened from $9.3bn in Q1 to $12.3bn in Q2 2026 [2].

This highlights that winning the intelligence race is no longer enough. The labs that justify today's prices will convert their model advantage into sticky products, distribution competitors cannot recreate, and ownership of the users’ workflows from start to finish.

Value

Investors funded a generation of L1s on the basis that whoever wins the infrastructure layer will emerge the victor of the cycle. Networks raised inordinate amounts of capital based on the VC thesis that infrastructure would prevail and would ultimately accrue the lion’s share of economic value… sound familiar?

The architectures differed, but the pitch was broadly the same: faster, cheaper or more performant infrastructure would win the market. However, once dozens of L1s and L2s became reliable and performant enough for almost every normal use case, marginal performance improvements mattered less. Once again, distribution, liquidity, applications and ecosystem design mattered more.

The networks that not only survived, but thrived, are the ones that built meaningful ecosystems, such as Solana and Base, which converted their network performance into real applications that people actually use. What didn't survive was the assumption that exceptional infrastructure would be enough to make the category winner-take-all.

AI labs are moving towards the same outcome.

Most intelligence becomes good enough

Open models do not need to win the frontier race, because the much larger opportunity sits in the share of work where another marginal improvement in intelligence does not change the result. Once several models clear the quality bar for an email, support interaction, research task or software workflow, buyers can route between them and optimise for price, speed or availability.

DeepSeek makes the comparison clear. NIST placed the April preview of V4 Pro behind the US frontier overall, yet it was already more cost-efficient than GPT-5.4 mini on most benchmarks both models solved, and that preview has since been replaced by a stronger checkpoint [3]. In OpenRouter's June snapshot, V4 Flash was roughly 56 times cheaper on input and 167 times cheaper on output than GPT-5.5 [4].

The best model will still earn a premium where a small capability difference changes the outcome, but that is a much narrower market than the total volume of work LLMs will perform. As open models become good enough across more of that volume, intelligence becomes increasingly substitutable and routing moves work towards the cheapest model that can do the job. Each new frontier lead then has less economic value, just as another marginal improvement in L1 throughput eventually did, and the durable moat moves towards products, distribution and ownership of the workflow.

A model can be good enough for email, support or routine coding while remaining unusable in a safety-critical system, where a small improvement may unlock an entirely new market. That leaves room for a frontier premium in some domains even as everyday intelligence becomes easier to substitute.

The frontier can stay expensive

The frontier can become more expensive even as the model layer commoditises, because the price of the best available intelligence and the price of a fixed level of useful intelligence are already moving in opposite directions. Scarce frontier capacity can sustain a premium while yesterday's capability diffuses into cheaper models.

Between April 2024 and November 2025, The Price of Progress estimates that the price of a fixed level of benchmark performance fell by roughly 5x to 10x per year. Across the same sample, the inference spend required to run the best available model rose by about 3x to 18x per year across three benchmarks [5]. The same market can pay more for the frontier and less for the capability most users need.

For investors, the frontier premium is attached to a moving target. Each new model can command scarcity pricing, but its performance becomes cheaper and faces higher risk of substitution as the rest of the market catches up, so the premium applies to a narrower set of tasks where marginal intelligence changes the outcome.

Routers are the new bridges

Routing turns model choice from a long-term vendor commitment into a per-task optimisation problem. OpenRouter puts more than 400 models from over 80 providers behind one API, and changing the model at the API boundary can be a one-string edit. Stripe's agreement to acquire OpenRouter is an acknowledgement of how valuable that abstraction layer has become [6]. Companies can reserve the frontier model for the few tasks that need it, then route everything else towards the cheapest model that clears its quality bar.

Usage can move quickly once credible alternatives sit behind the same endpoint. DeepSeek's share of OpenRouter token volume doubled from 9% in January to 18% in June, making it the platform's leading model creator [7]. This is one platform's token volume rather than global revenue or users, but it shows how little loyalty the model layer can command when price and performance are visible in the same place.

Production teams will still need evaluations, prompts, guardrails and fallback testing when models behave differently. Routing only needs models to be substitutable for enough work to put pressure on model pricing. Once the application decides which model handles each task, the lab with the best model no longer captures the economics of the whole workflow by default.

In a similar way, crypto’s bridges and aggregators abstracted away specific chain loyalty (or dependancy). Since then, we have seen many applications in crypto gain real adoption in which the user does not see, nor care, which chain they are using on the backend. In fact, most applications nowadays are multichain by default, when a few years ago, they typically would pick a home chain and stick with it. This trend has begun in the AI world, and over the coming years we will see it play out in the same way we have seen in crypto.

Applications own the customer

As intelligence becomes cheaper and easier to route, durable value moves towards products that own the customer and infrastructure competitors cannot quickly reproduce. Most software features built on a general model will become easier to copy, while proprietary data systems, difficult integrations and real operational infrastructure remain harder to prompt into existence. At @CambrianNetwork, for example, the product depends on heavy data infrastructure, running nodes and specialist knowledge that a thin software wrapper cannot recreate. As we say internally, ‘become unpromptable’.

Polymarket and Polygon show where the economics really accrue to. Looking at a snapshot of late August, Polymarket generated $15.3m of retained protocol revenue over 30 days against $2.1m for Polygon's base layer, 7.3 times as much [10]. The customer relationship still sits with Polymarket while Polygon is simply the infrastructure that processes the transactions underneath it.

OpenAI and Anthropic already have assets that most L1s never had: distribution, brand, trust and direct customer relationships. Their route to durable value is to use today's model supremacy to build products people genuinely want to use and incorporate into their daily life. This is something that we never typically saw from crypto L1s and is perhaps a lesson that the labs have learnt. Third-party companies can capture the same shift when they have stronger distribution or infrastructure the labs cannot easily reproduce.

It's all distribution and infrastructure

Justifying today's valuations requires OpenAI and Anthropic to become exceptional application and distribution businesses built on top of intelligence. At valuations approaching $1tn, it is not enough to simply have the best intelligence; similarly, it was not enough for an L1 to simply be the fastest or cheapest. The labs need to focus on products and infrastructure that cannot be prompted into existence, and that take advantage of their unique knowledge of users’ needs.

The comparison with L1s is economic rather than literal. Lab equity isn't a token, models aren't fungible smart contracts and AI capacity doesn't clear through an onchain gas auction. However, as crypto has shown us, markets can sharply reprice technical scarcity once it stops implying category dominance.

Ultimately, the key investment question is who owns the customer and workflows once the intelligence is commoditised. At current valuations, this doesn’t feel like a question the market is currently asking, but it undoubtedly will be in the coming months/years, particularly after we likely see some of the biggest IPOs in history. For me, I would rather own the applications with the greatest distribution and the infrastructure which remains unpromptable, because as intelligence becomes abundant, nothing else will have a moat.
