# China’s Private Rocket Maker Just Landed a Booster Like SpaceX’s Falcon‑9—Here’s How They Did It

## Event Overview
- On 19 August 2026 LandSpace successfully recovered the first‑stage booster of its Zhuque‑3 (ZQ‑3) rocket on the second attempt.  
- The booster performed a vertical, leg‑supported landing in Minqin County, Gansu Province, about 390 km downrange from the launch site.  
- The flight marked the first land‑based, Falcon‑9‑style recovery by a Chinese commercial launch provider.

## Technical Details
- **Rocket size and propulsion**: ZQ‑3 is comparable in size to SpaceX’s Falcon 9 and uses a stainless‑steel structure with liquid‑oxygen/methane engines.  
- **Landing method**: Two retro‑propulsion burns followed by deployment of landing legs, mirroring Falcon 9’s sequence.  
- **Design trade‑offs**: Landing legs reduce payload capacity but enable rapid refurbishment and reuse without extensive ground‑equipment repairs.  
- **Materials and cost**: The stainless‑steel body and methane propulsion could lower launch costs relative to Falcon 9.  
- **Improvement after prior failure**: Added thermal‑protection reinforcement and gas cylinders to purge methane, and reduced the number of landing‑ignition engines from five to three.

## Company Background
- **Founding**: LandSpace (蓝箭航天) was established in June 2015, shortly after China opened its aerospace sector to private capital.  
- **Growth**: The firm now employs over 1,200 people; its IPO application was accepted by the Shanghai Stock Exchange on 31 December 2025.  
- **Self‑reliance**: Early development of in‑house engines (since 2017), test stands, launch sites, and factories allowed rapid iteration without dependence on external suppliers.  
- **Industry context**: China hosts more than 20 private rocket companies and over 80 rocket models, making commercial spaceflight a rapidly expanding sector.

## Interview Insights (Dong Kai, Deputy Chief Designer of Zhuque‑3)

- **Post‑failure actions**:  
  - Reinforced thermal protection and added methane‑purge gas cylinders.  
  - Analyzed telemetry and optical data within two days; completed fault review by 5 December 2025.  
  - Followed a “presumption of guilt” investigative method, eliminating impossible causes until the true fault was identified.

- **Timeline**: Fault identification and corrective actions took about two to three months before the successful August launch.

- **Data‑driven design updates**:  
  - Compared flight data with ground simulations; adjusted models where deviations appeared.  
  - Reduced landing‑ignition engines from five to three after confirming three engines provided sufficient thrust, thereby lowering complexity and failure risk.

- **Philosophy**: Emphasizes solving engineering problems directly, avoiding unnecessary additions, and iterating based on empirical data rather than speculative designs.
