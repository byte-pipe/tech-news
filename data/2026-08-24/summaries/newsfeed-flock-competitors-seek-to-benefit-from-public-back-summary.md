# Flock competitors seek to benefit from public backlash : NPR

# Summary of “Flock competitors seek to benefit from public backlash”

## Background
- Dozens of cities have canceled contracts with Flock Safety, a company that sells automated license‑plate readers (ALPRs).
- Some municipalities are replacing Flock equipment with products from other vendors such as Axon, Motorola Solutions, and Verkada.
- A grassroots group called DeFlock DC is organizing opposition to Flock’s presence in Washington, D.C.

## Why Flock is facing intense criticism
- Flock’s ALPRs capture images of vehicle backs and use AI to record make, model, color, and other details.
- The company maintains a shared national database that allows police to search images from any jurisdiction, raising concerns about widespread surveillance.
- High‑profile incidents, such as the use of Flock data in a Texas abortion‑investigation and alleged stalking of women, have amplified public anger.
- Privacy advocates argue the database can be accessed by federal immigration agencies and other entities without adequate oversight.

## Competitors moving in
- Axon, known for body‑camera technology, reports increasing sales of its own ALPR units as cities drop Flock contracts.
- Motorola Solutions and Verkada also market similar license‑plate‑reader systems that have been used by law‑enforcement for years.
- Axon’s CEO highlighted “controls and privacy work” as a selling point, though the company declined to disclose how much business it has gained from former Flock customers.
- Denver and Longmont, Colorado, signed new deals with Axon after terminating agreements with Flock.

## Privacy and surveillance concerns remain
- Experts note that switching vendors does not eliminate surveillance; it merely replaces one camera system with another.
- Integrated policing tools—facial‑recognition, gun‑shot detectors, predictive policing, drones, and real‑time crime centers—pose broader privacy risks than ALPRs alone.
- The Electronic Frontier Foundation’s senior policy analyst stresses that the visibility of Flock’s cameras made the issue more salient, but the underlying technology landscape is unchanged.

## Responses from Flock and other stakeholders
- Flock announced changes such as shorter data‑retention periods and tighter safeguards on local data searches, which the ACLU called “largely unsatisfying.”
- Flock CEO Garrett Langley expressed confidence, claiming the company will add many new customers despite recent cancellations.
- Axon spokesperson emphasized the company’s long‑standing commitment to data protection and privacy in its communications with law‑enforcement agencies.

## Outlook
- The “deFlock” movement has led to roughly 100 cities deactivating or rejecting Flock cameras, according to DeFlock.org.
- While competitors are capitalizing on Flock’s decline, privacy advocates warn that the broader surveillance ecosystem continues to expand, and public scrutiny has not yet focused on many of those other technologies.
