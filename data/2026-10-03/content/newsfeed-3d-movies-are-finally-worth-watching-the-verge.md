# 3D movies are finally worth watching

﻿It took decades, but the technology is finally ready.

by
 
 
Sean Hollister
Oct 3, 2026, 12:00 PM UTC
* Link
* Share
* Gift
Sean Hollister
 
is a senior editor and founding member of The Verge who covers gadgets, games, and toys. He spent 15 years editing the likes of CNET, Gizmodo, and Engadget.

Why am I suddenly buying up every 3D Blu-ray I can find after 3D became one of thebiggest tech flopsof all time?

The technology’s finally ready for 3D movies to shine, more than 15 years since James Cameron’sAvatarwowed me on the big screen and went on to become thehighest-grossing filmworldwide. Video glasses are giving me the experience I’ve always desired.

I’ve always loved 3D movies, but viewing them was awkward. Walking into a movie theater with a pair of polarized glasses, I would only get half the resolution and roughly half the brightness, since those glasses had to split one reflected image between my two eyes. Almost every 3D technology cuts something you care about in half, whether it’s resolution (see also: Nintendo 3DS) or the refresh rate and flicker you get with active shutter glasses.

But what if you had a full screen for each eye? That’s what video glasses can provide.

The Xreal glasses have to be plugged into a USB-C source of 3D content, like a laptop or handheld or phone.
 
Photo by Amelia Holowaty Krales / The Verge
Standalone VR headsets like the Steam Frame, Meta Quest, and Apple Vision Pro also work.
 
Photo: Kelsey McClellan / The Verge

Last year, I began testing a pair ofXreal One Pro glasses, which primarily give you a gigantic flat screen, like a private theater, for whatever you plug in. But that screen doesn’ttechnicallyneed to stay flat. If you press and hold the right button, Xreal can give you independent left and right views for side-by-side (SBS 3D) content. With proper content, you get a full 1080p view for each eye.

There are SBS 3D toggles in popular video player apps for VR headsets like theMeta QuestandSteam Frame, too — and the Apple Vision Pro and upcoming Meta VR Glasses let you natively stream 3D films.

They’re breathtaking.

The water inMoana, a glass-covered clock inZootopia 2, the helmets inGravity, suiting up in giant robots inPacific Rim, lightcycles inTron: Legacy, comic book panels coming to life inInto the Spider-Verse— there’s just so much more depth to see.

When the ocean parts to let Moana through, it no longer feels like looking at a flat wall of water. It’s a living chunk of ocean with life swimming inside.The Lego Moviewent from “wow those are some realistic-looking CG Lego bricks on a flat screen” to “I just got teleported into an entire Lego diorama.” InAvatar, the holographic displays in the ops room are mind-blowing. Even the flat rectangular monitors have 3D layers within.

Almost everything in 
Avatar 
looks amazing in 3D, but the ops room is a special treat.
 
Image: Avatar.com

Cockpits and helmets andTron: Legacylightcycles andMad Max: Fury Road’s convoy of battling vehicles — anything with transparent glass is a treat. There’s distinct detail on the faces inside the glass, the structures outside, beads of water or scratches on the glass itself, even reflections of the outside world, depending on the film.

Billie Eilish’s new concert film, shot by James Cameron with 16twincameras including fourremote-controlled AGITO camera rigs, looked incredible in the Meta VR Glasses during a brief demo. He got up close and personal with the concert, like few VR concert recordings do. In a demo with theGoogle / Xreal Aura, I watched a tiny bit of the interactive nature documentaryGalapagos: The Last Edenand was entranced by the gorgeous baby seals, the grains of sand in their shiny fur, the fuzzy depth of the hair on their skin.

Regardless of the hardware, it almost feels like I can reach out and touch a character’s face in 3D, feel their emotions and wrinkles and scars more vividly, once they have depth.

Or, in the case of Werner Herzog’scult-classic 3D documentaryCave of Forgotten Dreams, I could appreciate how the world’s oldest surviving art isn’t flat — the 32,000-year-old cave paintings took form thanks to their three-dimensional canvas. “A bulging rock is now a bulging neck of a bison that attacks you,” Herzogexplained to usearlier this year.

The 
incredible Chauvet-Pont D’Arc cave paintings
 aren’t quite as incredible in a flat image.

When I fired upGravityfor what I thought would be a brief test, late at night in bed, I wound up watching the entire film. It all feels a bit like when I bought my first decent set of headphones — I never knew what I was missing, but now I want to revisit all my favorites to experience them more deeply.

Not all 3D content is equal. Few live-action movies were truly filmed with twin cameras in recent years. In fake or poorly converted 3D movies, you’ll often see a “billboard” effect where it looks like there are flat layers of people or objects in the background instead of true depth.

Even “fake 3D” can intrigue. Watching this scene from 
Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness
 in Meta’s VR Glasses, the kids were awkwardly billboarding — but my eyes were drawn to the couch’s textured blanket, the objects on the mantlepiece, the cartoon on the TV.
 
Image: Disney Plus

But the biggest catch is that 3D movies aren’t easy to just buy and play. Even though 3D films are still coming to theaters, you can’t necessarily stream them or buy a high-quality copy. 4K Blu-ray doesn’t support 3D at all. 3D Blu-rays were the last discs to offer 3D, and asFlatPanelsHDpointed out last year, that means most physical 3D movies are stuck in the past at 1080p, 24fps, without HDR.

Even 3D Blu-rays aren’t necessarily easy to find: I had to nab a copy ofCave of Forgotten Dreamsoff eBay, and paid a pretty penny to ship a rare-ish copy ofInto the Spider-Versefrom Thailand.

Here’s the good news: The leading headset makers are starting to convince Hollywood it’s time to stream their 3D collections instead.

In 2024, the Apple Vision Pro launched with by far the best 3D movie experience yet, now with 4K, HDR, spatial audio, some (Avatar) films at 48fps, HEVC encoding, free 3D upgrades for many films you’d already purchased in 2D, and exclusive access to the largest catalog of 3D films in existence on Disney Plus. (Modern Disney animated films are natively rendered in digital 3D, and most Disney-owned Marvel films got a 3D conversion to jazz up their theatrical appeal.)

Soon, you’ll no longer need a $3,500 Apple headset to watch those films or convert them from old discs — you’ll be able to stream them with a $350 Meta Quest 3S or $600 Quest 3, as well as the new $1,300 Meta VR Glasses, Meta director of entertainment Sarah Malkin confirms toThe Verge.

Meta’s headset will offer 3D movies to subscribers in the Disney Plus, HBO, Paramount Plus, and Peacock apps, she says, with some of that starting later this fall.

Meta has seemingly broken Apple’s exclusivity over next-gen 3D content. 
Here are some of my other thoughts on Meta’s VR Glasses.
 
Photo: Meta

And by the time the Meta VR Glasses launch in spring, there will be “hundreds of 3D films available” to rent or buy though Meta’s TV app, as well as some free 3D content. They shouldn’t cost more than traditional movies, either, she says: “Our 3D movies will also be in line with typical pricing for 2D Ultra High Definition (UHD) films.”

(In case you’re wondering, she also says the Meta VR Glasses’ three-hour battery life quote should cover a full 3D movie “watched in Theater mode if starting with the device fully charged.”)

In the meanwhile, I’m experiencing 3D the hacky way: by ripping discs. Every time I walk into a thrift store, I hunt for a 3D Blu-ray or two. I pop them into an old external USB Blu-ray reader, thenlet MakeMKVspend half an hour per movie to produce a 30-to-40GB multi-view (MVC) file. I pop thatinto BD3D2MK3Dfor another 50 minutes per movie to spit out a high-quality 3840 x 1080 “Full-SBS” file that’s half the size of the MVC.

I drop that onto an SD card, pop it into the Steam Deck, plug in the Xreal glasses, fire up VLC Media Player, then flip the glasses into 3D mode. It’s a lot of friction, but for me, the payoff is worth it.

Even though I’m mostly watching old movies, it feels like a taste of the future. But you know what they say: Everything old is eventually newagain.
