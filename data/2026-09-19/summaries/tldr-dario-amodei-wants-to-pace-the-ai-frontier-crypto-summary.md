# AI Labs’ Call to ‘Pace Frontier’ Gives Crypto Vets FTX Flashbacks  

## Overview  
- The article examines Dario Amodei’s essay “We Must Pace the Frontier,” which proposes slowing AI capability gains through third‑party evaluation, industry coordination, and eventual global agreements.  
- It draws parallels between Amodei’s approach and the narrative surrounding Sam Bankman‑Fried and FTX, suggesting a familiar pattern of a powerful player seeking top‑down regulation.  

## Amodei’s Three‑Step Plan  
1. Anthropic would grant embedded, employee‑level access to independent evaluators (e.g., METR) to verify safety practices and report incidents.  
2. Frontier labs in democratic nations would coordinate on shared safety standards and speed limits, requiring antitrust waivers from governments.  
3. A global coordination framework, including China, would address pre‑release testing and limits on recursive self‑improvement.  

## Immediate Industry Reaction

- Former President Trump dismissed the safety push, emphasizing U.S. leadership over China in AI.  
- Tech investor David Sacks urged labs to pace themselves without seeking antitrust waivers or treating METR as a neutral referee.  
- Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg argued that existing liability and self‑policing are sufficient, citing Meta’s unilateral delay of its Muse launch.  

## Crypto‑Era Comparison  
- The essay’s tone evokes the FTX era, where a single influential entity framed existential risk language to justify privileged treatment.  
- The timing coincided with Anthropic’s internal turmoil (Jacob Coxon’s resignation) and public warnings about a >10 % chance of AI causing catastrophic harm within a decade.  

## Critical Assessment of the Proposal  
- **Operational issues:** Amodei cites alignment incidents caused by flawed reinforcement‑learning pipelines; these are process failures that can be addressed through better quality assurance without external coordination.  
- **Research and technical challenges:** Alignment, interpretability, and testing are technical problems that labs can tackle competitively; they do not inherently require an industry cartel or government mandate.  
- **Democratic coordination:** The call for joint safety standards and antitrust waivers adds regulatory complexity that existing product‑liability law, reputational risk, and market forces already address.  
- Historical precedents (OpenAI’s 2019 decision not to release full GPT‑2, Meta’s Muse delay) show that unilateral restraint is possible without industry‑wide agreements.  

## Takeaways  
- While AI poses genuine risks, many of the concerns Amodei highlights can be managed through internal controls, market incentives, and existing legal frameworks.  
- The push for a coordinated “pace” may serve more as a strategic moat for dominant labs than a necessary safety mechanism.  
- The article cautions against conflating genuine safety work with attempts to secure regulatory advantage, echoing lessons from the crypto‑FTX episode.
