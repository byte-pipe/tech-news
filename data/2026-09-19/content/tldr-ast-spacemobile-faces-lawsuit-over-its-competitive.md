The satellite-to-phone provider AST SpaceMobile is facing a class action lawsuit from an investor, who claims the company overstated its ability to compete with SpaceX’s Starlink Mobile.

The lawsuit comes from a company shareholder named Edward Hunter, who is accusing AST SpaceMobile of violating federal securities laws by making “materially false and misleading” claims about the company’s business operations. Courthouse News Service firstreportedon the lawsuit, which was filed in a US district court this week.

In the 39-pagecomplaint, Hunter’s lawyers specifically allege the company “overstated the durability of AST’s competitive position in the satellite D2C (direct to cell) market,” which involves using orbiting satellites to deliver communications services to phones on the ground.

The complaint argues that AST’s competitiveness has been in question ever since SpaceX in Sept. 2025 acquired valuable radio spectrum from Boost Mobile’s parent EchoStar to eventuallyupgradethe company’s competing satellite-to-phone service in Starlink Mobile. “Defendants failed to disclose, inter alia, that AST’s competitive position in the satellite D2C market was not as strong or durable as Defendants had led investors to believe,” the complaint says at one point.

The Texas-based AST has beenworkingto launch dozens of “BlueBird” satellites to offer space-based communications to partners in AT&T and Verizon, giving them a way to eliminate cellular dead zones for their customers. A major goal is to power "over 150 Mbps per coverage cell." But the company is facing a major rival in SpaceX, which has already been offering Starlink MobilethroughT-Mobile.

SpaceX’s plans for Starlink Mobile have only become more ambitious with plans to offer satellite-to-phone connectivity with 150Mbps speeds per user using next-gen satelliteslaunchingnext year. Company executives have also been indicating that Starlink Mobile will even try to compete against the big three US carriers by harnessing satellites and ground-basedfemtocells, instead of traditional cell towers.

Meanwhile, AST SpaceMobile has been positioning itself as an alternative that AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon could harness through anew joint ventureon satellite-to-phone services. However, Hunter’s complaint alleges that AST purportedly stated its “unique competitive advantages and leadership position in the satellite D2C market.” His other gripe is that AST allegedly misled investors with its “increasing capital requirements” to compete with Starlink Mobile, and how the growing debt load resulted in share dilution, and thus losses for shareholders.

Hunter purchased a total of 18 shares in November 2025 and then this past June, at around $74. In May, AST’s stock price briefly soared to $133, but has since fallen to $58, with Hunter claiming he bought the shares at artificially inflated prices. His lawsuit is now demanding AST pay up in damages to affected investors who bought shares between March 4, 2025 and July 15, 2026.

AST SpaceMobile didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment. But in July, the company officiallydelayedits plan to kick off service from later this year to early 2027, after one of its launch providers, Blue Origin, suffered a major blow when its New Glenn rocketexploded during a ground test. AST had been aiming to operate at least 45 satellites by the end of 2026. But the company only has 13 BlueBirds in orbit, if you also count the company’s prototype BlueWalker 3 satellite.
