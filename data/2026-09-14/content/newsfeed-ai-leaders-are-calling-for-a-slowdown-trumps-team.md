As calls forartificial intelligence regulation intensify, US President Donald Trump’s team have made it clear that if lab leaders want to implement anindustry-wide slowdown, they’re going to have to do it themselves.

Anthropic chief executiveDario Amodeicalled on the US government to regulate AI companies within a wider effortto slow the paceof the technology’s development in an essay published Saturday. The essay received rare endorsement from both OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman andElon Musk, who owns chatbot Grok, following a week of intense debate about the technology’s threats to human safety. Microsoft chief executive officer Satya Nadella also called for pacing development.

Calls for AI slowdown andregulationhave intensified after a number of researchers voiced major concerns in the last week about the speed of the technology’s advance. “AI progress is increasing,” former DeepMind researcher Rishub Jaintold WIRED. “And as AI becomes more capable, it poses more risks.”

But David Sacks, co-chair of the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, wrote on X, “If the unreleased models are scary enough that you think you should slow down, I support your decision to be responsible. But stop pretending you need anyone else’s permission. Stop pretending antitrust law has to be suspended so you can form a cartel … Most of all, stop pretending the motivation to slow down is purely altruistic.”

Amodei had requested the government enable competing AI companies to work together on standards without breaching antitrust law, either by participating in discussions or issuing a waiver for the companies involved. He also called for government assistance in establishing international coordination, including with the Chinese Communist Party, though critics wondered how effective this would be. “China is very unlikely to join a global agreement, as you know,” Sacks wrote.

Concerns about the US ceding its lead in the technology to China have caused the US government to mostly push back against the notion of slowing the pace of its development. “You have a lot of very negative forces that are bringing it up that shouldn’t be bringing it up and they’re bringing up things that won’t happen,” Trump said during a visit to Ireland. “We’re leading China on AI,” he added, “and, frankly, I want to keep it that way because whoever wins AI, wins."

The US largely viewsChina’s AI ecosystemas an economic and national security threat and has previously been caught short by the country’sadvancesin the technology. “If Congress just races in and does some sort of emergency session to try to regulate AI, we will lose the race to China, and that is a threat to every single American,” House Speaker Mike Johnson told CNN on Sunday. “So, we’ve got to have balance. We’ve got to have steady hands at the wheel.”

In the meantime, companies say they are going to start taking voluntary steps to reestablish public trust that they are acting responsibly. On Sunday night, OpenAI’s Altman committed to a proposal by Amodei to give embedded third-party evaluators employee-level access to their firms to check that practices and products are safe and to report incidents. While these measures will have “significant costs,” he wrote on X: “Pacing will be well worth this cost; no amount of American competitive pressure should justify recklessness.”
