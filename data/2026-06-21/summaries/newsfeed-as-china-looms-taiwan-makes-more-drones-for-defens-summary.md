# Summary of “As China looms, Taiwan makes more drones for defense and the US military”

## Defense strategy and budget
- Taiwan plans to spend **$6.6 billion** over six years (2026‑2031) on domestically produced drones, targeting 208,000 coastal‑attack drones, 1,400 reconnaissance drones, and 1,320 uncrewed surface vessels.  
- Current inventory includes about **5,000 U.S.–made attack drones** plus a small number of locally built units.  
- Recent exercises demonstrated use of U.S.‑made Altius‑600 loitering munitions and Taiwan‑made drones for offshore strikes.  
- The budget proposal aims to break a legislative deadlock; opposition parties had previously vetoed funding for home‑grown drones.

## Drone business boom and international partnerships
- **Thunder Tiger** became the first Asian firm on the U.S. Pentagon’s “Blue Uncrewed Aircraft Systems Cleared List,” certifying its FPV drones ($3‑5 k each) for military use.  
- The company now offers larger kamikaze drones (~$30 k) based on the U.S. LUCAS platform, itself derived from Iran’s Shahed drones.  
- State‑run **NCSIST** developed a one‑way attack drone modeled on Israel’s Harpy.  
- Taiwanese firms export components and complete systems:  
  - $115 million in fully assembled drones (Jan‑Mar 2026), surpassing the full‑year 2025 total of $93 million.  
  - Components supplied to U.S. DoD’s $1 billion “Drone Dominance” program and to Ukrainian manufacturers.  
  - Thunder Tiger opened a Ohio plant capable of producing >60,000 drone motors annually.  
- Software collaborations: NCSIST partners with Anduril, Auterion, Shield AI; Thunder Tiger licenses Auterion AI; Ubiqconn integrates AeroVironment software for multi‑system control.

## Challenges and future outlook
- Political friction remains; the budget needed to overcome opposition in the legislature.  
- Competing with **DJI** (70‑80 % global commercial market) is difficult; Taiwanese drones are often 2‑3× more expensive.  
- Supply‑chain purity is an issue: some drones contain chips packaged in China, prompting scrutiny.  
- Current production capacity is ~15,000 drones/month; the Ministry of Economic Affairs targets >100,000/month by 2030.  
- Ukraine’s rapid scale‑up (from a few thousand to millions of FPV drones) serves as a model for Taiwan’s ambitions.  
- Civil‑defense groups are training citizens in drone operation, acknowledging that most systems still rely heavily on human pilots.
