# As ILO convention turns 30, India’s home‑based workers demand equal rights

## Background
- The article follows Shehnaz Bano, a 38‑year‑old mother of two who stitches leather jacket pieces in a one‑room home in New Delhi’s Kapashera neighbourhood.  
- Bano earns about 100 rupees (≈ $1) per piece, far less than the $200 + price of the finished jacket sold abroad.  
- Home‑based workers (HBWs) form a large, feminised segment of the global informal economy – an estimated 260 million people, 57 % of whom are women (WIEGO, 2024).  
- HBWs typically face low wages, lack of social security, irregular hours and unsafe conditions.

## ILO Convention 177
- Adopted on 20 June 1996 at the ILO conference in Geneva; entered into force on 22 April 2000.  
- Recognises HBWs as “real work” and calls for equal treatment with other wage earners.  
- Only 13 countries have ratified the convention; none from South Asia, despite the region’s concentration of HBWs and its role in fashion and manufacturing supply chains.  
- Activist Renana Jhabvala (SEWA) recalls the optimism at the 1996 adoption, while experts note that three decades later the convention has had limited impact in India.

## Situation of Home‑Based Workers in India
- Most Indian HBWs are women and are often invisible to policymakers.  
- Work is frequently organised through complex subcontracting, making employment relationships hard to identify and regulate.  
- Two main categories:  
  1. **Own‑account workers** – direct market access.  
  2. **Piece‑rate workers** – employed via intermediaries; more vulnerable due to arbitrary, low piece rates (e.g., Bano).  
- Workers combine domestic duties with production in cramped spaces; many cannot seek external employment because of childcare responsibilities (e.g., Sangeeta Devi).

## Voices from the Field
- **Shehnaz Bano:** “Just because I work from home, I don’t get equal pay or rights.” She migrated from beedi‑rolling in Uttar Pradesh to leather‑stitching in Delhi, but still faces long hours, eye strain and minimal earnings.  
- **Sangeeta Devi:** Works in an 8 × 8 ft room shared with her family; earns a dollar for every 100 garment pieces; cannot leave home due to childcare duties.  
- **Deepa Bharathi (ILO Decent Work Team):** Highlights invisibility, gender bias, and subcontracting as barriers to ratification and implementation; stresses need for visibility, fair pay, social protection, safe conditions, training, childcare and collective voice.

## Challenges and Recommendations
- **Low ratification:** Only 13 ratifications; South Asia’s non‑participation linked to gendered undervaluation of home work and fragmented subcontracting.  
- **Regulatory gaps:** Difficulty in labour inspection, lack of data, and absence of HBWs in policy frameworks hinder progress.  
- **Suggested priorities (ILO):**  
  - Increase visibility of women HBWs.  
  - Ensure fair wages and social protection.  
  - Provide safe working environments and access to training and childcare.  
  - Strengthen collective representation for HBWs.  

The article underscores that, thirty years after the adoption of Convention 177, Indian home‑based workers like Bano and Devi continue to fight for the equal rights and protections the convention promised.
