# Norway became a global salmon behemoth. Now it's facing the consequences

## Origins and market creation
- By the mid‑1980s Norway succeeded in breeding a fast‑growing, docile Atlantic salmon that could be kept in sea pens.  
- Japan, the world’s largest potential market, traditionally avoided raw salmon, preferring tuna.  
- The government launched “Project Japan,” a ten‑year campaign that sold 5,000 t of salmon to Japan and promoted salmon sushi through the Norwegian embassy in Tokyo.  
- The effort succeeded; salmon sushi is now a staple in Japanese cuisine and a global street‑food item.

## Industry scale and economic role
- Norway now supplies more than half of the world’s farmed Atlantic salmon, an $18 billion sector and the country’s second‑largest export after oil.  
- The industry is about 18 times the size of Tasmania’s and operates farms in Scotland, the Faroe Islands and Chile.  
- Large circular pens, each holding up to 200 000 fish, line the fjords, turning remote villages into industrial hubs and creating tens of thousands of jobs.  
- Politicians and powerful lobby groups view salmon farming as “the new oil,” positioning it as a cornerstone of Norway’s future economy.

## Environmental impacts
- Open‑net pens release fish waste, uneaten feed and chemicals directly into the water, acting as fertilizer that fuels harmful algal blooms.  
- Blooms choke kelp forests, deplete oxygen, and can cause massive die‑offs of marine life.  
- Rising sea temperatures intensify algal growth, while agricultural runoff and industrial pollution add further nutrients.  
- Studies show oxygen levels in deep fjord waters, such as the Hardangerfjord, have fallen by up to one‑third since the 1950s, threatening ecosystem health.  
- Legacy pesticides and heavy metals persist in the water for decades, indicating long‑term contamination.

## Scientific debate and regulation
- Biologist Tom Pedersen, a regional regulator, links oxygen loss to both climate‑driven warming and nutrient pollution from salmon farms, and has rejected permit applications that would quadruple farm size in his fjord.  
- Sjømat Norge spokesperson Krister Hoaas disputes the claim that fish farming is causing oxygen declines, highlighting contested interpretations of the data.  
- Photographer and freediver Aleksander Nordahl’s three‑year visual record shows rapid degradation of kelp habitats near farms, contrasting sharply with pristine areas farther away.

## Outlook
- The industry’s rapid expansion has sparked a national conversation about ecological limits and the sustainability of treating salmon farming as a post‑oil growth engine.  
- Ongoing scientific disagreement and regulatory push‑back suggest future growth may be constrained unless more closed‑system or environmentally‑friendly farming methods are adopted.
