# Let Me And A Food Scientist Sample The Abandoned Fish Sauce Terrorizing A Small Canadian Town

## Background
- St. Mary’s, Newfoundland and Labrador, a coastal town of ~300 residents, has been plagued by a massive fish‑sauce odor for over two decades.  
- The smell originates from 110 abandoned vats (≈10 ft tall each) that contain about 900,000 L of fermented capelin (small silvery fish) mixed with salt.  
- The vats were left behind when the Atlantic Seafood Sauce Company shut down in 2001 after regulatory issues.

## Cleanup Effort
- In 2019 residents began boarding up homes each summer to keep the odor out; a failed cleanup attempt occurred in 2016.  
- The provincial government allocated $2 million, and a local engineering firm started removal in July 2026, aiming to finish by October.  
- Process: cut open vats, pump viscous brown fish “goop” onto the floor, mix with peat moss, load onto ~200 trucks, and deposit in a polyethylene‑lined landfill sealed like a Ziploc.  
- Mayor Steve Ryan expressed emotional relief, hoping to end the “sauce plant mayor” label.

## Fish Sauce Chemistry (Interview with Food Scientist Bryan Quoc Le)
- Traditional fish sauce: gut enzymes break down proteins into amino acids; high salt preserves the mixture, suppresses pathogens, and enhances umami by providing sodium ions.  
- Glutamic acid (≈30‑40 % of protein) is the primary source of umami; pure glutamic acid tastes soapy without sodium.  
- Typical fermentation lasts about a year; the St. Mary’s vats have been fermenting for 20+ years, a timescale with little scientific literature.

## Long‑Term Fermentation Insights
- Slow‑growing yeasts and halophilic bacteria can survive high‑salt conditions and continue metabolizing over decades, similar to century‑old Korean soy sauces.  
- As microbes die, they release enzymes that further break down proteins, creating a continuous, slow fermentation.  
- Maillard reactions between amino acids and trace sugars can occur at room temperature over many years, darkening the sauce and generating complex flavor compounds.  
- Seasonal temperature swings (cold winters, milder summers) likely create a “refrigerated‑then‑warmed” fermentation cycle, adding another layer of variability.  
- The dominant volatile compounds are amines, produced when bacteria decarboxylate amino acids; humans are highly sensitive to amines because they signal decay, contributing to the overpowering stench.

## Community Impact
- Residents report that the odor is so strong in summer they cannot hang laundry or keep windows open, prompting seasonal boarding of homes.  
- The cleanup is seen as a restoration of quality of life and a chance for the town to move beyond the “sauce plant” stigma.  
- The interview highlights both the scientific curiosity about the unique, decades‑long fermentation and the human toll of living beside a massive, uncontrolled food‑processing waste.
