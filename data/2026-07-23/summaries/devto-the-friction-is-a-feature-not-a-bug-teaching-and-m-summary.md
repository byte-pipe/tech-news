# The Friction Is A Feature, Not A Bug: Teaching and Mentoring in the Age of AI

## Core Thesis
- Cognitive friction is essential for deep learning; removing it with AI tools turns learning into superficial memorization.  
- The author likens this to Talmudic study: growth occurs when one wrestles with difficult material, not when answers are handed over.

## Personal Teaching Philosophy
- Teaching and mentoring have always been inseparable from good engineering for the author.  
- Effective mentors use the Socratic method, asking probing questions that force learners to confront assumptions and debug themselves.  
- The goal is to build mental resilience, not just to make tests pass.

## AI as a “Yes‑Man” in the IDE
- Modern LLMs act as pathological yes‑men: they accept prompts without challenge and spit out boilerplate or copy‑paste fixes.  
- This eliminates the struggle that creates lasting understanding.  
- Study by Anthropic (52 engineers, AI‑assisted vs. manual) showed a 17 % drop in conceptual mastery and debugging skill for the AI group, despite no meaningful speed gain.  
- A minority who treated the AI as a study partner—asking follow‑up questions and injecting their own friction—performed as well or better than the manual group.

## The Learner’s Dilemma
- Junior developers face a catch‑22:  
  1. They must use AI because it is now ubiquitous in daily workflows.  
  2. Blind reliance leads to “superficial learning,” fostering anxiety and impostor syndrome.  
- Some developers counteract by deliberately adding friction: explicit guard‑rail prompts, handwritten notes, timers, and manual problem‑solving.

## The Value of Messy Context
- Emily Bender and Alex Hanna’s *The AI Con* argues that friction in information access is a feature.  
- Manually hunting bugs forces exposure to noisy documentation, GitHub issue threads, and conflicting Stack Overflow answers, building a richer engineering context that AI‑generated answers flatten.

## Takeaway for Mentors and Educators
- Embrace friction as a design feature in curricula and tooling.  
- Use AI as a supplemental partner rather than a replacement for the Socratic dialogue.  
- Encourage learners to create their own obstacles (e.g., self‑imposed prompts, note‑taking) to preserve deep learning pathways.
