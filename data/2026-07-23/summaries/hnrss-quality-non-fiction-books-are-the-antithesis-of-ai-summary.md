# Quality non-fiction books are the antithesis of AI slop

## Personal experience in the library
- Worked a college work‑study job shelving books in the A‑F section of the Library of Congress system.  
- To break the monotony, read a random sentence from each book; sometimes continued for several pages when a passage caught interest.  
- Discovered that the classification system and circulation data provided a “filtered, randomized” sample of genuinely good books.  
- This self‑directed learning felt more valuable than many formal classes.

## Shifts in library environments
- Traditional open‑stack browsing is being replaced by Learning Labs, digital hubs, and social spaces.  
- Physical copies of eclectic, older titles are increasingly moving to e‑editions or being discarded.  
- Despite these changes, the core value remains the books themselves, especially non‑fiction.

## Non‑fiction’s current status
- Even as overall non‑fiction readership declines due to AI chatbots and podcasts, the author argues we are in a “golden age” for high‑quality non‑fiction.  
- Major non‑fiction awards serve as a reliable proxy for identifying outstanding works.

## The Book Prize Index project
- Created a free searchable platform that aggregates finalists and winners of major English‑language non‑fiction prizes.  
- Data collection was automated using Claude and GPT‑5.6, pulling information mainly from Wikipedia.  
- The author funds hosting and API costs personally; optional subscription is offered for support.  
- The tool uses semantic search (embedding models) to improve discovery beyond simple keyword matching.

## Key features of the platform
- Simple phrase searches (e.g., “modern France,” “social history”).  
- Complex queries like “classic biographies that are surprisingly weird” return curated results from ~6,500 titles.  
- “Books like” functionality surfaces lesser‑known titles similar to a known favorite.  
- Visualizations include:
  - Color‑based arrangement of ~5,000 books.  
  - Charts showing which imprints and publishers have earned the most non‑fiction awards over the past century.  

## Insights on award trends
- The Pulitzer Prize for non‑fiction began only in 1962.  
- Number of non‑fiction prizes grew through the 1970s‑1990s, peaked around 2014, and shows signs of a modest decline after 2020.  
- Despite any decline, the “long tail” of award‑winning non‑fiction from recent decades remains consistently strong; many titles are out of print or low‑ranked on commercial sites yet still highly acclaimed.

## Conclusion
- The author’s tool aims to recapture the serendipitous, rewarding experience of wandering a well‑curated library shelf.  
- By leveraging AI for semantic search while grounding the dataset in recognized literary merit, the platform helps readers discover quality non‑fiction that algorithmic recommendation engines often overlook.
