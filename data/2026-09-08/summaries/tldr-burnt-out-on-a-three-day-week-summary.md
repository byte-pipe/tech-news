# Burnt out on a three‑day week

## The story of Maya

- Maya spent over ten years rising to a director track at a health‑tech firm, then stepped back for a health issue.  
- She now works three days a week as a fractional product leader at a startup while job‑hunting for a return to enterprise roles.  
- Despite the light workload, she feels burnt out and traditional advice (more rest, quitting) hasn’t helped.  
- In interviews she “bombs,” leading her to doubt her abilities and apply for lower‑tier roles, yet still sees no success.  
- Coaching revealed a gap: she sees herself as a high‑performer but is coasting in the fractional role, prioritizing the job hunt over the startup work.  
- This misalignment creates a self‑perception conflict; each interview rejection reinforces the feeling of being a “liar” about her capabilities, deepening exhaustion.  

## Why workload isn’t the root cause

- Burnout can stem from a mismatch between who you are (or believe you are) and what the role demands, not just from volume of work.  
- The mismatch may arise from the environment (role not using strengths, hidden work, misaligned team dynamics) or from personal engagement (holding back, self‑talk).  
- When misaligned, evidence piles up (slow startup progress, interview rejections, irrelevant meetings) and rest alone cannot break the cycle.  
- The core variable is the size of the alignment gap, not the number of hours worked.  

## The case of Jordan

- Jordan, a director known for getting things done, was tasked with leading a critical product transformation after a reorg.  
- Additional responsibilities (covering parental leave, communications for another product) crowded his calendar, pushing the transformation work to the side.  
- He felt he was “not adding value,” really meaning he wasn’t doing the work he was hired to do, leading to poor executive presentations and mounting frustration.  
- He described his situation in passive, victim‑oriented language, attributing burnout to external forces rather than the internal inconsistency.  

## Key takeaways

- Burnout often reflects a gap between self‑identity and current work reality, not merely overwork.  
- Recognizing and naming the misalignment is a pivotal step toward closing the gap.  
- Rest and reduced hours help only when the underlying mismatch is addressed; otherwise the same patterns re‑emerge.  
- Shifting from a victim narrative to an active stance—identifying where the gap lies and realigning effort with core strengths—creates a path out of burnout.
