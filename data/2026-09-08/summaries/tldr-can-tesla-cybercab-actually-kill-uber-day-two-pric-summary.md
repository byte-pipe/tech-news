# Can Tesla Cybercab Actually Kill Uber? Day‑Two Pricing From Austin

## Overview of the day‑two test
- Farzad starts at The Domain (north edge of Tesla’s Robotaxi geofence) and books a ride to the UT Austin Department of Neuroscience (≈8 mi).  
- The purpose is to capture a live price and wait‑time comparison with Uber and to explore whether a purpose‑built, driver‑less car can threaten Uber’s business model once the fleet scales.

## Price and wait‑time snapshot
- **Tesla Robotaxi app**  
  - Model Y: ~7‑minute wait, $19.68.  
  - Cybercab: ~30‑minute wait, $14.87 (chosen for the test).  
- **Uber app (same route)**  
  - UberX: ~$25, ~2‑minute wait.  
  - Uber Comfort: ~$32, ~1‑minute wait.  
  - Uber Premiere: ~$56, ~1‑minute wait.  
- Result: Uber wins on wait, Cybercab wins on price in this single observation. Farzad attributes the long Cybercab wait to “launch congestion” as many riders are using the limited public‑window rides.

## Geofence coverage and fleet size
- Early Robotaxi service was limited to a small south‑Austin pocket; the Cybercab is now bookable across the entire geofence, including the north‑edge Domain pickup.  
- Farzad estimates about **40 Cybercabs** operating inside the fence versus **10‑12 Model Y** Robotaxis at launch.  
- Texas DMV records list **45 Cybercabs** on Tesla’s statewide authorized roster; the June 2025 Model Y open was reported at 10‑20 units.

## Why Uber’s economics look broken (Farzad’s view)
- **Cost structure**: No driver wages, no traditional car‑ownership costs, lower fuel/energy and maintenance expenses.  
- **Target cost per mile** (future): ≤ $0.40, potentially ≈ $0.20 if remote supervision stays light.  
- **Uber’s response**: Integrate other companies’ robotaxis (Waymo, Zoox, etc.) into the Uber app. Tesla has drawn a line—Cybercabs stay on Tesla’s own network, not on Uber.  
- **Pricing pressure**: If Tesla can offer lower per‑mile rates, Uber must either charge a premium (risk losing riders) or cut driver pay (thinning its human‑driver network).  
- **Vertical integration advantage**: Tesla owns manufacturing, the autonomous‑driving stack, and the customer app. Uber must pay for third‑party vehicles and still add its own markup, making it hard to match Tesla’s prices profitably.

## Political and rollout considerations
- **Job impact**: Driverless miles erode driving wages; Farzad argues that attempts to freeze these jobs only delay inevitable displacement.  
- **Regulatory risk**: If cities restrict Cybercabs while Tesla’s factories keep producing, Tesla may need a “steering‑wheel‑and‑pedals” variant of the chassis for sale to the public, keeping the production line busy.  
- **Fleet‑partner model**: Tesla posted a public‑interest form (Sept 3 2026) inviting fleet operators to purchase Cybercabs and keep them on the Robotaxi network, taking a network cut. Farzad speculates a possible 30/70 split between Tesla and operators (not a disclosed figure).

## What the ride demonstrated
- Arrival with the app’s green glow, butterfly doors opening, door closed via phone, and trip start.  
- Urban and campus traffic navigation; no highway capability yet (desired next step).  
- Exit: rear and side camera views displayed, allowing Farzad to leave safely; the empty car handles a crosswalk politely before stopping.

## Bottom line (Farzad’s thesis)
- The Domain‑to‑UT price is an early, limited‑window sample, not a nationwide Uber demise.  
- If Tesla can continuously dump purpose‑built, driver‑less cars onto its own app at fares that human‑driver networks cannot sustain, Uber’s challenge is not merely a better tip screen but the loss of ownership of both the factory and the customer relationship once wait times equalize.
