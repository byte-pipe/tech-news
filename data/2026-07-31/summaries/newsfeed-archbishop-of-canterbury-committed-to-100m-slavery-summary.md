## Main developments
- Archbishop of Canterbury Dame Sarah Mullally reaffirmed the Church of England’s commitment to a £100 million reparative‑justice fund during a visit to Cape Coast Castle, Ghana.  
- She described being “deeply moved” by the conditions in the slave dungeons and the “door of no return,” linking the experience to the Church’s historical involvement in the transatlantic slave trade.  
- Mullally emphasized that repentance in Christian belief requires concrete action to support communities still affected by the legacy of slavery.

## Context of the fund
- The £100 million fund was approved three years ago after an audit revealed historic Church endowments had invested in a company that trafficked slaves.  
- Intended uses include investment, research and engagement programmes aimed at communities harmed by the slave trade.  
- The plan has faced criticism questioning the accuracy of the Church’s historical culpability, legal feasibility, and ideological opposition.

## Historical background
- The Church of England has previously apologized for its role in the slave trade, noting that:
  - Its missionary arm operated slave‑farmed plantations in the Caribbean to fund Anglican expansion.  
  - Some clergy owned slaves.  
  - The Church received substantial donations from slave traders.  
- Cape Coast Castle, under British control since the 1660s, served as a holding site for enslaved Africans before their forced passage to the Americas; a chapel above the dungeons symbolizes the Church’s involvement.

## Reactions
