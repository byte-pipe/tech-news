# Summary of “We mould trees to grow into the shape of chairs” – BBC News  

## Background  
- Alice and Gavin Munro have spent the last 20 years perfecting a method of shaping living trees into functional furniture.  
- Gavin’s interest began as a child after seeing an overgrown bonsai that resembled a chair; his experience with Klippel‑Feil syndrome and spinal surgeries taught him patience.  
- After studying at Chesterfield College and working with driftwood in California, the pair founded Full Grown in Derbyshire in 2006.  

## Growth Process  
- Young tree branches are trained and pruned over custom‑made recycled‑plastic forms that dictate the desired shape.  
- Branches are grafted together at specific points so the piece becomes a single, solid structure.  
- The typical growth period is six to nine years; once the shape is achieved, the tree is harvested and dried for an additional year, making the total time up to ten years per item.  
- Species used include willow, oak and ash; the orchard currently holds a few dozen projects ranging from stools and benches to chandeliers.  

## Business Development  
- The first generation of chairs, lamps and experiments emerged in 2012 after extensive trial and error.  
- Gavin serves as the designer; Alice is the communications director.  
- Full Grown plans to launch the “Full Grown Academy” to teach others how to grow their own chairs, responding to interest from garden owners, museums and the public.  

## Public Exhibitions & Recognition  
- A bronze cast of one of their chairs will appear at the RHS Chelsea Flower Show in the Plant Heritage Missing Collector Garden, marking a major milestone for the duo.  
- Their chairs have been featured in a 2022 Louis Vuitton display in London and are held in collections at the Saint Louis Art Museum, San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, National Museum of Scotland, Rotterdam’s Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen and Manchester Metropolitan University.  

## Future Outlook  
- Gavin describes the venture as a “50‑ or 100‑year journey,” noting that the learning curve has been longer than initially expected (12‑13 years rather than 2‑3).  
- The upcoming academy aims to share the accumulated knowledge and expand the practice beyond their own orchard.
