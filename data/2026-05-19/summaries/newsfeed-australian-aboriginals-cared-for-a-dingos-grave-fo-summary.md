# Australian Aboriginals cared for a dingo's grave for decades – Ars Technica

## Background
- The Barkindji people’s ancestors buried a dingo (called “garli”) in a mound of river‑mussel shells about 1,000 years ago.  
- Dingoes arrived in Australia 3,500–5,000 years ago, quickly becoming integrated into First Nations cultures and stories.

## Discovery
- In Kinchega National Park (New South Wales), Barkindji Elder Uncle Badger Bates and NPWS archaeologist Dan Witter spotted dingo bones eroding from a road cut.  
- At the request of the Menindee Aboriginal Elders Council, a joint team of Barkindji elders and archaeologists excavated the skeleton.  
- Radiocarbon dating of the shell layers dates the original burial to 916–963 years ago.

## Physical Findings
- The skeleton belonged to an elderly male dingo (estimated 4–7 years old, senior for a wild dingo).  
- Worn teeth and shortened femurs suggest age and possible domestication effects.  
- Healed injuries: a broken rib and lower‑leg fracture likely from a kangaroo kick, plus signs of arthritis.  
- The injuries had healed, indicating the animal was cared for after injury.

## Cultural Findings
- The dingo was interred in a carefully constructed shell mound, treated with the same ceremony as human ancestors.  
- Generations of Barkindji added shells to the mound—a “feeding” ritual that continued for centuries, honoring the dingo as a community member.  
- The burial demonstrates that dingoes were “deeply valued and loved” and were incorporated into kinship structures, similar to humans.

## Significance
- Confirms that dingo‑related burial traditions were more widespread than previously recognized.  
- Provides rare archaeological evidence of long‑term, multi‑generational ritual care for a non‑human animal in Australia.  
- Offers insight into the early domestication process and the social role of dingoes in pre‑colonial Aboriginal societies.

## Modern Reburial
- Before excavation, Barkindji artist Barb Quayle performed a smoking ceremony to cleanse the site.  
- After study, the dingo’s remains were reburied on Barkindji land in a “return to Country” ceremony, aligning scientific research with Indigenous cultural protocols.  
- Researchers note that the findings reinforce Barkindji oral history about enduring relationships with animals, ancestors, and Country.
