# What is really happening to jobs? Separating AI hype from reality

## Key takeaways
- AI’s effect on overall employment appears modest at present, though it may be contributing to a tougher job market for recent graduates.  
- Impacts on worker productivity are mixed but tend toward the positive side.  
- Firm‑level adoption of AI has sped up, but the pace varies widely across industries.  
- Current evidence is preliminary; future research may revise these conclusions.

## AI’s impact on aggregate employment is likely small right now
- Unemployment has risen across all occupations, with the most AI‑exposed quintile up 0.77 pp and the least exposed up 0.85 pp since 2022, indicating a general softening rather than AI‑driven job loss.  
- Employment trends in high‑exposure occupations remain stable; coding‑heavy jobs still show positive growth.  
- Job postings for highly AI‑exposed roles (e.g., software developers) have increased faster than for other occupations.  
- Firms that adopted enterprise AI saw a 10 % employment rise over two years, driven by those with the highest per‑capita AI spending.  
- Layoffs attributed to AI often reflect broader cost‑cutting or cash‑flow motives; the primary AI effect is role consolidation and hiring avoidance in automatable tasks.

## A tough labor market for young workers may be partly due to AI
- Unemployment for recent graduates reached 5.6 % in early 2026, up 1.6 pp from three years earlier.  
- Studies (e.g., Brynjolfsson, Chandar, & Chen) document a decline in early‑career employment in AI‑exposed occupations (software developers, customer‑service reps) after ChatGPT’s 2022 launch, while older workers’ employment stayed stable or grew.  
- Some of the early decline may stem from other factors: higher interest rates, pandemic‑era over‑hiring, and the shift to remote work that reduces on‑the‑job training value for younger hires.  
- When controlling for these confounders, the notable drop in entry‑level employment appears around 2024, coinciding with stronger AI capabilities and adoption.

## AI’s impact on worker productivity is mixed but generally positive
- Early studies show productivity gains in tasks that can be automated or augmented by generative AI (e.g., drafting, coding assistance).  
- Gains are uneven: high‑skill, creative, or highly interpersonal work sees smaller or ambiguous effects.  
- Firms report higher output per worker after AI integration, especially where AI spending per employee is high.

## Firm adoption has accelerated but unevenly across the economy
- Large tech‑centric firms and those with substantial AI budgets adopt rapidly, seeing measurable employment growth.  
- Sectors with lower AI investment (e.g., traditional manufacturing, some service industries) lag behind, creating an uneven landscape.  
- Adoption speed is influenced by data availability, regulatory constraints, and workforce skill gaps.

## Early evidence is hardly the last word on AI’s impacts
- The current data cover only the first few years of widespread generative AI deployment.  
- Ongoing research will need to disentangle AI effects from concurrent macro‑economic shocks (monetary policy, remote‑work trends, etc.).  
- Policymakers should monitor emerging patterns, especially regarding entry‑level hiring and sectoral disparities, before drawing definitive conclusions.
