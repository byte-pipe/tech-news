## Storytelling: how to stop being a vanilla brand (and forgettable)

Let’s start with a simple definition:

What is storytelling?

Storytelling in B2B marketing isthe use of a narrative to communicate a brand’s message, values, and offerings.Instead of just listing facts or product features, brands weave these details into a story, making the brand much easier to remember.

### What is a narrative in B2B?

Let’s answer this question with 2 angles

1. What a narrative is NOT:

In B2B, storytelling doesn't necessarily mean deeply emotional and amazing experiences. Instead, it's about creating a logical thread that connects your origin, your solution, and your customer's problem into one coherent story.

1. What a narrative IS:

A narrative is the overarching story that explains why your brand exists, what you're fighting against, and how you help your customers win. It's the "red thread" that connects everything.

### Why do you need a narrative?

Without a narrative, your audience will forget your features, IP, capabilities, and processesbecause they lack context.

A narrative helps the audience to:

* Understand the context of your product and why it matters
* Remember what problem you solve and with what solution
* Position your brand among the competition (you stand out)
* Create enthusiasm around the solution with a aha! moment

## 5 Storytelling frameworks to help you now

The frameworks I’m sharing here can be applied to a personal brand or a company brand. That said, it’s always easier to use the founder to personify the story especially in early stage.

### 1. The origin story with the Freytag’s pyramid

The Freytag’s pyramid will help you to pitch better byframing your business like a compelling story; showing the problem, the disruption, the journey, and the resolution.

#### The Freytag’s pyramid structures your narrative around key phases:

1. Exposition:What was the market like, and what did everyone accept as normal?
2. Rising action:What problem kept getting worse, and why did the existing solutions keep failing?
3. Climax:What is the product, and what insight made it the answer nobody had built?
4. Falling action:What proof shows it works: results, customers, adoption?
5. Denouement:What is the new standard the brand defends in the market today?

#### Inside the Freytag’s pyramid, you build your “origin story” and your “solution in context”

The origin storycovers stages 1 and 2 of the pyramid: exposition and rising action. It explains the world before your product and the problem that made it necessary.

→ It answers: what was the market like, what did everyone accept as normal.

The solution in contextcovers stages 3 to 5: climax, falling action, and denouement. It presents your product as the logical answer to the tension built before.

→ It answers: what insight led to the product, what proof shows it works, and what new standard the brand defends today.

### 2. The Pixar’s story framework (and the importance of causality)

When writing your origin story, solution in context, and overall narrative, you can use the Pixar’s story framework to connect the dots:

In this framework, you will notice one key element: the 'because of that' structure. It matters because it creates causality.

Causality in storytellingis the principle that every event happens because of a previous event, and leads to the next one.

And causality is crucial because it does 4 things for your story:

1. It creates momentum.Each event pushes the next one forward, so the story pulls the reader instead of the reader pushing through it.
2. It creates stakes.If actions have consequences, then choices matter. A character who acts without consequences is just decoration.
3. It creates meaning.The reader learns something about how the world works: “because he ignored the warning, he lost everything.” Remove the “because” and the lesson disappears.
4. It creates satisfaction.Endings feel good when they are the logical result of everything before them. Endings feel worthless when they arrive by coincidence.

### 4. Your big idea or point of view

Your solution should be more than a product, it should rely on an overarching idea that answers why it matters, how it helps, and what problem it solves.

A"big idea" in marketingisthe central, creative message that powers a brand or advertising campaign.

This concept is widely known in the infoproduct sphere where you need to attach the course, coaching, or cohort to a central idea.

#### Your big idea should have 3 components:

1. Intellectual Hook:The angle that reframes how the buyer sees their problem. It sounds like a fresh insight, not a pitch.
2. Primary Promise:The business outcome the buyer gets, stated in their language.
3. Unique Method:The specific mechanism that makes the promise credible. This is your process, technology, or approach that competitors do not have.

The 4 steps to find it:

1. Know Your Audience:Decide exactly who you are talking to.
2. Find the Tension:What is the biggest problem or frustration your customer has?
3. Name the Problem or Idea:Give the tension a memorable label the market can repeat.
4. Connect Your Solution:State clearly how your solution solves that problem in a way no one else is doing.

### 5. Sub stories to anchor the narrative into real life

A narrative is not only about the big picture. We’ll believe it if it also comes from little anecdotes, metaphors, or cases that make it sound real and concrete in our day-to-day.

#### Sub-stories happen during the entire journey

You could tell sub stories on:

* Analogy that simplifies the concept
* Credibility story that proves it in the wild
* Counter-intuitive lesson that dissolves a common objection
* Mini case that shows a concrete result

### Bonus framework: South Park Storytelling Pattern

In 2011 during a guest talk they gave to a screenwriting class at NYU's Tisch School of the Arts, Trey Parker and Matt Stone explained their storytelling rules. A short video clip of that session circulated widely online, and that clip is what made the "But / Therefore" rule famous.

Matt Stone and Trey Parker at The Amazing Meeting on January 20, 2007 (Creative Commons) - With added quote

Here’s the explanation of the “But / Therefore” rule:

Look at the beats of your story:If you can connect them with “and then”, your story is boring. Because “And then” is a list of events with no causality. This happens, and then this happens, and then this happens.

Instead, to build tension and momentum,each beat should be connected by either “but” or “therefore”. Each “but” creates an open loop the reader wants to close. Each “therefore” rewards them with a logical payoff. The story pulls itself forward instead of just listing facts.
