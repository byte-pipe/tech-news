I've learned that writing is much more enjoyable when you stop chasing numbers, that some of the best conversations begin in the comments, and that finding your voice takes time. More than anything, I've learned that the topics you're most connected to often become the ones only you can write.

## Why I Wanted to Write This

When I sat down to write this article, I realized it had been almost three months since I started writing consistently on DEV. During that time, I've published 25 articles, and this one makes 26. It's honestly hard to believe how much has happened in such a short time.

I was fortunate enough to have two articles featured in the Top 7, start a series called Dev Opportunity Radar, and eventually launch a website for it. Those are milestones I'm incredibly grateful for, but when I think about these past three months, they aren't the first things that come to mind.

What I remember most are the people.The conversations in the comments, the encouragement when I doubted myself, the community members who shared opportunities that became part of Dev Opportunity Radar, and the readers who reached out to tell me an article helped them or introduced them to something they might have otherwise missed. Those moments have meant more to me than any number ever could.

I don't consider myself an expert. Three months is only a small part of what I hope will be a much longer journey. But these past few months have taught me lessons I wish someone had shared with me when I first started writing. I hope they help someone who's just beginning their own.

## From Silent Reader to Writer

When I first joined DEV, I spent much more time reading than writing. I'd browse articles, learn from discussions, and admire the people who confidently shared their experiences and ideas. At the same time, I wasn't sure if I had anything meaningful to contribute myself, so for a long time I stayed a quiet reader.

I don't think there's anything wrong with that. Every community has people who learn by listening before they feel ready to join the conversation, and I was definitely one of them. Eventually, though, I started leaving comments. Then I shared a few thoughts of my own. Little by little, writing didn't feel quite as intimidating anymore.

English isn't my first language, and even today I still reread my articles wondering if I've explained something clearly enough or whether I've made a mistake. That self-doubt hasn't completely disappeared, and honestly, I don't know if it ever will. What's changed is that I've become a little more comfortable pressing Publish anyway, trusting that every article is another chance to learn and improve.

If I'd waited until I felt completely confident, I probably would have never written that first article at all.

## Lesson 1: Write Because You Enjoy It

If there's one lesson that stands out above all the others, it's this:write because you genuinely enjoy writing.

When I first started, my mood on a given day often depended on how an article was doing. I'd refresh and check my views, reactions, and comments more than I probably should have. I think that's completely normal, especially when you're putting something you've worked hard on out into the world. We all hope people will read it and connect with it.

Over time, though, my perspective started to change. I realized that if I only felt happy when an article performed well, I was giving too much control over my enjoyment to something I couldn't control. Views are wonderful. Reactions are wonderful. Being featured is wonderful. I'm incredibly grateful for every single one of those things, but they've become a bonus rather than the reason I write.

Today, when an idea pops into my head, the first question I ask myself isn't, "Will this get a lot of views?" It's, "Is this something I'm excited to write about?" If the answer is yes, I usually end up enjoying the entire process, from researching and writing to reading the conversations that follow.

The articles I'm proudest of aren't always the ones that reached the most people.They're the ones I enjoyed creating, because those are the ones that feel the most like me.

## Lesson 2: Comment More Than You Think You Should

If I could only give one piece of advice to someone who's new to DEV, it would be this:don't just write. Read. Comment. Join the conversation.

When I first started becoming more active here, I thought publishing articles would be the biggest part of my journey. Looking back, I don't think that was true. Some of my favorite memories didn't begin when I clicked Publish. They began in the comments.

Over the past three months, I've discovered incredible writers through comment sections, had conversations that made me think differently, and met people I never would have known otherwise. Some of those conversations even inspired future articles, while others introduced me to opportunities, ideas, and perspectives I probably wouldn't have found on my own.

Commenting has also changed the way I read. Instead of quickly scrolling to the next article, I spend more time thinking about what the author is trying to say. If something resonates with me, I try to tell them why. If I learn something new, I let them know. Sometimes it's a simple thank you, and other times it turns into a conversation that continues long after the article itself.

It's easy to think of DEV as a place where people publish articles, but to me it's much more than that. It's a community where people encourage one another, share ideas, celebrate wins, and learn together.

If you're just getting started, don't worry about writing the perfect article on day one. Start by reading what others are sharing. Leave thoughtful comments. Ask questions. Celebrate someone's achievement. Share your perspective when you have something to add. You never know where a single conversation might lead.

Writing brought me to DEV, but the conversations are what made me feel like I belonged here.

## Lesson 3: Don't Let Numbers Define Your Journey

Every notification used to feel exciting, and honestly, every time an article didn't perform the way I'd hoped, it was hard not to wonder what I could have done differently. Looking back, I was checking my stats way more than they deserved. I think that's something many of us experience when we start sharing our work publicly.

Over time, though, I realized I was comparing my beginning to someone else's journey. Some writers have been part of the community for years. Some have built an audience over a long period of time. Others have found a niche they're known for. It's not really a fair comparison because everyone's path looks different.

I also realized that numbers don't tell the whole story. An article with fewer views might spark an amazing discussion. A single comment from someone saying your article helped them can mean more than hundreds of extra reactions. Some of my favorite articles haven't necessarily been my most popular ones, and that's completely okay.

These days, I still look at my article stats because I think it's natural to be curious, but they no longer decide whether I consider an article a success. What matters more is whether I enjoyed writing it, whether I learned something through the process, and whether it started a meaningful conversation.

One example of this for me is Dev Opportunity Radar. When I first started the series, I'll admit I hoped it would reach more people than it has so far. There are weeks where I wish it had more views because I believe these opportunities could help more developers. Maybe there are things I can improve, and I'm always trying to learn.

But I've realized something more important:I can't let those numbers decide whether I keep showing up every Friday.

I started Dev Opportunity Radar because I kept discovering amazing fellowships, grants, hackathons, and resources after it was already too late to apply. I didn't want other people to miss those opportunities for the same reason. That purpose hasn't changed just because a particular edition gets fewer views than I hoped.

What's interesting is that the numbers only tell part of the story. If you only looked at the view count, you might think the series isn't doing particularly well. But when I read the comments, it's a completely different picture. People share opportunities I would have never found on my own. They celebrate getting accepted into programs. They suggest improvements, contribute Community Finds, and tell me an edition helped them discover something they would have otherwise missed.

That reminds me that impact isn't always measured by views or reactions. Sometimes it's measured by the conversations you start and the people you help, even when those numbers aren't immediately visible.

Dev Opportunity Radar has changed me more than I expected.

It gave me a reason to show up every Friday and helped me find a place in the community that feels uniquely my own.

Even if only a handful of people read each edition, that's still a handful of people I have the chance to help, and to me, that's reason enough to keep going.

## Lesson 4: Your Voice Will Change

One of the biggest misconceptions I had when I started writing was that I needed to figure out my writing style before I could become a consistent writer. I don't think that's how it works at all.

I think you discover your voice by writing. Every article teaches you something new about how you think, what you enjoy writing about, and how you connect with readers. As you keep writing, your confidence grows, your ideas become clearer, and you slowly begin to understand what feels authentic to you.

When I look back at some of my earlier articles, I can already see changes in the way I write. Some things I'd explain differently today, and there are ideas I'd probably approach from a different angle. I don't see that as a bad thing. If anything, it's a sign that I'm learning.

Even today, I don't think I've found my final writing voice, and honestly, I hope I never fully do.I hope it continues to evolve as I learn new things, meet new people, and gain new experiences.To me, that's one of the most exciting parts of writing. Every article becomes another small step in that journey.

So if you're just starting out,don't put too much pressure on yourself to find your "voice" before you begin.Start writing, keep showing up, and give yourself permission to grow. You might be surprised by how much your writing changes a few months from now.

## Lesson 5: Write What You Can Relate To

The articles I've enjoyed writing the most all have one thing in common. They came from experiences I could genuinely relate to.

Dev Opportunity Radar is the clearest example. As I mentioned earlier, it grew out of a frustration I'd lived through myself: missing opportunities simply because I found out about them too late. The opportunity itself was rarely the problem. Access to information was.

That experience made me wonder how many other people were missing opportunities for the exact same reason. So I decided to start sharing the fellowships, grants, hackathons, and resources I came across each week, hoping someone else would discover them while there was still time to apply.

It taught me something I keep coming back to. The articles people connect with the most aren't always about the most popular topics. They're often the ones that come from experiences only you have lived, because those experiences give you a perspective nobody else can write in quite the same way.

If I could give one more piece of advice, it would be this:don't spend too much time trying to figure out what everyone else wants you to write.Instead, think about the experiences you've had, the challenges you've faced, the things you've learned, or even the problems you've wished someone had solved for you. Sometimes the best ideas come from asking yourself, "What do I wish I had known a year ago?"

That's still the reason I enjoy putting Dev Opportunity Radar together every Friday.The purpose has always been bigger than the numbers.

## Lesson 6: Community Makes Ideas Better

One of the biggest surprises over the past three months is realizing that writing isn't really a solo activity. Every article may begin with one person behind a keyboard, but it rarely ends there.

Every time I press Publish, the article becomes something more because of the people who take the time to read it. Their comments make me think differently. Their questions inspire future articles. Sometimes they share their own experiences, and I end up learning just as much from them as they do from me. That's one of my favorite parts of writing on DEV: every article becomes the beginning of a conversation rather than the end of one.

Dev Opportunity Radar is probably the best example of this. I may be the one putting each edition together, but the series has never really felt like something I build alone. Community Finds was there from the very first edition because I wanted it to be a space where everyone could share opportunities, not just me. Since then, people have continued contributing opportunities I would've otherwise missed, Reader Updates have given people a chance to celebrate their own milestones, and the series has even grown into its own website. It no longer feels like just my series.

It's become something the community helps build every week, and much bigger than I could have built on my own.

Being part of that has changed me in ways I never expected. It's introduced me to incredible people and given me a place in this community that feels uniquely my own.

More than anything else, these past three months have reminded me thatcommunities grow because people choose to contribute in their own way.Some write articles. Some leave thoughtful comments. Some answer questions, share opportunities, or simply encourage someone who's just getting started. Every one of those contributions matters, and together they create the kind of community people want to come back to.

## Lesson 7: It's Okay to Take a Break

One thing I've learned over the past three months is that it's okay to take a step back every now and then.

Last week was actually the first week in a while where I didn't publish a new article, apart from Dev Opportunity Radar. It wasn't because I had run out of ideas or because I didn't want to write.

I simply didn't have a topic that I genuinely felt excited about, and I didn't want to force one just for the sake of staying consistent.

If you're just getting started, I do think it helps to have a gentle goal. Maybe that's one article a week or even one every couple of weeks. Having a small target can help you build a routine without making writing feel like another obligation.

That said, I don't think every goal has to be followed perfectly. I usually aim to publish one article a week (apart from Dev Opportunity Radar), but if I don't have an idea that genuinely excites me, I'd rather wait than force something just to stay on schedule. In my experience, the more you write, read, and interact with the community, the more ideas naturally start to appear.

I think there's a lot of pressure online to always be creating, always publishing, and always staying visible. For me, writing has always been something I look forward to, and I'd like to keep it that way.

At the end of the day, we're all human. The hobbies, passions, and projects we care about shouldn't become another source of burnout. If taking a short break means coming back with more excitement and fresh ideas,that's a much healthier approach than forcing yourself to create something your heart isn't really in.

## Final Thoughts

Three months ago, I simply wanted to become a better writer.I realize now I gained much more than that.

I became a little more confident every time I pressed Publish, discovered topics I genuinely enjoy writing about, found a community that constantly inspires me, and watched a small idea born from one of my own frustrations grow into something much bigger than I ever imagined.

If there's one thing these past three months have taught me, it's that writing is about so much more than publishing articles. It's about sharing experiences, learning from one another, and becoming part of a community where people genuinely want to see each other succeed. Some of the best parts of this journey didn't begin when I clicked Publish. They began when someone replied, shared their perspective, or simply started a conversation.

I don't know what the next three months will look like, and I hope they surprise me just as much as the last three have. I'm sure my writing will continue to evolve, my interests will change, and hopefully I'll keep finding new ways to contribute to this amazing community.

For now, I'm simply grateful that I decided to press Publish on that first article.
