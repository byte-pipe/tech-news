# Alberta voters weigh future in Canada

## Background
- Alberta holds its first non‑binding vote on whether to start a legal process toward a future independence referendum.  
- Elections Alberta calls it the province’s largest electoral event.  
- Polls suggest roughly 20 % of Albertans support separation, but most are expected to reject the step.  
- The issue revives long‑standing western alienation dating back to the Trudeau eras of the 1970s‑80s.

## Ballot questions
- Ten questions appear on the ballot.  
- Questions 1‑9 address immigration, provincial services (health care, education, social programs) and possible constitutional changes; voters answer “yes” or “no.”  
- Question 10 offers two choices:  
  1. Remain part of Canada.  
  2. Begin the legal process toward a separation referendum.

## Campaign voices
- **Cory Morgan** – founder of the Alberta Independence Party and Pathway to Independence; warns that dissatisfaction will keep growing if change is not achieved.  
- **Premier Danielle Smith** – argues a vote is needed to gauge Albertans’ will and promises to respect the result.  
- **Prime Minister Mark Carney** – campaigned in Edmonton urging a “no” vote, calling a separation vote a “dangerous bluff.”  
- **Jeremy Clive**, 23‑year‑old pilot, expresses desire to keep provincial revenues from flowing to Ottawa.  
- **Thomas Lukaszak**, former deputy premier and pro‑unity activist, cites Quebec’s 1995 experience as a cautionary economic example.

## Legal and constitutional context
- Secession would require negotiations with the other nine provinces and the federal government to amend the Constitution, as outlined by the Supreme Court.  
- Canada’s Clarity Act (2000) mandates a clear referendum question and result before negotiations can begin; no fixed majority percentage is defined.  
- First Nations’ treaty rights add another layer: a May court ruling found a separatist petition violated Indigenous rights for lacking proper consultation; the province is appealing.

## Economic stakes
- Alberta is Canada’s wealthiest province, home to the world’s fourth‑largest oil reserves and a major contributor to the federal equalization system.  
- University of Calgary analysis estimates separation could cost CAD 170 billion over five years, with total debt implications ranging from CAD 324 billion to CAD 442.3 billion.  
- An independent Alberta would assume a share of Canada’s federal debt and face higher interest payments.  
- Pro‑unity advocates warn of capital flight and loss of industry, drawing parallels to post‑1995 Quebec.

## Potential outcomes if “yes” wins
- Premier Smith says she would honor the vote and initiate steps toward a legally binding referendum, though the process would be gradual.  
- The province would need to renegotiate treaties with First Nations, whose groups have already objected to independence.  
- Constitutional amendment negotiations with all provinces and the federal government would be required before any separation could occur.
