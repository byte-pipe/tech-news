# The Fire Retardant in the Seafood Chain – Inside Climate News  

## Historical context of fishmeal fires  
- In the mid‑1960s three separate ship fires (South African vessel, Liberian steamship, Danish ship) were traced to fishmeal, a powder that can spontaneously combust as it decays.  
- Between the 1950s and 1970s at least 90 ship and plant fires were linked to untreated fishmeal, prompting concern among seafarers, insurers and dock workers.  

## Introduction of ethoxyquin as a fire retardant  
- 1967: Cargill announced the use of ethoxyquin, an antioxidant previously used for rubber tires and as a preservative on fruits and alfalfa.  
- Industry officials promoted mandatory treatment of fishmeal with ethoxyquin to prevent spontaneous combustion.  

## Effectiveness and regulatory adoption  
- Fires involving fishmeal cargoes dropped sharply in the 1970s (from ~16 per year to 5).  
- 1976: U.S. Coast Guard required ethoxyquin treatment on fishmeal shipments.  
- 1979: United Nations maritime body recommended ethoxyquin treatment for fire prevention.  

## Health and environmental concerns  
- Early studies (1969, U.N. FAO) noted toxic effects in laboratory animals at high concentrations.  
- 1987 British research identified severe damage and potential carcinogenic effects in rats.  
- 2015 EFSA highlighted an impurity, p‑phenetidine, capable of causing genetic mutations.  

## Regulatory landscape worldwide  
- 2017: European Commission suspended ethoxyquin use in animal feed across the EU; the UK and EEA members (Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein) have banned it.  
- The chemical remains permitted, under limits, in Australia, Japan, South Korea and the United States.  

## Legal actions and industry response  
- Multiple U.S. lawsuits (2022) alleged false “sustainable” labeling for products containing ethoxyquin; cases against Aldi and Gorton’s were settled out of court.  
- Canadian Food Inspection Agency requires labeling of feeds with ethoxyquin but does not test seafood for residues; a 2024 EU audit found only ~40 % of planned inspections were completed.  
- Cargill states it stopped using ethoxyquin in global salmon feed where it is not authorized and has explored alternative antioxidants since 2005.  

## Shift toward alternative additives  
- Producers are moving to natural antioxidants such as rosemary extract and vitamin E compounds.  
- Peruvian fishmeal exporter MSICeres reports 100 % of its exports to Europe, Canada, the U.S. and Australia are now ethoxyquin‑free; similar trends are observed in the U.K. and Norway.  

## Implications for the global seafood supply  
- With over half of worldwide seafood now farmed and fed fishmeal/fish oil, understanding feed additives like ethoxyquin is essential for assessing potential contamination of the food chain.
