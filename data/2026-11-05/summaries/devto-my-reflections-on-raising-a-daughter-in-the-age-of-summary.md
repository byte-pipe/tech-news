# My reflections on raising a daughter in the age of AI

## Why the question matters
- I wonder what skills my daughter should develop when AI can supply answers.
- Using AI as a developer is attractive, but for a child the learning process itself is valuable.
- A finished assignment and an acquired skill are not the same thing.

## Evidence from research
- A 2025 study (Bastani et al.) in a Turkish high school showed that students who used a basic GPT‑4 assistant improved during assisted practice but performed worse on unaided tests.
- A version of the assistant with learning safeguards (hints, teacher input) did not show the same decline.
- The study measured short‑term outcomes in one setting; it does not answer how every child should use AI, but it highlights the importance of what happens after the assistant is closed.

## When to introduce AI
- UNESCO (2023) recommends a minimum age of 13 for independent conversations with generative‑AI platforms, but age alone is not sufficient.
- I would consider:
  - The child’s goals and interests.
  - Their awareness that AI can be wrong.
  - Their ability to know when to ask a human for help.
  - Privacy considerations about the information they share.

## Practicing critical checking
- “Think critically” is vague; I propose a concrete sequence:
  1. Pick a specific claim from the AI’s answer.
  2. Locate the source cited (or search for one).
  3. Verify that the source actually supports the claim.
  4. Look for independent confirmation when the claim matters.
- Using familiar topics (e.g., a movie the child knows) provides a safe starting point.
- For unfamiliar topics, the same four‑step routine applies, possibly with a second chatbot to suggest alternative explanations or sources.
- Research on “lateral reading” shows that teaching students to leave an unfamiliar site and investigate elsewhere improves digital‑content judgment; this can be adapted to chatbot answers.

## Learning through dialogue
- Discussing the process (“What were you trying to do? Which suggestion helped? What would you change?”) makes the AI a tool rather than a shortcut.
- A rhythm I’m testing: first attempt unaided, then limited help, then a second attempt without the assistant.
- Recognize when AI enables an activity that would otherwise be impossible (e.g., simplifying vocabulary, providing a hint at the right moment).

## Supporting creativity
- When she invents a story, I want space for her own plot twists and surprising endings.
- AI can be used to develop ideas while keeping her decisions visible.
- Books remain essential: reading allows disagreement with an author, rereading passages, abandoning a text, and discussing it—all valuable experiences.

## Balancing screen‑free activities
- Parents in online discussions prioritize hiking, gardening, fishing, and other offline pursuits.
- I aim to keep technology fitting into a life that also includes activities with their own natural pace (planting a seed, walking and making up a story together).

## Modeling the behavior I expect
- I must show her how I check claims, admit uncertainty, and change my mind when evidence changes.
- A comment from r/daddit highlighted a child demanding a more authoritative source than an AI summary; I would welcome such challenges from my daughter.

## Ongoing question
- I will keep asking: after using AI, what does she understand, choose, and want to explore?
- My answer will evolve as she grows and as we discover what works for our family.
