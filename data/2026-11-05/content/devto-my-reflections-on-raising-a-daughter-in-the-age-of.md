Real-world math data and parent comments add depth

I'm raising a daughter, and I keep coming back to a question: what do I want her to learn to do for herself when a machine can produce an answer for her?

I use AI. I understand the appeal of asking for an explanation, a draft, or help getting past a problem. As a developer, I can see plenty of reasons to welcome tools that make difficult work more approachable.

Then I imagine a conversation I may eventually have with my daughter: “If you ask AI for help, why do I have to do this myself?”

I think that question deserves a better answer than “because you're a child.”

For a developer trying to deliver a working feature, saving effort can be useful. For a child learning to write, explain an idea, or solve a problem, some of that effort is the activity we care about. A completed assignment and an acquired skill are two different things.

That distinction gives me somewhere to start. It also leaves me with a lot of questions.

A2025 study by Bastani and colleaguesmakes this tension concrete. In a mathematics experiment at one high school in Turkey, students using a basic GPT-4 assistant did better during assisted practice, then performed worse than the control group when they had to work without it. A version designed with learning safeguards, including hints and teacher-provided information, did not show the same statistically significant decline.

The experiment took place in 2023 and measured short-term outcomes in a specific setting. It cannot settle the question of how every child should use every AI tool. What I take from it is a reason to ask what happens after the assistant is closed.

Could my daughter explain the idea in her own words? Could she try a similar problem? Could she tell me which decisions were hers?

I would find those answers more useful than the quality of the finished document.

The question of when to introduce AI needs more precision, too. Watching a parent demonstrate a tool, exploring it together, and having independent access are different experiences.

UNESCO's 2023 guidanceproposes thirteen as the minimum age for independent conversations with generative AI platforms. I would treat that as a policy recommendation, alongside the rules of the particular service. A birthday alone cannot tell me whether my daughter is ready for a particular kind of access.

I would also want to understand what she wants to do, whether she knows that answers can be wrong, and whether she knows when to ask a person for help. We would need to talk about the information she shares, including information about other people.

The question I'm trying to answer is what a gradual introduction could look like, and how I would recognize that it was helping.

One thing I would like to practice together is checking a confident answer.

“Think critically” sounds sensible, but it leaves a child with a lot to figure out. What does checking actually involve? Which part of an answer is a factual claim? Where should you look? How do you know that the page you found supports it?

In adiscussion on r/Parenting, a parent described looking at answers about a film their child already knew well. The answers mixed up actors and included incorrect details about the characters. Familiarity with the subject gave them something concrete to examine together.

That seems like a useful place to begin. A child can notice that a polished answer conflicts with something they know. The next step is harder: checking an answer about an unfamiliar subject.

For that, I would practice a small sequence:

* Pick a specific claim.
* Find where it came from.
* Open the source and see whether it actually supports the claim.
* Look for independent confirmation when the claim matters.

Asking another chatbot could suggest a different explanation or help locate a source. Two agreeing answers still wouldn't be enough to settle the question for me.

There is relevant research beyond AI. Aschool study of lateral readingfound that teaching students to leave an unfamiliar website and investigate it elsewhere improved their ability to judge digital content. The study concerned online information evaluation, rather than chatbot answers specifically, but it offers a practical direction to explore.

I would also want to apply that habit to answers I find reassuring or agree with. If I only investigate things I dislike, I am teaching my daughter something about defending a position.

The Reddit stories are useful here as examples of what families describe trying. They are personal accounts, with no way for me to establish how well they work or how typical they are.

In anotherdiscussion on r/daddit, a parent described a child beginning to use AI at school and then using it more frequently. The family started discussing it at home and reviewing questions and answers together. Those conversations brought up both factual errors and the use of AI for creative tasks.

The part I would take from that story is making room to discuss the process. What were you trying to do? Which suggestion helped? Which part did you change? What would you try differently?

For a task intended to teach a skill, I would like to experiment with a rhythm: a first attempt, some help, then another attempt with the assistant closed. That is a starting idea I would expect to adjust to the task and to my daughter.

Help can also make an activity possible in the first place. An explanation in simpler language, support with unfamiliar vocabulary, or a hint at the right moment might open a door. I would want to notice those benefits as well.

The same question comes up with creativity. If she wants to invent a story, I want there to be room for her own strange plot, uncertain sentences, and ending that surprises me. I would happily explore how AI could help her develop an idea while keeping her decisions visible.

This is also why books remain part of what I hope for her.

A summary can tell you what happens. Reading a book gives you time with the language, the characters, and your own reactions. I want her to have the experience of disagreeing with an author, rereading a passage, abandoning a book she dislikes, and finding one she wants to talk about.

That is an experience I value in its own right, and one I want her to have the chance to enjoy.

In athread about children growing up with AI, some parents described prioritizing, or planning to prioritize, hiking, gardening, fishing, and other activities away from screens.

Those comments reminded me to leave space for experiences that have their own pace. You can talk about a seed growing, but you can also plant one and wait. You can ask for a story, and you can make one up together while walking.

I want technology to fit into a life that has room for those things.

The most uncomfortable part of all this is my own example.

If I expect my daughter to check a claim, she needs opportunities to see me checking one. If I want her to admit uncertainty, I need to be able to say “I don't know.” If I hope she will change her mind when the evidence changes, I need to make that look ordinary.

One comment in thesame r/daddit discussion about AI usestayed with me: a parent described their child challenging their reliance on an AI search summary and insisting on a more authoritative source.

I would be glad if my daughter did that to me. I also hope I would remember to be glad in the moment.

For now, the question I want to keep asking is what she understands, chooses, and wants to explore after using the tool. I expect my answer to change as she grows and as we learn what works for us.

If you're already navigating this with your children, what has happened in practice?Was there a moment that made you change a rule, relax one, or introduce a boundary you hadn't expected to need?
