# Writing as a thinking tool

## Personal background
- In middle school I struggled with long texts and began taking extensive notes, using Tony Buzan’s mind‑maps.
- The notes served more as a processing tool than a storage device, helping me make sense of what I read.

## Why writing amplifies thinking
- Manuel Blum’s analogy: a finite automaton (no paper) vs. a Turing machine (with paper and pencil).
- Without writing we are limited to finite‑automaton capabilities; with writing we gain the full power of a Turing machine.
- Language externalizes thought, writing externalizes even more, driving human intellectual progress.
- Large language models inherit much of this externalized intelligence because they are trained on our written language.

## Out‑of‑core execution (external memory)
- Writing externalizes thinking, similar to out‑of‑core algorithms that use external memory.
- Example: drafting a paper review reveals gaps in understanding that are filled during writing.
- Freewriting can uncover connections not initially apparent, as when a 2018 post on Paxos vs. Nakamoto consensus seeded a research paper.
- Blog posts become personal external memory; rereading reinforces the neural pathways.

## Writing in the age of LLMs
- LLMs can generate text without the writer’s own thinking, but they lack access to the writer’s private intuition.
- Reliance on LLMs may bypass the thinking process; the “six‑page memo” tradition loses its link to clear thinking when auto‑generated.
- Writing remains a comparative advantage because it forces the writer to think, whereas a model merely predicts tokens.

## Rule 1: Mess up
- Produce large amounts of raw text without self‑criticism or concern for polish.
- The initial draft provides material for later reflection and improvement.
- Treat notes like a lab notebook, protecting ideas from early judgment.

## Rule 2: Tidy up
- Switch to the role of editor: critique, cut, and reorganize ruthlessly.
- Move discarded sentences to a junk buffer; most will not return.
- Multiple drafts reduce pressure for brilliance on the first pass.

## Rule 3: Keep it simple, plain, and succinct
- Embrace the style of Strunk & White and Vonneget’s plain prose.
- Complexity often masks weak thinking; clarity indicates thorough thought.

## Rule 4: Write without expectations
- Write primarily for yourself; external audience is secondary.
- Intrinsic motivation sustains the practice and preserves the thinking benefit.
