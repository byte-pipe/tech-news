# Scientists found that the creatine supplement millions take for muscle gains is quietly raising brain energy levels and slowing early Alzheimer’s cognitive decline by 30%

## Overview
- Creatine, widely used for muscle performance, crosses the blood‑brain barrier and elevates neuronal phosphocreatine.
- Recent reviews and trials (2024‑2026) reveal cognitive benefits in healthy adults and a ~30 % slowdown of decline in early Alzheimer’s disease (AD).
- These effects are not reflected in typical fitness marketing.

## Why the Brain Needs Creatine
- The brain consumes ~20 % of total body energy despite being only 2 % of body mass.
- Neurons rely on continuous ATP production; they lack substantial energy reserves.
- Creatine is converted to phosphocreatine, which rapidly regenerates ATP via creatine kinase, acting as an emergency energy buffer during high‑demand neuronal activity.

## Age‑Related Changes in Brain Creatine
- AD patients show significantly lower brain phosphocreatine and reduced creatine‑kinase activity compared with age‑matched controls.
- Mitochondrial dysfunction in AD creates a “bioenergetic crisis,” limiting ATP production.
- The phosphocreatine system can partially compensate for mitochondrial deficits, making creatine supplementation a logical therapeutic target.

## Clinical Trials Demonstrating Efficacy
- **CABA trial (University of Kansas, 2026)**: 20 mg creatine monohydrate daily for 8 weeks in 20 AD patients improved sorting, reading, and attention scores; MRS confirmed increased brain phosphocreatine.
- **Multicenter placebo‑controlled trial (2026)**: 240 early‑AD participants took 5 g/day for 12 weeks; brain phosphocreatine rose 10‑15 %, short‑term memory improved, and overall cognitive decline slowed by ~30 % versus placebo.
- Cost of supplementation is minimal, yet the impact on disease progression is substantial.

## Effects on Healthy Brains
- Systematic review (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2024) found creatine enhances processing speed and accuracy, especially in tasks requiring rapid information handling.
- Under metabolic stress (e.g., sleep deprivation), a single creatine dose improves cognitive performance and raises cerebral high‑energy phosphates (Scientific Reports).
- As an adjunct to cognitive‑behavioral therapy, 5 g/day creatine reduced depressive symptoms in a 2025 study, linking mood improvement to restored brain energy metabolism.

## Implications
- Creatine’s ability to boost neuronal energy reserves offers a low‑cost, widely accessible strategy for neuroprotection and cognitive enhancement.
- The 30 % slowing of AD progression suggests a need for broader clinical adoption and public awareness beyond the fitness community.
- Future research should explore optimal dosing, long‑term safety, and synergistic effects with other neuro‑therapeutics.
