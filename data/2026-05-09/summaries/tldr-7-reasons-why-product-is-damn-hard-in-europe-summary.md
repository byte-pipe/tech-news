# 7 Reasons Why Product Is Damn Hard In Europe

## Introduction
- Author recounts impulsive move from São Paulo to Germany to become a Product Manager, highlighting cultural shock and the long‑term impact of that decision.  
- The piece is a personal, opinion‑driven newsletter based on 8 years of work in Europe, consulting for 50+ companies, and a decade in South America.  
- Central claim: European product teams face systemic obstacles that make success unusually difficult, despite the presence of notable successes (e.g., GetYourGuide, Spotify, Booking.com, Revolut).

## 1. The Roadmap Theater
- Companies create exhaustive, time‑boxed roadmaps (quarterly or yearly) and mistakenly treat them as the de‑facto strategy.  
- Consequences: teams feel forced to deliver on every listed feature, lose sight of overarching objectives, and cannot question whether work aligns with the real product vision.  
- Recommended focus areas for a genuine strategy:  
  - Desired outcome (objective)  
  - Target audience (who we serve and who we don’t)  
  - Differentiation (value proposition)  
  - Success metrics (how we measure results)  
- Teams should be empowered to challenge requests by asking how each item serves the audience, differentiates the product, and contributes to success.  
- **Lesson:** Ask the hard, often‑ignored questions instead of hiding behind a detailed roadmap.

## 2. PM Means Project Manager, Not Product Manager
- In many European firms, “PM” is interpreted as Project Manager: responsibilities revolve around scope, tasks, status reports, and strict timelines.  
- This focus leaves little room for customer interaction, strategic thinking, or decisive product choices.  
- Project‑centric language (scope, resources, timelines) replaces product‑centric language (investment, initiatives, bets).  
- Product work should emphasize:  
  - Evaluating the worth of an initiative rather than committing to a fixed schedule  
  - Treating work as experiments that can be dropped if they prove wrong  
- **Lesson:** Shift the vocabulary and mindset from project management to product management to regain influence and focus on value creation.

## 3. Clarity Is A Prison
- The author’s experience as a CEO illustrates how demanding precise, long‑term roadmaps can trap leaders in unrealistic expectations.  
- Investors often request detailed forecasts, but over‑promising creates a “prison” of expectations that can lead to frustration and loss of credibility when plans inevitably change.  
- Instead, framing the conversation around market opportunity, confidence in capturing a share, and the need for discovery can keep expectations realistic while still securing investment.  
- **Lesson:** Resist the pressure for absolute clarity; embrace uncertainty as a space for learning and adaptation.

## Takeaways
- European product environments are heavily influenced by cultural attitudes toward predictability, planning, and terminology.  
- Over‑reliance on rigid roadmaps, conflating product and project roles, and demanding premature clarity all hinder agile, value‑driven product development.  
- To improve, organizations should:  
  - Define and communicate a clear, high‑level strategy separate from the roadmap.  
  - Re‑educate stakeholders on the distinct responsibilities and language of product management.  
  - Allow flexibility and discovery, avoiding the trap of overly detailed long‑term forecasts.  

These three points form the first part of the author’s seven‑reason framework; addressing them is a crucial step toward easing product challenges in Europe.
