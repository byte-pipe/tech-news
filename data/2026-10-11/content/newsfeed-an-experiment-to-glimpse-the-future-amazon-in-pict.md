From above, the patch of Amazon forest looks like an immense tapestry, full of shades and textures. Rising from the green landscape are metal towers that form six rings above the canopy. It is here, 80 kilometres north of Manaus in Brazil, that David Lapola, a climatologist at the State University of Campinas (Unicamp) in Brazil, and his team come to listen to the forest breathe.

The rings mark the site of the Amazon Free-Air CO2Enrichment Experiment (AmazonFACE), which is set to launch in the next few months. The decade-long project will test how the forest might respond to atmospheres of the future, when rising carbon dioxide levels are likely to affect plant growth and carbon storage.

CO2is an essential ingredient in photosynthesis, the process that plants use to fuel their metabolism. By converting this gas — along with sunlight and water — into sugars and oxygen, trees in the Amazon maintain their growth while removing CO2from the atmosphere.

According to the sixth assessment report of the United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) — the organization that assesses and synthesizes climate science to inform global policy — an increase in atmospheric CO2might lead to increased rates of photosynthesis in the forest, removing more carbon from the air and fuelling plant growth1. It’s this cycle, referred to as the ‘fertilization effect’, that Lapola and his team are seeking to test.

Once set up, sensors along a central tower in each ring will measure wind direction and CO2levels. Tubes along the outer towers of three of the rings will release CO2-enriched air at levels based on the sensor readings, bathing the trees inside their perimeter. The three other rings serve as untreated controls.

“Our atmosphere has about 420 parts per million (p.p.m.) of CO2. What we do is increase it to about 620 p.p.m. and see what happens,” Lapola says. The researchers will look at how this affects some of the Amazon forest’s key processes, beginning with the carbon cycle.

Lapola and his team want to know whether the extra carbon captured under high CO2levels is indeed stored in trees, as predicted by the IPCC. Lapola says that although photosynthesis is the main way in which carbon enters the Amazonian ecosystem, it is not the only route. “It could be that [carbon] enters tree leaves and escapes through the soil. If that’s the case, we won’t have the response the world expects — that more carbon gets stored in the forest,” he explains.

Versions of the AmazonFACE experiment have been conducted in other forests around the world, but this marks the first in a tropical ecosystem. Previous experiments “were mostly in temperate forests, where the diversity of tree species is much lower than in the Amazon”, Lapola says.

One of those experiments was near Sydney, Australia: a 2017 study showed that more photosynthesis did not translate into more carbon being stored in the mature eucalyptus forest2. Low phosphorus levels in the soil were cited as a possible reason — another factor that the AmazonFACE experiment seeks to test.

“Phosphorus is essential in the construction of cell membranes, genetic material, energy molecules and complex carbohydrates that are structural to tree trunks,” Lapola explains. When the soil is balanced in nutrients, carbon absorbed from the atmosphere later becomes mostly trunk tissue, a long-lived form of carbon storage. Most of the Amazon forest stands onterra firmesoil, which is low in phosphorus. However, it is unclear how this might limit the trees’ ability to convert carbon into organic matter.

Changes in CO2levels might affect another component of the forest, too — water. An increase in atmospheric carbon levels means that stomata — the tiny pores on the backs of leaves — do not need to be open for long to collect the carbon needed for photosynthesis.

This could protect trees from drought, says Lapola, but it might also lead to a drier atmosphere in the area, because the trees have less time to transpire. “Multiply that process for a whole forest, and you disturb the hydrological cycle locally and beyond,” he adds.

To test this theory, the team is collecting data on water levels in trees in the plots. A metal band placed around each tree trunk will be checked monthly using calipers — a tool with adjustable jaws — to measure the trees’ thickness, width, diameter and depth. Trees expand and contract seasonally owing to shifts in water levels in the trunk tissue. Tree growth will also be measured.

The AmazonFACE project is led by the National Institute for Amazonian Research (INPA) in Manaus and by Unicamp. Lapola coordinates the project on behalf of Unicamp and ecologist Beto Quesada has the same role on the INPA side. The project first started to take shape during a workshop at INPA in late 2011, says Lapola, with construction commencing in 2014.

The project has not been without its obstacles, he adds. There were delays between the project’s inception and building the ring structures. In 2017, the team had hoped to have two or three years of baseline data already collected, says Lapola, “but the money [for the rings] wasn’t coming”. He says that the team continued to collect baseline measurements, such as CO2levels in the forest.

In November 2021, as world leaders convened in Glasgow, UK, to debate climate policy at the UN COP26 climate summit, the UK government announced a donation of £2.5 million (US$3.35 million) to AmazonFACE. The Brazilian Ministry of Science invested another 32 million reais (US$5.7 million) in 2022, and finally the rings could go up.

As Lapola walks around the site, which he visits monthly, he remembers the early days of the project. “There are things within the rings that I placed with my own hands, like the plastic platforms we must walk on, so the vegetation just below won’t get cramped,” he says. Nowadays, he simply checks in with those who are gathering the data that will inform future climate models.

“My field visits are less exciting than one would normally imagine. I get surrounded by documents, forms to fill, calls to make — as well as press visits and welcoming authorities,” he says.

“I miss being in the forest — even when I schedule field visits months in advance, it’s not always possible to be on the ground,” he adds. Not being in the forest can feel disappointing at times, but he knows the project is in good hands. “The team makes the magic happen.”
