# Summary of “‘Wallace and Gromit,’ 90% Alone”

## 1. Nick Park’s time on *A Grand Day Out*
- The film premiered at the Bristol Animation Festival in November 1989 alongside *Creature Comforts*.
- Initial media coverage was modest; a magazine described it as a “marvelous Lancastrian model animation” about Wallace, Gromit, and a moon‑bound rocket.
- Park was surprised by the Oscar nomination, noting he hadn’t realized short animated films could be nominated.
- *Creature Comforts* won the Academy Award, but *A Grand Day Out* secured a BAFTA and launched a major franchise.
- Park created roughly 90 % of the film himself, lacking a full team and treating it as an overly ambitious graduation project.
- After the film aired on Channel 4 in 1990, it attracted admiration from Terry Gilliam, Ray Harryhausen, and a wider audience.
- Peter Lord (Aardman co‑founder) acknowledged that Park’s “Nick Park look” reshaped the studio’s style, eclipsing earlier works.

## 2. Early influences and education
- Park began animating in his teens with 8 mm cameras, producing shorts like “Rat and the Beanstalk” and “Walter Goes Fishing.”
- His childhood inspirations included British stop‑motion TV shows (e.g., *Roobarb*, *Clangers*), Monty Python, Ray Harryhausen films, and comics such as *The Beano*.
- He studied at Sheffield Art School and the National Film and Television School (NFTS), immersing himself in film history, lighting, storyboarding, and avant‑garde animation.
- Eastern European animators (Starewicz, Trnka, Švankmajer) particularly influenced his desire to move beyond conventional children’s puppet animation.

## 3. Production details of *A Grand Day Out*
- Originated in 1982 as Park’s graduation film; he revived characters from an old sketchbook, initially drawing Gromit as a cat and giving Wallace a mustache.
- The plot—building a basement rocket to the moon—served as a vehicle for Wallace’s cheese obsession.
- Collaboration was limited: friend Steve Rushton helped with the first script; Alison Snowden and David Fine contributed props; a school technician built the rocket’s console; actors Peter Sallis and Peter Hawkins provided voices as a favor.
- Budget constraints meant Park handled most of the animation; he later eliminated Gromit’s dialogue, relying on expressive eyes, which defined Gromit’s silent, intelligent persona.
- Wallace’s mouth evolved from a small opening to a “coathanger” shape to match the voiced cheese line, illustrating Park’s iterative design process.

## 4. Reception and lasting impact
- Early visits from Aardman’s David Sproxton and Peter Lord noted the ambition and charm of Park’s work, prompting ongoing contact.
- The film’s success established Wallace and Gromit as iconic characters, giving Park a distinct visual style (close‑set eyes, exaggerated mouth movements, expressive canine eyes).
- *A Grand Day Out* demonstrated how a largely solo effort could reshape a studio’s identity and influence the broader animation landscape.
