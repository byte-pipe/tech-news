# Acute hunger grips nearly 20 million people in war‑battered Sudan  

## Key Findings  
- The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) reports that **19.5 million** Sudanese are facing acute hunger, about 40 % of the population.  
- Fourteen areas in North Darfur, South Darfur and South Kordofan remain at risk of famine; **135 000** people are in “catastrophic” hunger.  
- An estimated **825 000 children** are expected to suffer severe acute malnutrition.  

## Conflict‑driven drivers  
- The three‑year civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) has caused hundreds of thousands of deaths, massive displacement and the collapse of food and health services.  
- RSF sieges of El‑Fasher and Kadugli contributed to famine conditions; El‑Fasher was taken by RSF in October, while the army broke the siege of Kadugli earlier this year.  
- Ongoing hostilities around major supply routes (e.g., El Obeid) and the threat of renewed sieges keep access to food and aid severely limited.  

## Human‑impact and coping mechanisms  
- Families resort to extreme survival strategies: eating leaves, animal feed, and even breaking into closed slaughterhouses to obtain animal skins for food.  
- Lack of food and health‑care access is expected to cause many deaths, especially among children and the most vulnerable.  

## Additional aggravating factors  
- Drone warfare has become the dominant mode of combat, killing at least 880 civilians since January and targeting markets, hospitals and power stations.  
- The rainy season beginning in July coincides with the lean planting period, likely worsening food insecurity.  
- International dynamics, such as the US‑Israel conflict with Iran, are driving up prices of food, fuel and fertilizer, threatening harvests later in the year.  

## Reporting limitations  
- IPC and other figures may underestimate the real situation because of restricted access for humanitarian workers and monitors.  

## Call to attention  
- Experts describe Sudan’s crisis as “invisible,” urging continued international focus and advocacy beyond the statistics.
