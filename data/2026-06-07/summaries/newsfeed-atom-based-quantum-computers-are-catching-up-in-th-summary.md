# Atom-based quantum computers are catching up in the race to usefulness | New Scientist

# Summary of “Atom‑based quantum computers are catching up in the race to usefulness”

## Background
- Quantum computers promise breakthroughs in material and drug discovery and could break current internet encryption.
- The dominant approach uses superconducting circuits (e.g., Google, IBM), but neutral‑atom systems—ultracold, electrically neutral atoms—are emerging as a viable alternative.
- Atom Computing, led by Ben Bloom, focuses on building a neutral‑atom quantum processor.

## New Achievements
- Demonstrated repeated error detection and correction within the same device, a prerequisite for practical quantum computing.
- Scaled error‑correction groups from 16 to 32 qubits without adding errors; larger groups actually showed lower error rates.
- Maintained continuous error‑checking for up to 90 successive cycles, illustrating the possibility of “error correction ad infinitum.”
- Combined all necessary capabilities (large qubit count, low error rates, sustained operation) in a single experiment, a first for neutral‑atom platforms.

## Significance
- Shows that neutral‑atom qubits can rival superconducting qubits in both scalability and reliability.
- Aligns with parallel advances: Google (2023), University of Science and Technology of China (2025), and Harvard (2025) achieved similar qubit‑count and error‑rate improvements in other technologies.
- Positions neutral‑atom quantum computers as strong competitors in the race to useful quantum machines.

## Remaining Challenges
- Despite 90 rounds of error checking, some residual errors accumulated, indicating room for further reduction in error rates and faster computation.
- Overall speed and error‑rate performance still lag behind ideal targets; ongoing work aims to address these issues.

## Outlook from Researchers
- Bloom and his team are actively refining error‑mitigation techniques and expect continual performance gains.
- Jeff Thompson (Princeton) and Mark Saffman (Wisconsin‑Madison) view the experiment as a “tour de force” that paves the way for continuously operating neutral‑atom computers, though they note the need to curb accumulated errors.
- Consensus among experts is that physical limitations hindering neutral‑atom systems are diminishing, and rapid industry‑wide progress is anticipated.
