# Are we getting to the point where it’s safe to gene‑edit babies?

## Background
- In 2018 a Chinese researcher used CRISPR‑Cas9 to create three gene‑edited babies, sparking worldwide condemnation.
- The main criticism was the technique’s safety: CRISPR‑Cas9 cuts both DNA strands, often causing unwanted mutations and chromosomal abnormalities.
- Base editing, an improved CRISPR form, changes a single DNA letter and cuts only one strand, reducing the risk of off‑target damage.

## Recent US Study
- Researchers at Columbia University (Dieter Egli et al.) applied base editing to healthy two‑cell human embryos donated by parents.
- Two target edits were attempted:
  - First edit succeeded in ~75 % of cells with no detectable off‑target changes.
  - Second edit succeeded in ~50 % of cells but frequently produced unwanted mutations.
- The authors attribute the difference to guide‑RNA design and suggest better design could minimise off‑target effects.

## Remaining Obstacles
- **Mosaicism**: Base editing did not achieve uniform changes in every cell of an embryo; resulting embryos contain a mixture of edited and unedited cells.
- Mosaic embryos could still develop the disease the edit intended to prevent.
- Current testing methods (e.g., sampling a single cell from an IVF embryo) cannot reliably detect mosaicism.

## Potential Solutions
- Editing germ cells (sperm or eggs) before fertilisation could eliminate mosaicism, as the edited genome would be present in all subsequent cells.
- A start‑up claims to generate sperm from stem cells, opening the possibility of editing these precursor cells, though this has not yet been demonstrated in humans.

## Conclusion
- While base editing shows promise in reducing off‑target mutations, the problem of mosaicism means germline gene editing is not yet safe for clinical use.
- Solving mosaicism—potentially through pre‑fertilisation editing of gametes—is a prerequisite before regulators would consider approving germline interventions.
- Ethical considerations about whether we should edit human embryos remain separate from the technical safety issues.
