Main content
 

 

I’ve owned a few dogs in my lifetime. I’ve learned that their attention span can vary a lot. If they see a squirrel or other small critter, their focus on that animal is intense and exclusive. They can’t see or do anything else until that animal moves away. Those distractions are highly interesting to the dog and can cause them to forget all else. Tech vendors, I’ve learned, can be dogs, too.

ERP vendors, like many other technology purveyors, can get overly enthused about a new technology. When that happens, their focus gets shifted, sometimes temporarily and sometimes permanently.  That means that their attention span can go overly and singularly deep into the new tech while their customers want something else: value. That situation isn’t necessarily a good thing for the vendor or the customer.

We’re at another one of those moments where ERP leaders can’t help but chatter on and on about a new technology – this time it’s the many flavors of AI. But what does the customer really want and are these AI tools the tonic they need?

What software buyers want today is still the same thing they wanted decades ago: value. Value is what has driven millions of businesses to buy/license/subscribe to new tech for decades. And this is a logical behavior as businesses have to justify their capital expenditures. Many ERP deals have required approvals from a board of directors with most also needing a green light from a Steering Committee. This is an important point: Vendors should not forget that executives, boards, etc. will want to understand how buying new technology is going to be value creating and accretive.

## Value requires knowing where you are & where you’re going

To do a good job of calculating a potential technology’s ROI, you need to know what your 'As-Is' processes, workflows, data, etc. cost and how they work. For that you need process documentation, access to best practices, policies, control requirements, compliance mandates, and more. What all of these inputs enable is a way to document the staffing requirements, error rates, rework activities, exception processing routines, approvals, and other metrics of your current operations.

When evaluating new technology, a smart technology buyer will develop a new set of processes, workflows, etc. This To-Be state reflects new methods, processes and technologies that the customer believes will deliver a better value proposition over the status quo.

When one compares the staffing, throughput, service level, efficiency and other metrics from the As-Is and To-Be states, the differences are what will justify this new technology purchase. Smart tech vendors provide buyers with tools that:

* Aid in configuring the new solution
* Document existing processes
* Identify non-standard activities and non-value-added work
* Spot where controls are lacking
* Identify missing or broken integrations
* Document the hundreds or thousands of ad hoc spreadsheets and reports that have been added to the legacy solutions
* Identify all of the best of breed tools/apps in use currently
* Provide best practices especially those that optimize operations in specific verticals/micro-verticals
* Recommend needed skills, staffing and roles for the new 'To-Be' workflows

But that’s not necessarily happening with ERP and other vendors today. While vendors might insist that they “sellsolutionsnot just software”, the reality is quite different.  For a software vendor to offer a whole product, they would need to include many of the items above (and more). ERP vendors have often ignored these whole product needs and leave this work to integrators, consultancies and systems implementers.

Surprisingly, I had a recent conversation with a software executive on this point and she doubted that any software buyer needed an economic or ROI justification to purchase their ERP solution. That statement flummoxed me and I consulted with two other ERP industry analysts to see if they agreed with her. They didn’t. In fact, the industry analysts confirmed my own client interaction experiences where ERP decisions almost always need an economic justification as well as a thorough risk analysis.

That vendor interaction also stood out as this person seemed to think that since AI solutions can be deployed more quickly than prior generations of software products that a ROI assessment is not needed. That’s simply not true and here are a couple of reasons why:

* Even if/when a vendor can provide 'To-Be' workflow designs, the buyer still will not know if the vendor’s suggested approach will be more or less labor intensive than existing methods. The proposed new flow(s) could slow down other processing, require more approvals than the status quo, or, simply require too many hands touching on the transaction.
* Likewise, the prior, pre-AI workflows never had a ‘person in the middle’role that needed staffing. If an agentic AI workflow is lacking guardrails, prone to hallucinations, or, just plain quirky, your firm might need more people supervising the AI workflows than were staffed in the original pre-AI processes.

A solid ROI analysis might also identify long-tail AI costs that don’t show up in assessments of AI pilots. For example, where’s the accounting for costs and labor associated with:

* Switching out LLMs from time to time to, hopefully, get more reliable and useful results
* Investigate aberrant AI processing results and then making adjustments to the underlying data in use and/or adding additional guardrail and deterministic logic to prevent future gaffes.
* Finding, hiring, paying and training subject matter experts to fill all of the needed ‘person in the middle’roles.

## AI’s fatal ROI flaw

As if the above isn’t off-putting enough, there’s this little problem of why vendors can’t do the ROI calculations today. This matter involves 2-3 costs that cannot be accurately budgeted today and these could be quite material. These costs are:

* Token/Usage Costs– This is a substantial unknown as pricing is changing frequently with considerable variability each month. For customers who want certainty (for budgeting purposes), these costs can be material and difficult to connect to specific agents or other AI powered apps. If a customer learns, post-implementation, that token costs are excessive, how can they decide which capabilities to turn-off or throttle back?
* Tolls and Access Fees– If an AI agent needs data from the application database of another vendor’s solution, that other vendor may assess a toll charge/access fee. The right to access one’s company data may be restricted or economically difficult. Legacy software vendors see these fees as a way to increase their own revenues and a way to lock customers into their own tools/apps while discouraging the use of best-of-breed tools.
* Basic AI Costs vs Premium App Costs– Many ERP vendors are providing access to a number of their AI capabilities at no additional charge (e.g., a Generative AI powered job description generator). However, a number of other, more advanced capabilities will come with an added cost. Unfortunately, application software vendors have yet to draw a bright line as to which capabilities fall into which camp and how much the premium apps will cost.

The bottom line is that you can’t really know whether an AI solution is a good deal or not if you don’t know what it will actually cost. ERP vendors and others are using compute services and AI capabilities from third-parties who haven’t settled on a pricing mechanism for their ‘product’ just yet and are continuing to invoice their customers in differing ways and formulas as they learn more about what AI data center costs will entail longer term, what specialized AI chips will cost, etc.

## Is value really a requirement today?

Some readers might argue that it has been ages since a solution like ERP could throw off a positive ROI. They would argue that many efficiency and productivity gains were already accounted for in previous ERP implementations. And, they’d be right about that. That’s why some people don’t event speak of ROI – instead, they discuss Total Cost of Ownership (TCO). TCO assumes there may be little financial upside to a new piece of software but its functionality is needed as a cost of doing business (e.g., it produces completed tax forms).

The TCO angle is a fair one to surface except that it has the same problem that ROI does. Notably, neither calculation can be completed with all of the costs included. We can’t do that in the current AI environment.

Value and value realization are definitely important to software buyers. Whether it’s software for personal or business use, the purchase must justify the expenditure of someone’s hard earned capital. The transaction should help create more than enough value to offset the potential disruption to the business, the cost of the capital deployed for this solution, etc. In most cases, it should return more value than what it costs. Value could also include attributes like error reductions, productivity/efficiency gains, avoidance from fines/penalties, improved employee morale, meeting government requirements, etc.

But, no matter how you define value, can you truly say whether you’ll get your desired value if you don’t even try to quantify it? Too many software vendors aren’t really helping customers/prospects determine what if any value they’ll get.  Are they also helping to determine the ROI from arisk-adjustedperspective? Remember, there are a number of risks in these tools from hallucinations, probability errors, bad training data, inadequate guardrails, missing persons in the middle, etc.

## What’s the vendor narrative now?

Oooph! That’s an uncomfortable conversation.

Today’s narrative in ERP software is an unattractive mess. Vendor messaging is all about Agentic AI, agent factories, AI platforms, no/low code tools and other technology components. Value is rarely, if ever, mentioned in all of this AI-dominated technology cheerleading.

Today’s narrative is one note: AI, AI, AI, AI ….ad nauseam.

The narrative is technical and literal. Nothing about it is aspirational or experiential. If the messaging ever touches on the human aspect, it’s often about cutting headcount. This makes the current messaging heartless, devoid of empathy and not at all addressing the people that will need to use the new solutions. Seriously, do you know any clerks, staff accountants, supply chain planners, etc. who really are excited about what AI is doing to their jobs?

But more than anything else, today’s narratives are almost always non-differentiated. Go ahead, listen to several ERP vendors talk about their offerings. It’s all the same. You won’t find much of anything there to noticeably compare one solution to another. Now, the vendors will approach the sales & marketing of their solutions with manic energy and huge amounts of enthusiasm. But enthusiasm is NOT differentiation.

Software solutions should always tie back to the value they are creating for their users/customers. Are your vendors doing this now?

## My take

This conference season has all been about Agentic AI and the cheerleading of each vendor’s AI architectures. Cheerleading is not the same as fact-based value delivery proof. The industry definitely needs better tools to document the potential and achieved value that their solutions can deliver/are delivering.

While the financial aspect of the narrative is notably absent this year, it can’t remain this way without adversely impacting future sales. Customers will act rationally and logically. They will gladly experiment with some algorithmic, generative and agentic AI capabilities but large-scale adoption will need better proof points. If Wall Street and vendors are waiting for massive adoption to happen, the value narrative and required tools need help.
