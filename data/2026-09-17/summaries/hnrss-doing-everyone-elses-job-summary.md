# Doing everyone else's job – Summary

## Core ideas
- Doing tasks that belong to other roles gives insight into how those roles operate and what they need.  
- When a new tool or feature is ignored by its intended users, taking the initiative to integrate it into their system can be effective (e.g., Intel adding support for its CPU into Microsoft’s compiler).  
- Managers who do not actively manage can still be bypassed by working directly with their team members, provided you do not claim credit.  
- Getting a feature added is often easier by submitting a well‑crafted patch than by navigating a rigid Scaled Agile planning process.  
- The author observes a 20 % / 80 % work distribution, with a tiny (<5 %) group handling the “someone else’s job” tasks that keep the organization alive.

## Benefits
- Direct involvement in multiple roles makes you one of the few who truly understand how the organization functions.  
- Organizations that are struggling but still viable tend to reward those who fill the gaps.  
- Indirect rewards include reputation, influence, and a clearer view of systemic problems.

## Pitfalls & regrets
- Avoiding areas that seem “boring” or outside your core responsibilities can lead to preventable crises later.  
- Relying on others to complete essential work that you know will not happen can cause the organization to “drown” in problems.

## Practices to avoid
1. **Parallel duplication instead of true assistance**  
   - Building an in‑house solution when a satisfactory external product exists, merely to increase headcount, can waste resources.  
   - Decentralizing a service across departments (each building its own version) may please managers but harms overall efficiency and standardization.  
   - Both cases prioritize short‑term managerial comfort over long‑term outcomes.

## Insight from Dan Luu (quoted)
- Founders often claim that knowing the jobs they hire for makes scaling easier.  
- Hiring is easier when you have performed the role yourself, but the real challenge becomes managing people who excel at jobs you cannot do.  
- The limiting factor for large teams is the ability to manage complementary expertise you lack.

## Takeaway
- Proactively doing others’ work can be a strategic advantage, but it must be applied thoughtfully: avoid creating redundant effort, and focus on outcomes that benefit the whole organization rather than merely satisfying individual managers.
