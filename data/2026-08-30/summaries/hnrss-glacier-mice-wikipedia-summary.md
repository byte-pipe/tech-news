# Glacier mice

## Definition and composition
- Colonies of globular mosses that form on glaciers and nearby environments.  
- Consist of several moss species and can host nematodes, springtails, water bears, and other microorganisms.  

## Distribution
- Observed in diverse locations: Alaska, Chile, Greenland, Iceland, Svalbard, Uganda, Venezuela, and several sub‑Antarctic islands.  

## Reproduction
- Harsh glacier conditions may favor asexual reproduction of the mosses within the mice.  

## Movement characteristics
- Exhibit non‑random, herd‑like movement across ice, not explained solely by wind or slope.  
- Average displacement is about 2.5 cm (0.98 in) per day.  
- Accelerometer data show they rotate and roll, exposing all surfaces over time.  

## Proposed mechanism
- In the Northern Hemisphere, sunlight heats the southern side of the dark moss, melting ice there and creating a small depression.  
- The moss rolls into the depression, repeating the process and producing a gradual southward drift; the opposite occurs in the Southern Hemisphere.  

## Ecological role
- Retain heat and moisture, creating microhabitats that support microorganisms unable to survive on bare ice.  

## Longevity
- Studies suggest glacier mice can persist for six years or more.  

## History
- First described in 1950 by Icelandic meteorologist Jón Eyþórsson, who named them “jökla‑mýs” (glacier mice).  

## References
- Key studies include works by Greenfield‑Boyce (2020), Hotaling et al. (2020), and earlier research documenting their ecology and morphology across various glaciers.
