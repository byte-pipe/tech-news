# The Internet Is Kind of a Predatory Cesspit Now

## Early internet nostalgia
- Recalled the 1990s web as slow, ugly, and amateur‑driven, built by hobbyists sharing niche interests.  
- Despite scams, viruses, and other bad actors, the space felt like a public square without a profit motive.

## Predation moves from fringe to core
- Scammers once had to hunt victims; today platforms locate, profile, and push them a payment link.  
- The network itself now optimises the “pitch‑to‑payment” funnel, making exploitation the organising principle.

## The modern machine of vulnerability
- Any human insecurity is turned into a commercial niche (courses, newsletters, private groups, referral codes).  
- Consumers become both buyers and distributors, often earning nothing while promoting products they barely understand.

## Addiction as the business model
- Feeds deliver cycles of outrage, fear, envy, lust, and hope, each paired with something to buy.  
- Users “doom‑scroll” to acquire anxiety that influencers monetize, then repost the pitch, turning consumption into distribution.

## Grift economy size and participation
- The author’s 2020 view of a small, morally degenerate grifter class proved wrong; the economy is massive and participatory.  
- Many people devote all waking hours to streaming, posting, recruiting, and selling, treating every interaction as a lead.

## The illusion of entrepreneurship
- Most participants never succeed; they serve as free labour for platforms and cheap distribution for higher‑up sellers.  
- Affiliate marketers, life‑coach “coaches,” and dropshipper “teachers” recycle the same content, creating a social‑first pyramid.

## Predator‑prey blur
- Grifters often believe the worthless products they sell, because admitting failure would invalidate their sunk investment of time, money, and identity.  
- Recruiting new victims sustains both the fraud and the seller’s self‑justification.

## Endless need versus satisfied demand
- Traditional trade ends when a need is met; online grifts must keep the need alive (e.g., grievance merchants, wellness influencers).  
- Misery generates recurring revenue; satisfaction would end the funnel.

## Platform reinforcement loops
- Recommendation systems run reinforcement‑learning experiments on human weakness, optimizing attention, retention, and conversion.  
- Operators cannot fully explain the loops, regulators struggle to understand them, and social harm appears as an externality.

## Selection pressure favors sensationalism
- Honest experts receive few views; sensationalists (crypto hype, wellness cranks, conspiracy podcasters) attract millions.  
- The business model, not factual accuracy, determines success.

## Dopaminergic, Skinner‑box economics
- The system thrives on anticipation, uncertainty, and variable reward; most refreshes bring nothing, increasing urgency.  
- Social media combines a Skinner box with commission structures, handing users referral codes and the title of “small business owner.”

## Crypto as the apex of the grift
- Crypto bundles alienation, precarity, gambling, techno‑mysticism, and libertarian rhetoric into a predatory investment product.  
- Promotion drives price movement, which is presented as adoption proof, recruiting more buyers and creating a self‑reinforcing propaganda network—a pyramid scheme with a podcast department.

## Generalised lesson
- Across the internet, the cheapest product is empty promises; the most scalable labour force is the addicted user.  
- Selling a worldview turns users into free advertisers, because criticizing the product now feels like criticizing one’s own identity.
