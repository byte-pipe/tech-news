# License Plate Reader Searches Should Require a Warrant

## Overview
- The author, a former crime analyst who works with police departments, argues that while ALPR (automated license‑plate‑reader) technology is valuable, its historical data searches should be subject to a warrant.
- Cites the case *Schmidt v. City of Norfolk* where the judge ruled the current lack of warrant requirement “not today,” implying future change.
- Emphasizes that cameras will become ubiquitous, making the warrant question a matter of “when,” not “if.”

## Key Arguments
- **Ubiquity of Cameras**  
  - Expectation that cameras will be installed at most intersections soon.  
  - Once widespread, ALPR data will be comparable to cell‑phone location data, which already requires a warrant after *Carpenter v. United States*.

- **Benefits of ALPRs**  
  - Low cost (under $3,000 per unit) and easy integration with existing hardware (e.g., smartphones).  
  - Even a modest increase in arrests per camera can yield a positive return on investment.  
  - Anticipates broader adoption of cheap, high‑quality public‑space cameras (e.g., Ring‑type systems).

- **Active Flags vs. Historical Searches**  
  - Active flagging (e.g., immediate alert when a stolen plate is read) is generally reasonable without a warrant, especially in exigent circumstances.  
  - Historical searches—querying cached data to trace a vehicle’s past movements—are analogous to CSLI or geofence warrants and should require judicial oversight.

- **Current Legal Landscape**  
  - *Carpenter* established that detailed location data from private providers needs a warrant because of the high expectation of privacy.  
  - Subsequent cases (*Chatrie*, *Beautiful Struggle v. Baltimore*) reinforced the principle that extensive historical surveillance constitutes a search.  
  - By analogy, ALPR historical data should be treated the same way.

- **Policy Recommendation**  
  - States should enact statutes mandating warrant procedures for historical ALPR searches to protect privacy while preserving legitimate law‑enforcement uses.

## Practical Considerations
- **Exigent Circumstances**  
  - Short‑term data retention (e.g., New Hampshire’s 3‑minute window) may be insufficient for many emergencies; a few hours could be more appropriate.  
  - Real‑time alerts for active crimes (e.g., a robbery) remain justified without a warrant.

- **Current Practices**  
  - Many departments already retain extensive logs (plate, latitude/longitude, timestamp), enabling detailed movement mapping.  
  - The author’s own experience shows how quickly a single plate can generate a dense location history.

- **Potential Abuse**  
  - Existing safeguards against misuse of ALPR data are described as “laughable,” underscoring the need for stronger legal controls.

## Conclusion
- ALPR technology is a cost‑effective tool for policing, but the unchecked retention and retroactive querying of its data pose significant privacy risks.  
- Aligning ALPR historical searches with the warrant requirements applied to other forms of digital surveillance would balance public safety with constitutional privacy protections.
