# Mark Ritson: Brands are all in on creators. Meanwhile in reality, the economics crash

#### ByMark Ritson,Founder, educator, consultant

 MiniMBA
 

August 10, 2026|10 min read

Listen to article4 min

As a new report exposes the harsh economics facing creators, Mark Ritson argues that marketers should keep investing in the channel... if they can barter for what they are actually getting.

A 'real' image of Mark as an influencer (prompted via ChatGPT)

Creators are huge. Everyone says so. Cannes Lions this year had more creators than clients, and they all sported considerably better tans. Every CMO deck now contains the obligatory creator slide, wedged between the AI slide and whatever we are calling purpose these days.

“Creator-led” has replaced “digital-first” as the phrase that ends careers slowest. And the money has followed the mouth. Some 86% of American marketersnow use influencer marketing, up from 64.5% in 2020, according to eMarketer.Brands will spend more than $12bn on creators in the US alone this year. Goldman Sachssizes the wider creator economy at $480bn by next year.

Huge.

Enormous.

The biggest thing in marketing. At this rate, the creator economy will absorb 148% of marketing budgets by next Tuesday.

Advertisement

Who is telling you this?

Well, it’s the creator economy firms, and they are… well… creative with their numbers. Ask the industry how big it is, and you will get four different answers.

This year, the sector is worth $34bn.

Or $39bn.

Or “more than $40bn.”

Or, if you prefer the most excitable vendor decks, $47.8bn.

That is a $14bn spread between estimates of the same market in the same year, and the estimates come, almost without exception, from influencer marketing platforms, influencer marketing agencies and influencer marketing trade bodies. And that spread equals Equatorial Guinea’s entire GDP in 2026. In other words, no one really knows how big it is.

The people most convinced that the influencer economy is the biggest thing in marketing are, it turns out, influencers, for influencer marketing. The inmates are not merely running the asylum. They are running the whole mental health system and most of healthcare too.

Which brings us to the sector’s favorite number.

Brands earn $5.78 for every dollar spent on influencer marketing.You have seen it. You have probably had it quoted at you by an agency with a straight face. It comes from Influencer Marketing Hub, and it is denominated in “earned media value.” EMV, for the uninitiated, is what you get when you count up impressions you did not pay for, multiply them by a rate card you just invented and declare the result in financial terms you don’t really understand.

It is not money. Nobody banks EMV. No CFO has ever paid a dividend from or for it. It is astrology with a wonky spreadsheet attached. And it sits on top of an audience that is partly cardboard anyway.One audit of 100,000 creator accounts found 37% of influencer followers showed signs of being fake or purchased.

Meanwhile, over in the reality corner of the creator economy, the one no one spends any time in, something rather different is going on. The arse is falling out of it, and we know because the industry just told us so itself. A new August trends report from New Engen – a creator agency, note, not a hater –lays out the numbers the sector prefers not to headline.

More than half of all creators now earn less than $15,000 a year from their content, according to Influencer Marketing Hub’s own earnings report,up from 48% just two years earlier.Brands worked out that they could pay 40 nano-creators the price of one mid-tier professional and call it ‘authenticity’ at scale, sonano and micro accounts vacuum up almost half of US creator spend, per eMarketer.The mid-tier professional, the supposed middle class of this glittering new economy, is the one starving. Marisa Kay, a creator with 44,000 followers, announced last year that she was quitting influencing for a corporate job. And I bet she had plenty of company walking through the exit line to reality-land.

Advertisement

Those who stay are monetizing in ways that should give every worshipper at the creator altar a moment’s pause. The hot new revenue stream for the squeezed middle is paid DMs. Fans now pay creators $20 or $30 a message for a “personalized” reply, a workout tip, a review of their song lyrics. The trade press reports this development with genuine triumph, as though charging strangers by the unit for simulated interaction were a business model rather than a desperate distress signal. In several documented cases, the personalized replies are outsourced, so much for authenticity.

And the audience is quietly leaving the party too. Some 55% of Americans now post less to social media than they did five years ago, and 51% describe maintaining a social presence assomething that “feels like work,” according to a 2026 Incogni survey of US adults. Time spent on Instagram is still climbing, but Meta’s own data reveals why: the algorithm is force-feeding short video into the feed. Engagement is rising because the machine is shoveling shit harder than before, rather than because the humans are showing up more. The influencer economy is thinning at both ends. Fewer influencers can afford to stay in, and fewer civilians are awaiting their influence.

None of this means creators are a dead strategy. Marketing has a pathological habit of swinging between total infatuation and total rejection like a pissed-up trapeze artist with a personality disorder. It’s all genius! It’s all shit! Repeat until breakdown.

So, for the record: creators work. They are agoodmarketing tactic if you understand why they work, and when to deploy them.

Firstly,influencer marketing was always a reach medium.It was more lucrative to price it as a trust medium.

What you were buying, underneath all the talk of authenticity and community and parasocial connection, wasreach, attention and human faces, which have stopped the scroll and transferred feeling to brands since the dawn of advertising time. That is a perfectly good thing to buy. It is simply worth less than the trust story charged for it. Nor does it “upend the nature of marketing forever” or “end the era of big advertising as we know it.”

Advertisement

Calm down, dear.

Creators were oversold. They are a channel. A decent, useful, mid-ranking channel with genuine strengths, significant fraud, collapsing talent economics and measurement that partly runs on self-promotion and make-believe. Like all the others. The correct response is strategy, perspective, training and calm.

Because the oldest lesson in marketing is the one every shiny new object makes us forget. There is no killer tool. There has never been a killer tool.Tools work best in combination, each covering the weaknesses of the others, which is why TV plus search beats either alone, why brand plus activation beats both apart, why the most important word in the long and the short of it is the little one beginning with “a,” and why the best work of the past decade has been resolutely multichannel. Campaigns are called campaigns for a reason. They are military operations, fought with lots of weapons at once, coordinated toward a single objective. They were never meant to be won withone gleaming new radar gun, purchased at enormous expense, that turns out to be about half as good as the man from Radar Gun Inc told us it was.
