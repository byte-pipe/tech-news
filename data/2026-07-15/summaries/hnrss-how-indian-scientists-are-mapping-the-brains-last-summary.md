# How Indian scientists are mapping the brain's last frontier

## Main achievement
- SGBC at IIT Madras created **Anchor**, the world’s most detailed three‑dimensional cellular‑resolution atlas of the human brainstem.  
- The atlas merges more than 500 tissue sections from fetal, childhood, and adult brains, using high‑resolution microscope images and eight chemical markers.  
- It identifies over 200 clusters of cells and nerve pathways, allowing seamless navigation from whole‑brain MRI to individual neurons.

## Importance
- Bridges the long‑standing gap between medical imaging (MRI) and cellular pathology.  
- Users can zoom from the entire brainstem seen on MRI down to single neurons while preserving exact spatial relationships.  
- The digital atlas is freely available online as a reference tool for neuroscientists, neurologists, and neurosurgeons worldwide.

## Potential applications
- Comparative analysis of healthy versus diseased brainstem tissue for conditions such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, stroke, SIDS, and COVID‑19‑related neurological damage.  
- May improve neurosurgical navigation of the delicate brainstem, enhancing patient safety and outcomes.  
- Provides a framework for studying how neurological disorders reshape the brain at the cellular level.

## Expert commentary
- Shubha Tole describes the project as an “unprecedented integration” of engineering, neuroscience, and medicine, placing India on the international stage.  
- Rebecca Folkerth says the atlas fulfills a long‑standing dream of matching brain scans with microscopic anatomy.  
- Partha Mitra predicts a transformative impact on the study of neurological disease and on mapping protein distribution across brain regions.

## Methodology and scale
- Around 20 scientists spent 18 months manually analyzing more than 200 brain sections, combining MRI data, microscopic anatomy, and 3‑D reconstruction into a single digital resource.  
- The approach relies on high‑resolution images of thin post‑mortem slices, making detailed cell‑level mapping affordable compared with costlier molecular techniques.  
- SGBC now hosts over 200 researchers, engineers, and technicians collaborating with international partners.

## Future directions
- Plan to image more than 100 whole human brains across different life stages and neurological disorders, creating a comprehensive reference library.  
- Aim to reveal how diseases alter the brain cell‑by‑cell, advancing understanding of conditions such as Alzheimer’s, autism, and infection‑induced damage.  
- Continue leveraging engineering and computation alongside biology to map the brain’s remaining unknown territories.
