# A revolutionary stem‑cell therapy must proceed responsibly to achieve its full potential

## Background
- Shinya Yamanaka’s 2006 discovery of induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells showed that four genes can reprogram adult mouse cells to an embryonic‑like state.  
- The method was quickly replicated in human cells, opening the possibility of generating patient‑specific healthy tissue for disease treatment.  

## Recent clinical progress
- Early 2025 saw conditional approval in Japan of two iPS‑cell therapies: one for Parkinson’s disease and another for heart failure.  
- Clinical trials worldwide are now evaluating iPS‑cell therapies for macular degeneration, diabetes, and other conditions, with results expected over the next five years.  

## Safety challenges
- Undifferentiated stem cells can form tumours; therefore, therapies must ensure complete reprogramming and removal of embryonic‑like cells.  
- Development timelines have lengthened to address these safety concerns, emphasizing rigorous pre‑clinical testing.  

## Need for responsible development
- Rapid, unregulated advancement could cause a harmful trial, jeopardising the entire field.  
- Researchers and regulators must establish clear guidelines and prioritize patient safety to maintain public trust.  

## Manufacturing and accessibility
- Autologous (patient‑specific) iPS therapies are costly and slow; “off‑the‑shelf” allogeneic products from donor iPS lines aim to reduce cost and increase availability.  
- Improved manufacturing processes are expected to attract more companies and broaden patient access.  

## Outlook
- Yamanaka likens translational research to a marathon, stressing that steady, patient‑focused progress is essential.  
- With responsible stewardship, iPS‑cell therapies have the potential to benefit large populations and fulfill the promise of regenerative medicine.
