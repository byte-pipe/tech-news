# The Amazon tax

## Main argument
- Amazon’s search‑ad system is framed as “legal theft,” not a tax that funds public benefits.  
- The platform generates roughly **$1 billion each week** from these ads, enough to give every employee a **$35,000 cash bonus** with money left over.

## How the ad system works
- Ads appear alongside organic search results, often pushing less‑relevant items ahead of the best‑reviewed, best‑priced products.  
- Even manufacturers of the top‑ranked products must bid on ads to protect their sales, creating a **zero‑sum competition** where the total category sales remain static.  
- The presence of many ads forces sellers to spend on clicks just to maintain market share, not because ads are inherently effective.

## Impact on sellers and consumers
- Sellers bear the ad costs, but those costs are ultimately transferred to **consumers**, making products more expensive.  
- Brands may prioritize cheaper, lower‑quality versions of their products to free up budget for clicks, degrading overall product quality.  
- Amazon (and previously Google) have an incentive to **worsen organic search results**, encouraging even more ad purchases.

## Evidence and examples
- The publisher’s most successful ad was the search term **“Seth Godin The Knot,”** costing about **$1 per click**—essentially paying Amazon to show an ad for a book the buyer was already planning to buy.  
- A prior critique (Cory, three years earlier) noted that the scale of these ads has **already doubled**, confirming rapid growth of the system.

## Conclusion
- Amazon’s ad model reduces the efficiency of search, inflates prices, and discourages product innovation, contradicting its historic claim of being “customer‑centric.”  
- Although the practice is **legal**, it primarily benefits Amazon and advertisers, while **customers bear the hidden cost**.
