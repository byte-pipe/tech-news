As so many career counselors and lifestyle coaches have explained, first impressions matter. A bad one can set the tone for an entire relationship, whether that’s with a person, a novel, a restaurant, or a car. And the 2026 Volkswagen Jetta Sport and I did not get off to the best of starts.

Picture the scene: You’ve been at home for a total of one night out of the previous eight, and you’ve just landed at the airport after an eight-hour flight. It’s 85˚F (30˚C) and raining. You could take the metro home. Sure, 80 minutes isn’t quick, but all you have to do is sit there, then walk home a block. But you can’t because there’s a car to be reviewed, waiting to be driven home. In rush hour.

I will confess, I did not know VW made a Jetta Sport. For one thing, it’snotthe Jetta GLI. That car has plenty of power and torque and uprated suspension, and it can even be ordered with a six-speed manual, unlike the Golf GTI, althoughbased on our most recent GLI drive, you may regret ticking that particular box.

There’s no special engine under the hood here, just the same 1.5 L four-cylinder turbo like all the other Jettas, running on VW’s modified version of the Miller cycle, where the intake valves close earlier than most engines during the suction stroke to boost efficiency. At 11.5:1, the compression ratio is quite high, even for a more modern turbocharged engine, but it helps generate 158 hp (118 kW) and 184 lb-ft (250 Nm), all of which goes to the front wheels via an eight-speed automatic transmission.

On that first hot, rainy afternoon, the power and torque delivery left a lot to be desired. There are no sport or eco modes, just one throttle map for all situations. And below 2,500 rpm, the engine was easy to modulate on a part-throttle. But keep that part-throttle and let the revs past that threshold, and the combination of gearing and turbocharger throws a giant slug of torque at the road, making you look like a moron who shouldn’t be allowed out in public as the car lurches and accelerates faster than you planned.

That initial drive took even longer than the metro would have due to road closures and construction. Learning how to modulate the throttle in heavy traffic wasn’t helped by an alert that sounded every time I exceeded 20 mph, warning me about winter tires. The car was not, in fact, on winter tires, but I crossed that speed threshold plenty of times as I crept along I-66. The following day, a quick read through the manual revealed how to disable that alarm, though it offered no advice on smoothing out the power delivery.

Even after days, this truculent response to being driven gently remained, although it could be partially ameliorated by leaving the transmission in S rather than D. This holds each gear longer, leaving you at the mercy of the throttle map and turbocharger, albeit with a concomitant hit to fuel economy because the gearbox won’t shift to the next ratio so soon.

By Friday, I was thoroughly underwhelmed with the Jetta Sport and ready for it to be gone.

## I thought I hated you, but I was wrong

My decision to take the Jetta Sport to Cars and Coffee on Saturday morning was not entirely mean-spirited. In fact, it turned out to be revelatory. Traffic was unusually absent, and the roads were abnormally cambered and twisty. Taken by its scruff, the Jetta showed a different character. And I’m not talking ten-tenths tire squealing, either. But using the transmission in manual mode—pushing it to change up a gear and pulling it toward you to downshift, the opposite of a real sequential shifter—lets you keep the engine out of the “nothing, nothing, nothing—WHOA” section of the power band. It’s not particularly fast at changing gears, but it works.

The steering gives you enough information about what’s happening at the front tires to let you know what a sensible cornering speed should be, and the car’s electronic brains will use the brakes as an electronic limited-slip differential, slowing a slipping wheel to allow the torque to go to the side of the axle with grip.

I expected to arrive at Cars and Coffee ready to mock the four-door VW, but I arrived a little humbled at how much I enjoyed the drive.

It helps that the Jetta Sport starts at just $25,305, which makes it the second-cheapest Jetta you can buy. In fact, this is one of the very cheapest cars of any make or model on sale today. So if you aren’t looking to spend more than that and absolutely must have a brand-new car from a dealer, this is what you get. And if you haven’t bought a car in more than a decade and $25K sounds like it should be BMW or Cadillac money,here’s a useful inflation calculatoryou can use to translate the Jetta Sport’s MSRP into numbers your brain can make sense of.

Fuel economy is a combined 33 mpg (7.2 L/100 km), with 29 mpg (8.1 L/100 km) in the city and 40 mpg (5.8 L/100 km) on the highway, which is better than similarly priced rivals like the Toyota Corolla or Kia K4. But you’ll only see those high numbers if you stay out of the turbo’s power band. It’s not a combined high-40s like a hybrid, but you’re not buying a brand-new hybrid at this price, either.

For the 2026 standards, there are some novelties, like a physical key that has to be inserted into the steering column lock before you can start the car. Yes, there’s remote locking, but you can forget about a push-button start. You do still get Apple CarPlay and a couple of USB-C ports, though, and the 8-inch infotainment screen is clear, even if it’s not big and flashy. The multifunction steering wheel also has real buttons, as the Jetta never got a facelift with the controversial capacitive steering wheel, but the climate controls are on a capacitive panel that is at least backlit at night.

I won’t pretend that the Jetta Sport would be at the top of my buying list. But I also won’t be particularly upset if one happens to be my next rental car. First impressions matter, but they can also be misleading.
