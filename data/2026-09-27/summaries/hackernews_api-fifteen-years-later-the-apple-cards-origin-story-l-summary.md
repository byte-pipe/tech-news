# Fifteen years later, the Apple Cards origin story — Lex on Tech  

## Overview  
- In 2011 Apple launched the **Cards** iOS 5 app, letting users design custom letterpress cards that Apple printed and mailed.  
- The app was a **Steve Jobs‑initiated** project, codenamed **Speed Racer**, built during the last year of his life.  

## Author’s Background & Conflict with Apple  
- Lex wrote about the app for *Macworld*; Apple later asked for an update emphasizing the 100 % cotton paper, which Lex and his editors declined.  
- Other writers have also faced push‑back from Apple over coverage of Cards.  

## Insider Account (Mike)  
- **Mike** worked for the primary printing partner that handled Apple’s iPhoto print products since 2006, managing operations in the US, Czech Republic, and Japan.  
- In 2010 Apple added a new iPhoto print line (**Downieville**) requiring fine letterpress on “toothy” cotton stock, using restored 1850s Heidelberg presses.  

## Steve Jobs Origin Story  
- During a walk after dinner, Jobs suggested sending a thank‑you card directly from an iPhone, prompting Apple to create a dedicated Cards app.  
- The project launched quickly, using brand‑new designs and letterpress templates, and aimed to ship both in the US and Europe.  

## Technical & Logistical Challenges  
- **Letterpress & Debossing**: Cards were heavily debossed on cotton paper, a process distinct from traditional letterpress embossing.  
- **Invisible Barcodes**: Apple required no visible barcodes; a UV‑visible barcode was sprayed on envelopes, allowing USPS to scan each step while keeping the envelope appearance clean.  
- **Custom Stamps**: In the US Apple used a unique heart‑shaped stamp; in the Czech Republic a standard stamp was selected.  
- **Printing Process**:  
  - Three‑pass workflow: pre‑coating, letterpress shell imprint, then digital photo imprint via HP Digital Indigo press.  
  - Ink adhesion to cotton stock was problematic and required extensive trial.  
- **Postal Integration**: Apple coordinated with USPS and Czech Post, employing Bell + Howell sorting equipment and extensive engineering support (including engineers flown in from China).  

## Launch Experience  
- Forecasting was poor; actual first‑day demand was far lower than expected (“fit in a shoebox”).  
- Despite the mismatch, Apple prepared massive logistical resources, with trucks and staff on standby for the October 2011 launch.  

## Aftermath  
- The Cards app was eventually sunset, and the involved printing companies have largely disappeared.  
- Mike, still bound by anonymity, reflects that the project remains “the pinnacle of incompetence, project mis‑management and intercontinental mayhem,” yet it stands as a unique chapter in Apple’s history.
