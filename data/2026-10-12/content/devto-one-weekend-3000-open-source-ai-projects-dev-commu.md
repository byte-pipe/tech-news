Hacktoberfest: Open Source Reflections

Every October for the past twelve years, developers around the world have taken part in Hacktoberfest, a month-long celebration of open-source software: code that anyone can use, inspect, and improve. For most of that history, the rules were simple: Contribute a set number of pull requests and earn a T-shirt.Thousands of people made their first contribution to open source because of Hacktoberfest.

This year, we changed the format.Hacktoberfest 2026 is focused on open-weight models and open-source artificial intelligence: models and tools that anyone can download, run and modify. Participants are learning how these systems work, building their own AI tooling, and adapting existing open models for new uses.

When we announced this year’s theme in August, we had hoped to support 300 in-person gatherings.There are now more than 500 Fests planned in over 60 countries, ranging from university hackathons to small groups of developers meeting at their local library or office. In the first week alone, participants in-person and online submitted more than 3,000 projects.

On Oct. 1, the first day of the event, students at COMSATS University in Abbottabad, Pakistan, gathered for a Hack Day. By evening, they had built a locally hosted hospital-management platform, a multimodal companion for visually impaired users, and a tool that helps newcomers make their first open-source contribution. The group photo they sent us afterward says more than any statistic I could share: a group of people who chose to spend their day learning to build something together.

For many people, AI is something experienced alone: typing into a chat window, using a tool built by someone else and governed by someone else's pricing and policies. What I'm seeing this month is a clear appetite for something different. People want to sit next to each other, run a model on their own machine, take it apart, and understand how it works.

That is why we built this year's Hacktoberfest around open innovation. Open source has always worked because nobody owns the tools. Anyone can look under the hood, and anyone can pull up a chair. We believe AI should work the same way: shaped by people building in public in India and Ireland, Peru and the Philippines, all over the world.

Hacktoberfestruns through Oct. 31. When it ends, I don’t expect its impact to be measured solely in code. It will show up in mentorships that started at a folding table, in local tech communities meeting for the first time, and in people who walked in unsure what an AI model was and walked out having built something with one.
