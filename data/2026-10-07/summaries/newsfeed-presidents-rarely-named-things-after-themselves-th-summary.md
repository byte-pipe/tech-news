# Presidents rarely named things after themselves. Then came Trump

## History  
- The practice of sitting presidents attaching their own names to public assets is uncommon.  
- In December 2025 Trump’s name was added to the Kennedy Center’s exterior, but a judge ordered its removal, ruling that only Congress can rename the “living memorial” to John F. Kennedy.  

## George Washington  
- Washington appeared on the $1 bill only long after his death; early Congress rejected putting a president’s portrait on coins to avoid monarchical symbolism.  
- He generally avoided self‑celebration, though the federal capital was named Washington while he was still in office.  

## Dams Named for Presidents  
- A few dams bear presidents’ names (Teddy Roosevelt, Calvin Coolidge, Woodrow Wilson), but the naming was driven by supporters, not the incumbents.  
- The Hoover Dam was originally called Boulder Dam; Interior Secretary Ray Lyman Wilbur renamed it in 1930 to honor Herbert Hoover’s role in the Colorado River Compact.  
- After FDR took office, Interior Secretary Harold Ickes pushed to revert to “Boulder Dam,” and the name change was not solidified until Congress voted in 1947 to restore “Hoover Dam.”  

## Presidential Libraries and Personal Branding  
- Franklin D. Roosevelt did not seek many eponyms, yet he established the Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library and Museum, a public‑private institution he initiated.  
- Trump announced plans for a “presidential library” in Miami, describing it more as a hotel with a grand façade and a 747 Air Force One on display, ensuring his name will dominate the building.  

## Trump’s Recent Naming Pushes  
- Trump has publicly demanded his name be placed on the Kennedy Center, threatening closure if denied.  
- He and supporters have called for his name on naval vessels, airports, and other infrastructure, reviving a rare trend of a sitting president seeking personal branding on public assets.  

## Takeaway  
- Historically, presidents have shied away from self‑naming to avoid the appearance of a personality cult.  
- When naming does occur, it is usually driven by political allies or post‑presidential honors.  
- Trump’s aggressive pursuit of name placement on prominent sites marks a notable departure from this long‑standing norm.
