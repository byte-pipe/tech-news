Presidents rarely named things after themselves. Then came Trump
 
While Trump pushes to get his name on the Kennedy Center and other public sites, history shows that sitting presidents have almost always avoided putting their brands on government assets.

President Trump recently underscored his desire to have his name appear on the facade of the Kennedy Center with a threat. "Frankly, if we don't do that, it's going to close," Trumptoldreporters. "It'll end up being ripped down."

 

Trump or his supporters have called for the president's name to appear on everything fromnaval battleshipstoairports. It's pretty rare for sitting presidents to stamp their identities on infrastructure, currency and other public assets — though history shows there are a few exceptions.

 

### George Washington "didn't want a lot of fuss"

 

Discussions regarding this issue go all the way back to the first presidential administration.

George Washington is now the face on $1 bills, but that didn't happen until long after his presidency and his death. When Congress was figuring out what to put on the inaugural American coins, the House of Representatives said the president shouldn't be on the currency because that's something a monarchy would do.Victoria Arocho/APhide caption

"They were trying to figure out what was the right balance between the prestige required to give this new office respect, but also recognizing that they were not trying to re-create a monarchy," saidLindsay Chervinsky, executive director of the George Washington Presidential Library and author ofThe Cabinet: George Washington and the Creation of an American Institution.

 

Chervinsky said the issue first came up at the federal level during Washington's presidency around theCoinage Actof 1792. Congress was figuring out what to put on the inaugural American coins, given the habit in Great Britain and other nations of plastering the reigning monarch all over theirs.

 

"The House of Representatives said that the president should not be on currency because of this monarchical tie," Chervinsky said.

 

Chervinsky said Washington shared their concerns. The president usually shied away from ostentatious self-celebration. "He didn't want a lot of fuss," she said.

 

But sometimes, Washington didn't seem to mind it. "They did name the federal city Washington to honor him while he was still in office," Chervinsky said.

 

### But what about the Hoover Dam?

 

 

### Politics

### Marketing or a cult of personality? Trump's name and face are all over the place

Most administrations have generally kept the names and faces of sitting presidents off public assets since then, viewing it as unseemly.

 

On the few occasions when a sitting president's name has appeared — such as on dams named forTeddy Roosevelt,Calvin CoolidgeandWoodrow Wilson— it tended not to be the man in office pushing for it himself. "Usually it is political supporters," Chervinsky said. And the history around the naming process can be fraught.

 

The most prominent example of this is the Hoover Dam.

The massive public works project located near the Boulder Canyon on the Arizona-Nevada border had been known in the early stages of its development as theBoulder Dam. Hoover's secretary of the interior, Ray Lyman Wilbur, suggestedrechristeningthe project in 1930 as the Hoover Dam, owing to the lead role Hoover had played in its planning. "Hoover negotiated the deal among the states known as theColorado River Compactthat allocated the river's water among seven states," said Kenneth Whyte, author ofHoover: An Extraordinary Life in Extraordinary Times. "So it was generally agreed that it was deserved."

Apparently, the president thought so too. "Hoover, I think, was pleased about it," said Whyte. "But he did not talk a lot about it at the time. He didn't want to be seen to be doing self-aggrandizing things."

 

But the Franklin D. Roosevelt administration was having none of it. After Roosevelt took office in 1933, his secretary of the interior, Harold Ickes, called for the dam to revert to its original moniker.

 

 

### Author Interviews

### A Concrete 'Colossus': The Hoover Dam At 75

"Ickes based this argument on the fact that the original legislation that authorized Congress to fund the project was called the Boulder Canyon Project Act," said Marist University's David Woolner, asenior fellowand resident historian at the Roosevelt Institute, and the author of several books about the 32nd U.S. president. "And he did not like the politics of Herbert Hoover."

 

Woolner said Roosevelt did not object to the change. He kept his political rival's name out of his 1935dedicationspeech, consistently referring to it as "Boulder Dam."

 

The naming coup was short-lived.

 

Although there was some initial opposition from Democrats, in 1947, Congress voted unanimously tochange the name back. Woolner said this happened "in recognition of Hoover's early support for the project, and after the former president gained praise for hishumanitarian workduring and after the First and Second World Wars."

 

### There's always the presidential library ... or "library"

 

Roosevelt, for his part, wasn't "particularly anxious to have things named after him," Woolner said.

 

But that didn't stop Roosevelt from lending his name to one major project during his presidency — theFranklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library and Museum— "which of course he initiated," said Woolner.

In keeping with this tradition, President Trump told reporters at apress briefingearlier this year he is planning to build himself a presidential library in Miami.

 

Except it probably won't be a library. And presidential libraries are, technically speaking, public-private assets.

 

### Politics

### What to know about Trump's future presidential library, which he says may be a hotel

"I don't believe in building libraries or museums," Trump said. "But it's most likely going to be a hotel with a beautiful building underneath and a 747 Air Force One in the lobby."

 

One thing that seems certain, judging by avideo renderingon the president's social media channel promoting the project: Trump's name will be prominently displayed on its facade.
