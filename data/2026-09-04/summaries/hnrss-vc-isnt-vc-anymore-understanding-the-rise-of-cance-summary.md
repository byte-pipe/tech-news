# VC isn’t VC anymore — understanding the rise of Cancer Capital

## Overview
- The article argues that today’s “venture capital” has morphed into “Cancer Capital,” a system dominated by a few billionaire firms that operate without market, regulatory, or democratic accountability.  
- The author writes from personal experience as a founder who raised tens of millions and served on boards, stressing that the critique is not driven by personal grievance but by observed industry corruption.

## How Venture Capital Turned into Cancer Capital
- **Original role of VC**: Intended as a small, high‑risk portion of the overall capital market, not the default funding source for startups.  
- **Growth into dominance**: Looser regulations allowed a tiny elite (≈1 % of firms) to combine VC with private‑equity activities, managing tens of billions of dollars.  
- **Loss of risk exposure**: These mega‑funds earn management fees (≈2 % of $50 B) regardless of portfolio performance, making them effectively unaccountable and no longer true venture capital entities.  
- **Impact on smaller VCs**: The remaining 99 % are forced to operate under the rules set by the dominant firms, even if they disagree with the practices.  
- **Shift in power dynamics**: Founders are no longer the primary drivers; instead, VC firms publish extremist political agendas and select founders to execute them.  
- **Public unawareness**: New entrants to tech assume the old VC model and are confused when their ideas are redirected toward the goals of Cancer Capital.  
- **Political perception**: Politicians and media still view VC as a job‑creator, unaware that its primary aim now is wealth concentration for a few tycoons.  
- **Public money involvement**: Pension funds and retail retirement accounts increasingly fund these firms, exposing ordinary investors to high‑risk bets they never chose.  
- **IPO decline effect**: With fewer tech IPOs, Cancer Capital firms cash out early, profiting from companies that never become profitable, while ordinary investors suffer when those companies eventually go public.  
- **Legal transformation**: By shedding VC legal status, these firms can buy unlimited shares, trade assets between their own funds, and extract value from portfolio companies without any real earnings.
