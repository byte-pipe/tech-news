Back in 2009, Arswrote aboutwhat wasbeing reported as a leaked trailerfor the then-upcomingLeft 4 Dead 2, saying the footage “looks like it’s straight from a high-gloss action movie.” Today, we need to offer an important update to this crucial, 17-year-old story: The trailer “leak” was orchestrated by Valve itself.

That news comes courtesy of former Valve writer Chet Faliszek, whosaid in a newly posted videoabout cut lines in the game that “forLeft 4 Dead 2with the ESRB… we leaked our trailer ahead of time because they wouldn’t let us release the trailer.”

Faliszek is referring tothe Entertainment Software Rating Board, the self-regulating industry group that, since 1994, has offeredsometimes questionableage-appropriateness ratings for thousands of games submitted to its purview. While many smaller and/or online-focused game makers skip out on getting those ratings these days, at the time, any publisher that wanted space on major retailers’ shelves needed to submit to the ESRB’s standards.

 The “leaked” trailer that was apparently too hot for the ESRB.

 

Those standards included restrictions on how companies could market their games as well. And looking back at the“leaked”Left 4 Dead 2traileron YouTube, you could argue that itfalls afoul of guidelinesthat prohibited trailers with “graphic and/or excessive depictions” of “violence… the use of weapons… [and] blood and/or gore” as well as those with “crude and/or offensive language.”

At the time, Valve had some recent firsthand experience with the ESRB’s sometimes onerous enforcement of its marketing standards. Earlier in 2009,the ESRB requested a change to aLeft 4 Dead 2promotional imagefeaturing a hand with three gnawed-off fingers, leaving two intact digits indicating the number of the sequel. To pass the ESRB’s muster, Valve shot a new version with two of those fingers back in place (and simply curled down) that eventuallyappeared on the game’s box art.

“Faliszek pointed out that this is a game where people are torn apart using a chainsaw,”we reported at the time. “But bitten off fingers on the logo? That’s crossing some line.”

## “Likely to cause serious offense to the average consumer”

The ESRB of nearly two decades ago could also be quite restrictive regarding game trailers in particular. In 2007, the ratings grouprequested the removal of trailersfor D3’sDark Sectorand 2K’sThe Darknessafter they had already been shared across the web (behind age gates on most major news sites). Ina statement at the time, the ESRB’s Patricia Vance said that “the mere presence of an age gate does not permit a publisher to simply put whatever content it wishes into the trailer. All trailers must still conform to [the Advertising Review Council]’s Principles and Guidelines, which prohibit the display of excessively violent content or any content likely to cause serious offense to the average consumer.”

According to Faliszek, Valve decided to get around this potential enforcement action for theLeft 4 Dead 2trailer by simply seeding it as a “leaked” version thatit knewwouldquickly spreadaroundthe Internetto effectively market the game (even tothose who were loudly planning to boycott its release). At the time, though, some people doubted the purported “unofficial” nature of the trailer’s release, as evidenced bythis contemporaneous Reddit thread.

“You have to remember… Valve was in fights with the ESRB, with Australia, with Germany, with a bunch of countries about the profanity in the game, the violence,” Faliszek said in his recent video. “I’ll just admit that [we leaked the trailer]. I think we can’t get in trouble for that now. It’s like 20 years ago.”

Despite Valve’s marketing subterfuge at the time, theLeft 4 Dead 2trailer footage seems downright tame compared to the kind of blood and gorethat’s almost standard in game trailers released these days. And that same “leaked” footage was eventuallyused as the intro cinematic in the game itselfjust months later, when itmerely got an “M for Mature” rating from the ESRB. If the violence or language in that trailer was really “excessively violent” or “likely to cause serious offense,” the group could have given the game a much more restrictive AO (Adults Only) rating,as it initially did forManhunt 2just two years before.

Today, Valve and other game publishers don’t have to worry about a repeat of this kind of marketing scenario. The ESRB’supdated advertising principlesnow explicitly allow trailers to include “any content from the game or live-action or pre-rendered sequences… provided that such content reflects the game’s true nature and does not supplement or exceed the Pertinent Content in the game.” Seventeen years ago, though, Valve had to lie to the ESRB, and the Internet at large, to get itsLeft 4 Dead 2trailer footage into the world.
