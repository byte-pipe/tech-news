The language of India's young, perpetually-online population has finally reached one of its oldest institutions.

Days aftera youth-led protest movement forcedthe resignation of education minister Dharmendra Pradhan, India's parliament found itself speaking the protesters' language.

"Delulu." "FOMO." "Clock it."

Until recently, those words belonged to Instagram comment sections, group chats and meme pages. On Tuesday, they echoed across the Lok Sabha (lower house of parliament) instead.

As MPs debated a new anti-exam leak bill, introduced by the government after weeks of protests led by theCockroach Janta Party (CJP), Gen Z slang flew freely through the chamber.

Shiv Sena party's Shrikant Shinde accused the opposition of being "MIA" - missing in action - gripped by "FOMO", or the fear of missing out, before eventually becoming "delulu", Gen Z shorthand for detached from reality.

Congress leader Deepender Singh Hooda, meanwhile, invoked the viral protest catchphrase "waste-guna-huiya" - a deliberately nonsensical meme popularised during the demonstrations - to poke fun at the government.

Government lawmakers embraced the internet's lexicon too.

Bansuri Swaraj of the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) declared that Prime Minister Narendra Modi had "clocked it" - Gen Z slang for getting something right or spotting the solution - by introducing the law.

Her fellow MP Tejasvi Surya accused the opposition of being "delulu" for criticising the bill after failing to pass similar reforms while in power.

Across the aisle, opposition leader Supriya Sule briefly stepped outside the debate to marvel at the aburdity of the moment.

"Why are we getting into the lingo of Gen Z?" she asked. Moments later, she was using "delulu" herself.

It was an unusual moment. A movement that had begun online, fuelled by memes and Instagram reels, had not only forced the resignation of a cabinet minister but now seemed to be reshaping the language of the parliamentary debate that followed.

The CJP began as a satirical internet collective. But outrage over alleged exam irregularities soon turned it into an unlikelyrallying pointfor broader frustrations over unemployment, failing institutions and political indifference. It also spoke a different language.

The movement was organised on WhatsApp, coordinated on Telegram and lived on Instagram. Its politicsunfoldedthrough "fit checks" - showing your outfit - "Get Ready With Me" videos, first-person vlogs, memes and sardonic edits. Protests became reels within hours, drawing millions of views and pulling more young people onto the streets.

The government's response increasingly unfolded on the same platforms. Modi, whose political rise has long been intertwined with social media, joined the digital battle with selfie-style videos and direct appeals to young people as the protests gathered momentum.

Opposition MPs later mocked some of those efforts in parliament, arguing it was easier to change camera angles than answer students' concerns.

It would be tempting to see all this as evidence that Indian politics had suddenly discovered Gen Z.

But Rahul Verma, a political scientist at the Centre for Policy Research, cautions against reading too much into a single parliamentary debate.

Politicians have always borrowed the language of popular culture, he says.

"They have an understanding of where their electorate is. That's their job."

The examples stretch back decades.

Parliamentary speeches have long borrowed from cricket, advertising and popular cinema. In 2019, the catchphrase "How's the josh?" leapt from Uri: The Surgical Strike - a blockbuster inspired by India's 2016 cross-border military operation against Pakistan- into the political mainstream. Originally a soldiers' rallying cry in the film, it soon echoed across election rallies and Parliament.

So the language may be new, Verma argues, but the instinct behind it is not.

Rather than signalling a fundamental shift in Indian politics, he says it may simply reflect politicians doing what they have always done: trying to speak the language of the voters they hope to win over.

But internet slang is different. Unlike a Bollywood catchphrase or a cricketing cliché, it isn't coined by scriptwriters or sporting heroes. It emerges from millions of online users, evolving collectively, mutating at breakneck speed and often disappearing as quickly as it appears.

That, says digital anthropologist Payal Arora, is what makes its journey into parliament significant.

"Language is often the first thing politics borrows when it is trying to connect with a new constituency," she says.

But borrowing a generation's vocabulary, she argues, is easier than engaging with the frustrations that produced it.

Using Gen Z slang signals "cultural awareness, not necessarily political transformation".

In the days since the protests ended, the government has appeared increasingly uneasy about the movement's digital afterlife. Authorities have sought the removal of some protest-related content, while police have pursued legal action against several students and activists linked to the demonstrations.

CJP leaders accuse the government of trying to intimidate protesters, making the withdrawal of those cases one of their key demands after Pradhan's resignation. They have warned they will return to the streets if the cases are not dropped.

The protest's internet humour also became one of its biggest points of contention.

Critics argued that memes, profanity and irreverent jokes trivialised a campaign fighting to strengthen India's education system.

Supporters insisted irony was simply the language of a generation raised online - that humour and demands for accountability could comfortably occupy the same screen.

Whether parliament's new lexicon endures remains to be seen. Internet slang moves fast, with trends often disappearing as quickly as they emerge.

One CJP protester, who asked not to be named, said hearing politicians adopt Gen Z slang left her distinctly unimpressed.

"Speaking our language isn't going to impress us without meaningful change," she said. "We want accountability, not politicians trying to copy us. That's cringe."

"We didn't ask them to speak like us," she added. "We asked them to listen."
