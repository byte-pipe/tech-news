# I’m 38 and I Can’t Support Myself Anymore

## Introduction – personal crisis
- I am 38 and had to email my mother asking for a loan, which triggered a panic about a lifetime without stable income.  
- The feeling of no longer being “economically useful” appeared early in my life, but I only named it after my disability became full‑time five years ago.  
- Social media posts praising discipline (“go to the gym despite pain”) highlight the cultural pressure to stay productive even when ill.

## The Gospel of Productivity
- Modern Western culture inherits the Protestant work ethic: hard work, discipline, and frugality are equated with moral virtue.  
- Although the theological basis has faded, its imprint remains in self‑help podcasts, hustle narratives, and wellness regimes that demand constant bodily optimization.  
- When chronic illness or disability prevents consistent work, the loss feels like a personal moral failure because worth is measured by productivity.  
- The common question “What do you do?” is interpreted as “How do you make money?” reinforcing the link between identity and income.  
- Illness therefore strips both financial resources and social legitimacy, denying cultural permission to feel worthy.

## The Cost of Having a Body
- Economic systems penalize bodies that cannot produce consistently; nearly 25 % of working‑age disabled adults in the U.S. live in poverty, versus less than 10 % of non‑disabled adults.  
- Disability incurs high direct costs: medical care, specialists, medication, mobility aids, therapy, accessible housing, delivery services, rideshares, and caregiving.  
- Everyday survival tasks (shopping, cooking, paperwork) become metabolically expensive for a chronically ill body.  
- Disability benefits are often set below the poverty line, creating a “benefits cliff” where earning a little more can eliminate essential assistance.  
- Invisibility of many disabilities fuels suspicion; people constantly feel pressured to prove the legitimacy of their suffering.

## The Fiction of Self‑Sufficiency
- Capitalism relies on the myth of an independent, self‑sustaining body unaffected by illness, grief, or aging.  
- Even the “self‑made” worker depends on collective care: families, educators, public infrastructure, medical staff, and emotional support.  
- Care labor (parenting, elder care, disability support) is culturally devalued and frequently unpaid, despite being essential for societal functioning.

## What I Owe Just to Stay Alive
- I experience a mix of gratitude and humiliation over needing help, and the prospect of a long, uncertain future feels terrifying rather than liberating.  
- In my twenties I could juggle a 9‑to‑5 job with music production and travel, barely managing but still able to blend in with peers.  
- Now the loss of autonomy is heavy; asking for financial help feels shameful, and I worry about how money reshapes relationships, even as I recognize the blessing of having a family willing to assist.
