# No dead ends: What the Dutch can teach us about tackling youth unemployment

## Context – UK youth unemployment crisis
- A recent report highlighted that almost 1 in 8 people aged 16‑24 in Britain are NEET (not in education, employment or training).  
- Alan Milburn warned that without urgent action, 1 in 6 young people could become NEET within five years.  
- The UK NEET rate for 18‑24‑year‑olds is 15.1%, compared with the Netherlands’ 4.9%.

## Dutch principle – “No dead ends”
- The guiding philosophy is that every stage of a young person’s journey must lead to further education, training or work.  
- Under Dutch law, children must attend school from age 5 to 16 and then stay in education or training until they obtain a qualification or turn 18.

## Education structure and qualification requirement
- **Kwalificatieplicht** (qualification requirement) obliges youths to achieve a recognised qualification before leaving formal education.  
- From around age 12, pupils are streamed into three secondary tracks based on teacher recommendations and test results:  
  - **VMBO** – practical route leading to vocational training.  
  - **HAVO** – prepares for universities of applied sciences.  
  - **VWO** – academic route to research universities.  
- The early streaming system is controversial, with concerns about self‑esteem and unequal opportunities.

## Vocational pathways and work‑study
- The **beroepsbegeleidende leerweg** allows students 16+ to combine part‑time work with study, often working most of the week and attending school one or two days.  
- Employers can request customised college programmes to meet specific industry needs.  
- Apprenticeships and state‑supported work‑study placements provide hands‑on experience; pay and job security vary by profession.  
- Skilled trades (e.g., electricians, roofers, technicians) are promoted as well‑paid, aspirational careers.

## Real‑world examples
- **Amelie** chose the VMBO track at 10, later studied fashion, left at 17, and struggled to find an internship. The compulsory qualification prevented her from dropping out, keeping her on an educational path.  
- **Destiny**, originally from Bonaire, entered a beauty‑therapy course, secured a paid internship, and transitioned directly into salon work, illustrating the seamless education‑to‑employment link.  

## Alternative support and safety nets
- Organisations like **Mooi Jong** work with school‑referred pupils at risk of becoming NEET, providing personalised guidance and interventions at multiple “junctions” where disengagement could occur.  
- Schools receive state funding for health and wellbeing, which can be used to bring in specialist providers, creating layered safety nets.  

## Truancy and early intervention
- Every absence is recorded; repeated lateness triggers conversations and involvement of municipal attendance officers.  
- Students classified as “thuis zitters” (people sitting at home) still generate a school budget that can fund external support.  
- Sanctions for unexplained truancy may include fines, community service or juvenile supervision orders.  

## Benefits and ongoing challenges
- Youth unemployment is rising despite the system; the Dutch government is simplifying access to benefits through the **UWV** (Employee Insurance Agency), which offers welfare payments, job‑seeker support and employer connections.  
- The “one‑stop‑shop” approach aims to keep at‑risk youths engaged before they become NEET.  

Overall, the Dutch model combines compulsory qualification, early streaming, strong vocational pathways, employer partnerships, and comprehensive safety nets to minimise dead‑ends for young people, offering lessons for the UK’s approach to youth unemployment.
