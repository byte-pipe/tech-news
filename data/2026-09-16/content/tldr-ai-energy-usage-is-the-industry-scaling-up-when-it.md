Main content
 

 

In the cloud era, organizations wanted to offload their data centers, as they sought the advantages offered by of software, infrastructure, and compute as a service. In the global games of Tetris that followed, small on-premises blocks became vast agglomerations of computing power owned by hyperscalers, and vanished. Out of sight, out of mind.

Nobody cared, as the colossal warehouses full of blinking servers were now hundreds of miles away, or even thousands – located next to a retail park on some dark desert highway, perhaps, rather than belching fumes into a village school at playtime.

But then everything changed. First, the arrival of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, and the rest promised instant, zero-effort access to machine intelligence. And while the prompt might have come from a laptop or a smartphone, the response came from a massive warehouse full of GPUs: a token factory.

Soon there were thousands of them in the US alone, with hundreds more coming onstream, while hyperscalers have been offering farmers millions of dollars to convert their fields into powered land. For America, it seems, the priority is data farms, not food security.

And second, as the US has seized political control over the token factories with threats and menaces to its allies, the rest of the world has realised it needs to reindustrialise too. So, suddenly those warehouses full of blinking servers are coming to you and your community, thanks for the urgent need for digital and data sovereignty, and for resilience against geo-political shocks.

In a landmass like the US, in vast states such as Texas, Wyoming, or Nevada, the local environmental impacts of AI might not be obvious, but in a nation as small as the UK, that data center may indeed be next to a village school.

And it is not just the fumes and noise that will affect local communities, but also that data center’s energy impact. So, what will that impact be?

## Environmental injustice

Oliver Hayes is Head, Global Policy and Campaigns, for an organization called Global Action Plan. Since 1993, it has worked with both communities and enterprises to counter what it calls “environmental injustice”. Speaking at a Westminster Energy Forum policy conference on data centers last week, Hayes set out the scale of the challenge facing the planet. Hayes said:

The International Energy Agency [IEA] has said that a typical AI-focused data centre consumes as much electricity as 100,000 households, while the biggest under construction today requires twenty times that amount. So, that’s two million households worth of electricity. This is at the core of why energy is becoming such a hot topic and is such a big problem.”

So, if there are roughly 5,500 data centers in the US, then they may be using the equivalent energy of up to half a billion households. And if each of those were thelargestAI facilities – purely for the sake of argument – then the figure would rise to 11 billion. Clearly these are not the actual numbers, as many of those data centers are relatively small. However, as a mental snapshot of thepotential scale of demand, the picture is alarming.

Hayes continued:

In November last year, the IEA predicted that under certain decarbonisation scenarios, 71 terawatt hours (TWh) of electricity would be needed by UK data centers alone in 2050. To add some context, that’s more than one quarter of the UK’s total electricity consumption last year. And those projections were double what they put out in 2022, so we can expect they will keep going up.”

Globally, forecasts of data center energy usage vary from 1,114 TWh by 2050, cited by BloombergNEF and others, and 5,000 TWh, predicted by Shell. Note that the smaller of those two figures alone equates to one tenth of the planet’s energy usage.

In the UK, whose land mass is smaller than Oregon’s, the implications of a single new-build AI data center using the equivalent energy of 100,000 households – rising to two million with a massive facility – are serious.

Bear in mind, the largest UK city, London, has a population of just over nine million, while the smallest, St Davids in Wales, is home to just 1,751 people. The point is this: in most cases, an AI data center will use far more energy than the local community does, because most British towns have populations of tens of thousands, and most cities a few hundred thousand.

And with pressure to categorise AI data centers as critical national infrastructure, they may be given preference in energy and water usage during any shortfall of either resource.

There are further concerns, said Hayes:

Clearly, there are implications for all this in terms of carbon emissions. We’ve looked at just the published emissions from data center developers in their planning applications. They're not required to say what emissions they will produce, but where some of them have, we've examined the figures. And just 10 of the applications in the planning system now would effectively wipe out the annual CO2 saved from switching to electric cars.

According to Hayes, 10 data centers’ published emissions, +2.7 million tonnes of CO2, would counterbalance the -2.9 million tonnes saved by switching to electric vehicles in the UK.

He concluded:

This is the heart of the issue: hard-won progress on decarbonisation risks being undermined by the rapid, unconstrained build-out of data centers.

## Mis-match

Part of the problem is a mis-match between the speed of AI development and the slowness of upgrading national grids, explained the UNin its own recent report. Noting that data center energy consumption is set to double by 2030, the UN said:

A large facility can often be built and connected within two to five years, but expanding transmission lines and other grid infrastructure can take more than 10 years because of lengthy planning, approval and construction processes.

In a small, densely populated country such as the UK, those planning and approval processes would take far longer, unless local objections are overturned by government order to seize the economic initiative.

Plus, until the nuclear industry can catch up with data centers’ demand for local generators, much of this energy will be supplied by either gas or fossil fuels, a challenge for which the UK is certainly not prepared. Hayes added:

With onsite gas generation, we hear that more than 100 data centres are asking about plans to burn gas. This is very bad news for anyone who is concerned about emissions.

Then he made a critical point:

There are, of course, going to be applications of AI which are beneficial to the environment. And the lower, more focused applications of AI are where the biggest gains will be. But it’s consumer generative AI where we see the fewest. And how this will play out for people, communities, and their bills [will largely be bad], plus there is the negative projection around jobs as well.

So, for all these reasons and more, we are seeing a huge amount of local resistance. People are very concerned, and they don't feel that the benefits are likely to be accrued by them. This issue will get bigger and bigger, and it’s not going to go away.

Indeed. At this point, however, I decided to throw a spanner into the conference’s works.

A recent investment note from financial analystJoachim Klementsuggested that the whole model of vast data centers and token factories running Large Language Models and industrialised inference may be a massive error. Little more than a (very loud) historical blip, in fact, like a giant bubble of hot air bursting (my words, not his).

Citing a recent Stanford University report by Saad-Falcon et al,Intelligence per Watt: Measuring Intelligence Efficiency of Local AI, Klement suggested that Small Language Models (SLMs) run on phones and laptops may be sufficient for most tasks, with rising accuracy in tests.

The original Stanford report explains:

Two advances create an opportunity to rethink it: small, local LMs (less than 20 billion active parameters) now achieve competitive performance to frontier models on many tasks, and local accelerators (eg Apple M4 Max) can host these models at interactive latencies. This raises the question: can local inference viably redistribute demand from centralised infrastructure?

Answering that excellent question demands measuring whether local LMs can accurately answer real-world queries and whether they can do so efficiently on power-constrained devices, such as laptops. And the answer? Yes, in many cases, according to the researchers.

Summarising their findings, Klemont explained:

SLMs achieve this performance at energy and compute costs that are between 50% and 85% lower than for an LLM, depending on the SLM and hardware used in the computer. What is more, SLMs are catching up rapidly. […] In 2023, the success rate of SLMs in reasoning tasks was typically 50% or so across all five difficulty levels. By October 2025, the SLMs achieved 99% success for the easiest reasoning tasks in levels 1 and 2, 85% to 92% success in harder tasks (levels 3 and 4) and only lagged LLMs in the hardest tasks of level 5 (51.5% success rate).

Now project those improvements a few years into the future, and we can see that the ‘big iron’ approach of US hyperscalers and AI giants may be short lived, especially as token expenditure is soaring in most organizations.

I put this point to the conference. Unsurprisingly, the data center builders sat on their hands and stayed silent, but Hayes responded with a useful point:

Yeah. This is one of the key reasons why – and this may seem like a terribly old-fashioned view – the government should say, ‘Here is the parameter, and here are the limits of how much energy capacity we think should be given over to data centers. Because if you start from the point of ‘this is what we can actually accommodate’, and ‘these are the harms we can tolerate and mitigate’, and ‘this is how we're going to pay for it, then the industry is going to have to develop within those limits.

And this allows for the developments raised in the question. Certainly, other countries are not following the same approach. For example, the Chinese approach is far less of a brute force and scale approach that we see from America’s Large Language Models. So, there are many benefits from a government saying ‘Here is the framework under which you're allowed to develop as an industry. Stick within it and see what you can do.

## My take

Would that be so bad? And be honest, do you really need a vast data center in Orgeon to summarise what your colleague just said in a meeting?
