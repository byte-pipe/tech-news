# Most people prefer traditional architecture

## Overview
- The modernist movement, emerging in the 1920s, came to dominate global architecture by the 1950s, emphasizing unornamented designs, steel, and concrete.  
- Historically, both supporters and critics noted that modernism was unpopular with the general public (e.g., John Summerson, 1940; Wolf von Eckardt, 1977).

## Visual preference surveys
- Since the 1990s, around twenty properly sampled surveys have examined public preferences for architectural styles.  
- Over 60 % of respondents in every survey favor traditional architecture; many surveys report support exceeding 85 %.  
- Preference is largely independent of age, gender, politics, socioeconomic status, and nationality.  
- The findings suggest widespread public discontent with dominant modernist trends.

## Evolution of surveying methods
- Early surveys (e.g., Metuchen, NJ, 1979) used uncontrolled photographs and non‑representative public‑meeting participants, limiting causal conclusions.  
- Improvements included better image editing, controlled image pairs, and larger, representative online panels.  
- Recent advances in artificial intelligence have lowered costs and increased the feasibility of large‑scale, reliable visual preference studies.

## Notable studies
- **Metuchen (1979)**: Residents preferred traditional, human‑scale streetscapes and rejected featureless, car‑dominated designs.  
- **Halpern (1987, Cambridge)**: Non‑architecture students and architecture students showed opposite preferences for building images, highlighting a professional‑public divide.  
- **British Market Research Bureau (1998)**: 94 % of potential new‑build buyers preferred traditional house designs; 92 % of other respondents wanted traditional houses built near them.  
- Subsequent surveys in the United States, Canada, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Chile have replicated the strong preference for traditional architecture.

## Implications
- The consistent public preference for traditional architecture challenges the dominance of modernist styles in contemporary design.  
- Policymakers and developers can use visual preference data to guide design codes, streetscape improvements, and housing projects that align with public taste.  
- Ongoing research and AI‑driven image generation promise even more precise assessments of architectural preferences in the future.
