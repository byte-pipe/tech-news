# AI energy usage – is the industry scaling up when it should be scaling down?

## Overview
- The shift to cloud computing moved on‑premises servers to massive hyperscaler data centers, distancing the public from their environmental impact.  
- The rise of generative AI models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok) created “token factories” that require huge GPU farms, prompting a rapid expansion of AI‑focused data centers in the US and elsewhere.  
- Geopolitical concerns about data sovereignty are driving the construction of more local data centers, bringing energy and environmental issues to nearby communities.

## Environmental injustice
- Oliver Hayes (Global Action Plan) highlighted that a typical AI‑focused data center consumes electricity equivalent to **100,000 households**; the largest under construction would need **20 × that amount** (≈2 million households).  
- With ~5,500 US data centers, total electricity demand could approach the equivalent of **½ billion households**; if all were the largest type, the figure would rise to **11 billion households** (illustrative only).  
- IEA forecasts for the UK: **71 TWh** needed by data centers in 2050, > ¼ of the nation’s total electricity consumption, double the 2022 estimate.  
- Global projections vary: **1,114 TWh** (BloombergNEF) to **5,000 TWh** (Shell) by 2050, the lower bound representing ~10 % of global energy use.  
- In the UK, a single AI data center could out‑consume the entire local community, raising concerns about preferential treatment as “critical national infrastructure” for energy and water.  
- Emissions data: 10 planning applications disclosed **2.7 Mt CO₂** annually, enough to offset the **‑2.9 Mt CO₂** saved by UK electric‑vehicle adoption.

## Grid‑capacity mismatch
- UN report: data‑center construction can be completed in **2–5 years**, but expanding transmission lines and grid infrastructure often takes **>10 years** due to planning and approval delays.  
- In densely populated regions (e.g., the UK), local objections can further stall projects unless overridden by government orders.  
- Until nuclear capacity scales, many data centers rely on **gas or other fossil fuels**, with > 100 centers reportedly exploring onsite gas generation.  
- Hayes warned that while niche AI applications can aid the environment, consumer‑grade generative AI offers few benefits and may increase household bills and job insecurity.

## Emerging counter‑argument
- Investment analyst Joachim Klement, citing a Stanford report (“Intelligence per Watt”), argues that the current model of massive data centers may be a **bubble**.  
- The report suggests **Small Language Models (SLMs)** running on phones and laptops can handle most tasks with improving accuracy, offering a more energy‑efficient alternative.

## Key takeaways
- AI’s rapid expansion is driving unprecedented data‑center energy demand, risking reversal of recent decarbonisation gains.  
- Local communities, especially in smaller nations, face disproportionate environmental and resource burdens.  
- Grid upgrades lag behind AI deployment, potentially locking in fossil‑fuel reliance.  
- Emerging research points to a possible shift toward smaller, edge‑based AI models that could dramatically reduce energy intensity.  
- Ongoing public resistance reflects concerns that AI benefits are unevenly distributed while costs are borne locally.
