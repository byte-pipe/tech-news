# The right kind of AI sceptic - by James Stanier

# Earn your scepticism

## Introduction
- The AI debate is split between believers in a transformative impact and those who view it as merely a useful tool.  
- The author, James Stanier, has experienced strong reactions to his pro‑AI writings, prompting reflection on how opinions are formed.  
- The article aims to show how to combine optimism and scepticism by grounding views in personal experience rather than identity.

## The two kinds of sceptic
- **Scepticism as a conclusion** – formed after hands‑on use of AI tools, supported by concrete observations.  
- **Scepticism as an identity** – adopted from surrounding discourse without direct experience, often expressed with memes (“just autocomplete”, “stochastic parrots”).  
- Real‑world examples:  
  - Engineers who dismissed AI until they tried Claude Code or Cursor and changed their stance.  
  - Developers who extensively used tools yet remained unconvinced because large codebases or niche languages still challenge the models.  
- In some communities, rejecting AI signals belonging; dissenters risk exclusion (the “crab‑bucket” effect).  
- Engaging with the tools does not require changing one’s mind; it simply provides an informed position.

## The enthusiasm mirror
- **Ungrounded enthusiasm** mirrors ungrounded scepticism and can cause greater harm, especially when leaders over‑promise.  
- Notable failures:  
  - Klarna’s claim of replacing 700 support agents with AI, later rehiring due to quality loss.  
  - Executives at Microsoft and Google announcing high percentages of AI‑generated code without reliable metrics.  
  - Start‑ups promising autonomous AI agents that turned into vaporware.  
- Mandates to use AI without leadership modelling, clear guidance, or proper tool evaluation breed cynicism.  
- Both extreme sceptics and extreme enthusiasts protect their identity, dismiss contrary evidence, and prioritize being right over being accurate.

## How to hold your view well about AI
- **First practice:** Use the tools yourself—beyond demos, short videos, or second‑hand opinions.  
- Engage with the best available model on a genuine problem from your own work to form a concrete, experience‑based opinion.
