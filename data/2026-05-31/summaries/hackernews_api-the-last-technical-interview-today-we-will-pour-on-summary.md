# The Last Technical Interview – Summary

## Overview
- The author, Steve Yegge, reflects on 35 years of experience conducting technical interviews at Amazon and Google.  
- He argues that the traditional technical interview process is fundamentally broken and is reaching its end, though the industry has not yet agreed on a clear replacement.

## Why the Current Process Fails
- **Inconsistent Evaluations**: Different interviewers often reach opposite conclusions about the same candidate.  
- **Poor Predictive Power**: Interview scores rarely correlate with on‑the‑job performance; many top performers were initially rejected.  
- **Systemic Bias**: Unconscious biases lead to both false positives (unqualified hires) and false negatives (qualified candidates turned away).  
- **Reliance on “Band‑Aid” Roles**: Positions like Amazon’s *Bar Raisers* or Microsoft’s *As‑Appropriate* act as babysitters, acknowledging that regular interview loops cannot be trusted.  
- **Statistical Evidence**: Internal analyses at Google showed that interview outcomes were statistically terrible, with a large fraction of hires later deemed poor fits.

## Illustrative Stories
- **Bar Raisers at Amazon**: A small elite group could veto hires, highlighting lack of confidence in regular interview panels.  
- **Google Hiring Committee Calibration**: Committee members unknowingly reviewed their own interview packets and voted to reject two‑thirds of them, exposing the process’s self‑contradiction.  
- **Cultural Resistance**: Questioning the interview system is often met with hostility (“farted in church”), and many defend it because they themselves survived the gauntlet.

## Consequences
- The interview process has become a “pseudo‑science” that cannot reliably answer whether a candidate can do the job.  
- HR teams recognize the flaws but lack the power to enact sweeping changes; technical leadership tends to preserve the status quo out of inertia.

## Outlook and Next Steps
- The author calls for a **radical redesign** of talent assessment, acknowledging that no simple “silver bullet” exists yet.  
- He suggests that once the industry collectively accepts the problem, it can begin exploring alternatives, though specific solutions are left for future discussion.
