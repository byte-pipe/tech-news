On 20 August, 2025, American restaurant chain Cracker Barrel released its new identity to the world, and what followed was a rebrand backlash for the ages. At the centre of the controversy was a newlogo designwhich sported a minimalist aesthetic and, more pressingly, the erasure of iconic old-timer good ol' Uncle Herschel. Critics demanded justice be served, and at last, in the year of our Lord 2026, it cometh.

After a messy fallout, Cracker Barrel's CEO Julie Felss Masino is stepping down from her role, soon to be replaced by the CEO of Bloomin' Brands, David Deno. Regardless of how you feel about the controversial rebrand, one pressing issue has reared its head. A bad logo can now cost you your job.

 

(Image credit: Cracker Barrel)

## What's woke got to do with it?

Cracker Barrel's rebrand was criticised for a number of reasons – some more measured than others. Many saw the new minimalist design as a sterilisation of the brand's quirky heritage, while others claimed it was the victim of the 'blandification' epidemic spreading across the design world. Then MAGA joined the conversation...

Soon cries of 'woke' were being hollered across social media as notorious MAGA figureheads such as mini Donald Trump Jr. lambasted the brand for its new logo. Over on X, Floridian Republican Byron Donalds wrote, "No one asked for this woke rebrand. It’s time to Make Cracker Barrel Great Again.” Woke War Room (yes, that's a real account) added, "Cracker Barrel’s new logo isn’t an accident – it’s CEO Julie Felss Masino’s project. She scrapped a beloved American aesthetic and replaced it with sterile, soulless branding," directly pointing the blame at the CEO herself.

 

(Image credit: SOPA Images via Getty Images)

## Facing the fallout

Despite Cracker Barrel's CEO revealing thesurprising reason behind its controversial rebrand, critics were still unconvinced. Turns out the ol' "highway visibility" excuse wasn't enough for Uncle Herschel purists who staunchly opposed the new look.

In August 2025, the critics won, and Cracker Barrel's old logo was reinstated. Many internet whingers were filled with a sense of pride, but for some it wasn't enough – they needed to know it would never happen again.

That brings us to today, where CEO Julie Felss Masino has officially stepped down from the company. In her place steps David Deno with the patriotically worded introduction: “Cracker Barrel is a truly iconic American brand, defined by its unique combination of warm country hospitality, timeless appeal, and deep connection with guests across generations.” Translation: Cracker Barrel will return to its traditional, all-American roots.

## Is a bad logo worth expulsion?

In short, no. The nature of design is so subjective that a failed rebrand shouldn't cost someone their career, but clearly the Cracker Barrel controversy was much more than a simple case of bad design. For such an inoffensive, corporate rebrand, the politically charged backlash was unprecedented – a rebrand is always going to ruffle feathers, but no one could've predicted the onslaught directed at Cracker Barrel.

Ever since MAGA media got hold of this story, Masino has had a target on her back, naturally making her the scapegoat of the situation. Was it a poorly executed, uninspired rebrand? I would argue yes, but placing the blame solely on the former CEO is, in my opinion, unwarranted. In some respects, it's likely easier for Masino to go down with the ship, but it highlights a much more sinister issue with branding backlash.

Social media isn't afraid to get personal. In the information age, angry consumers no longer need to shake their fists at a faceless corporation – a quick Google search can tell you exactly who to blame. A failed rebrand is no longer just bad design; it's a cultural attack, a desecration of tradition engineered to makeyouangry. A face to direct one's rage is a new level of validation, especially when it results in the dethroning of a CEO. Yet, that doesn't make it right.
