# The Return of Sail Power: Cargo Ships Are Turning Back to the Wind

## Overview
- After more than a century of reliance on engines, commercial shipping is re‑introducing wind‑assisted propulsion.
- Systems are added to existing vessels and work alongside engines to cut fuel use when wind conditions are favorable.
- Over 100 large merchant ships now carry modern wind‑propulsion equipment, representing >5 million deadweight tons.

## Current Installations
- **Maersk**: planning a 35 m rotor sail on an 8,700‑TEU container ship; tests start 2027 (first rotor sail on a containership).
- **Vale – Sohar Max**: 400,000 dwt ore carrier fitted with five 35 m rotor sails; expected fuel reduction up to 6 %.
- **Idemitsu Tankers**: two VLCCs to receive Norsepower rotor sails in 2028.
- **LNG carriers**: joint study of a 174,000 m³ vessel equipped with BAR Technologies’ WindWings.
- **Neoliner Origin**: 136 m ro‑ro ship with two 76 m carbon‑fiber masts (≈3,000 m² sail area) for primary propulsion.
- **Airbus ro‑ro concept**: combines wind propulsion, alternative fuels, and optimized routing for trans‑Atlantic service.
- **eSAIL & suction sails**: Maersk Tankers deploying eSAIL on MR tankers; Atlantic Orchard fitted with four 26 m suction sails.

## Technology Types
- **Rotor sails**: vertical cylinders that spin, creating lift via the Magnus effect.
- **Rigid wings/foils**: aircraft‑style wings mounted vertically.
- **Suction‑based eSAIL**: uses suction to increase aerodynamic lift.
- **Soft‑sail systems**: flexible fabric sails controlled automatically.
- **Hybrid rigs**: combinations of rigid and soft elements.

## Integration & Automation
- Systems are highly automated, adjusting to wind speed, direction, vessel speed, and heading.
- Weather‑routing software helps capture optimal wind without major schedule changes.
- Norway’s WINTEGRATE program unites shipbuilders, classification societies, and technology firms to embed wind propulsion with engines, batteries, and power‑management systems.

## Economic & Environmental Impact
- Wind is a free energy source, offering a decarbonisation path amid expensive low‑carbon fuels.
- Retrofit projects typically achieve single‑digit to low double‑digit fuel savings; purpose‑built ships can attain higher reductions.
- Even modest percentage savings translate into large absolute fuel and emission cuts for big vessels.

## Challenges & Limitations
- Wind variability and the need for deck space.
- Structural demands: resistance to heavy weather, corrosion, and cargo‑handling operations.
- Height restrictions from bridges, cranes, and ports.
- Route suitability varies; some corridors are more wind‑friendly than others.

## Regulatory Landscape
- IMO is drafting interim safety guidelines for wind‑assisted systems, expected later this decade.
- Classification societies are developing rules; shipyards are gaining installation experience; manufacturers are scaling production.

## Outlook
- Wind propulsion will not replace engines but will become a complementary component of the propulsion mix.
- Growing operational experience, integrated design approaches, and regulatory progress suggest wind‑assisted technology is moving from niche experiments toward mainstream commercial shipping.
