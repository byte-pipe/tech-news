# After being sidelined, Boeing's Starliner to get starring role in NASA's spaceflight plans

## Key developments
- NASA plans to order two additional crewed Starliner missions from Boeing as early as next week.  
- The agency will allocate funds to fix Starliner’s thruster problems and to certify a new launch vehicle, since the Atlas V rocket is being retired.  
- A competition for the replacement launcher will likely involve United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan and Blue Origin’s New Glenn.

## Context: SpaceX’s Crew Dragon retirement
- SpaceX has signaled it will retire Crew Dragon by 2030, focusing on the larger Starship and its satellite constellations.  
- In a recent podcast, COO Gwynne Shotwell declined to commit to extending Dragon’s service and suggested Boeing should step up.  
- NASA has paid roughly $3.1 billion to SpaceX and $5.1 billion to Boeing for the Commercial Crew program; Dragon has completed 13 successful crewed ISS flights.

## Starliner’s troubled history
- Uncrewed test flights in 2019 and 2022 suffered multiple issues; the June 2024 crewed test flight experienced severe thruster failures, nearly endangering astronauts Butch Wilmore and Suni Williams.  
- NASA classified the 2024 flight as a “Type A” mishap and returned the crew to Earth aboard a Dragon capsule.  
- An internal review blamed both design/engineering flaws and poor decision‑making/leadership at Boeing and NASA.  
- Boeing has already absorbed over $2 billion in charges related to Starliner delays.

## NASA’s rationale for supporting Starliner
- With Dragon’s future uncertain, NASA needs a second crew‑transport capability beyond 2030 to support low‑Earth‑orbit activities, including private space stations that will replace the ISS.  
- Maintaining Starliner provides a safety net for commercial station operators, though the lack of competition may affect pricing.  
- Boeing’s lobbying influence and existing investment also factor into NASA’s decision to share development costs.

## Potential impact on Blue Origin
- Blue Origin is developing a crewed “Space Vehicle” for launch on its New Glenn rocket, but it remains years and billions of dollars away from operational status.  
- If Starliner were launched on New Glenn, it would give Blue Origin a certified human‑flight launch vehicle, reducing its own certification burden.  
- Shared infrastructure (crew launch arm, etc.) could benefit both Boeing’s Starliner and Blue Origin’s future crew vehicle.  

## Outlook
- NASA’s financial support and launch‑vehicle certification efforts aim to keep Starliner viable as a second U.S. crew transport option after Dragon’s eventual retirement.  
- The upcoming launch‑vehicle competition and possible collaborations with Blue Origin will shape the commercial crew landscape for the 2030s.
