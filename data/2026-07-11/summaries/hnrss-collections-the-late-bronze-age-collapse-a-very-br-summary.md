## Evidence and Methodology
- **Archaeological focus:** Site destruction identified by demolition of key buildings or thin ash/rubble layers.  
- **Epigraphic fragments:** Scattered inscriptions supplement the archaeological record but are limited.  
- **Interpretive limits:** Archaeology shows *what* happened more readily than *why*; precise dating is often uncertain.

## The (Partial?) Collapse
- Chronology: c. 1220 BC – c. 1170 BC, observed as a series of destruction events.  
- The “wave” model (Aegean → Anatolia → Levant → Egypt) is useful but oversimplified; many sites escape destruction, some decline slowly, and some urban centers collapse while surrounding settlements persist.  
- Example: Tiryns loses its political core, yet the surrounding urban area declines later.

## Late Bronze Age Context (c. 1500 BC – c. 1200 BC)
- **Concert of Powers:** Interconnected state systems in Mesopotamia, Syria, Anatolia, and Egypt with diplomatic, economic, and cultural ties.  
- **Borders:** Fuzzy; peripheral territories often functioned as tributary kingdoms with loose central control.  
- **Key polities:**  
  - Hittite Empire (Anatolia)  
  - Assyrian Empire (northern Mesopotamia)  
  - Kassite Babylon (southern Mesopotamia)  
  - New Kingdom Egypt (Egypt and southern Levant)  
- **Economic complexity:** Large armies required long‑distance bronze trade (copper + tin) and other high‑value goods, creating mutual dependence among the powers.

## The Aegean Periphery
- Miniature state structures in Greece and Crete with palatial centers (e.g., Knossos, Tiryns).  
- Cyprus acted largely independently but was integrated into the trade network.  
- Visual comparison: entire Greek/Minoan urban cores could fit within a single Near Eastern monumental complex such as Karnak.

## Timeline of Collapse (Simplified)
1. **c. 1220 BC:** Early instability in Mycenaean palace states; scattered destructions (e.g., Thebes) and increased fortifications suggest growing trouble.  
2. **c. 1200 – 1180 BC:** Collapse or destruction of most Mycenaean palace centers across Greece.  
3. Subsequent phases (not fully detailed in the excerpt) involve destruction waves moving through Anatolia, the Levant, and finally Egypt.
