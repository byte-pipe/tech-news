# Crypto in 2026: Oh, This is the Bad Place  

## The Bad Place Analogy  
- The author likens today’s crypto‑driven financial chaos to “The Bad Place” from *The Good Place*: a world of absurd, self‑reinforcing horrors that feel normal.  
- Examples: a presidential memecoin, federally licensed bets on U.S. military assassinations, and a shadow dollar system moving global poor’s savings to opaque firms.  

## Markets vs. Crypto Instruments  
- Traditional markets are price‑discovery mechanisms that reflect external realities (e.g., wheat, corporate cash flows, interest‑rate expectations).  
- Crypto assets lack this epistemic value:  
  - Bitcoin’s price only reflects Bitcoin itself.  
  - Meme‑coins price only the belief of selling to a “greater fool.”  
  - Event‑contracts (e.g., Iran‑strike) price only insider trading activity.  
- Because these prices convey no useful information about the real world, they erode public trust in markets.  

## The Narrow Legitimate Use  
- Crypto can provide censorship‑resistant payment rails for dissidents, people under capital controls, or those fleeing oppressive banking systems.  
- This humanitarian niche is real but extremely limited and does **not** justify the broader speculative and gambling ecosystem.  

## Policy Failure: Retail Targeting of High‑Risk Instruments  
- U.S. finance historically confines high‑risk products to sophisticated, capitalized participants via regulated channels.  
- The crypto industry deliberately bypasses these channels, offering the same products directly to unsophisticated retail customers.  
- This choice—prioritizing “sucker farming” over legitimate aggregation, saving, or hedging—structures the entire critique.  

## The Casino Pipeline  
- Crypto serves as a high‑throughput onboarding funnel for retail gambling:  
  - Social‑media exposure → easy app onboarding (e.g., Coinbase).  
  - Purchase of low‑cost meme tokens (e.g., CumRocket).  
  - Watching volatile price swings creates thrill and habit formation.  
  - Subsequent steps lead users into prediction markets, leveraged tokens, and stablecoin “savings” products.  

## Prediction Markets and Event Contracts  
- Federally licensed exchanges now list contracts betting on extrajudicial actions (e.g., U.S. assassinations).  
- Heavy order flow originates from accounts embedded within the “kill chain,” turning geopolitical violence into tradable speculation.  

## Dollar‑Denominated Stablecoins & Monetary Sovereignty  
- New statutes legitimize a “shadow dollar” system: stablecoins pegged to the U.S. dollar but issued by private, opaque entities.  
- Savings of the global poor are funneled into these stablecoins, effectively transferring monetary sovereignty to a handful of firms.  

## The Political‑Economic Defense Machine  
- A coalition of industry lobbyists, sympathetic regulators, and financial‑tech allies defends the status quo:  
  - Argues that crypto innovation drives competition and financial inclusion.  
  - Downplays systemic risk by labeling participants as “eligible contract participants.”  
  - Uses the humanitarian narrative to mask profit‑driven retail exploitation.  

## Policy Recommendations  
- Re‑classify crypto‑based speculative products as gambling and subject them to existing gambling regulations.  
- Require that any high‑risk derivative or event contract be traded only with qualified institutional counterparties.  
- Impose strict transparency and capital‑adequacy requirements on stablecoin issuers, treating them as de‑ facto banks.  
- Preserve the narrow humanitarian use case by creating a regulated, permissioned layer for censorship‑resistant payments, isolated from retail speculation.  
- Educate the public about the self‑referential nature of crypto price signals to restore trust in genuine markets.
