# Energy minister plans 'nuclear renaissance' with up to 10 reactors built by 2040

## Main objectives of the strategy
- Double Canada’s electricity‑grid capacity by 2050 using low‑carbon baseload power.  
- Position nuclear energy as essential to a “low‑carbon economy” and to Canada’s ambition to become an energy superpower.  
- Expand domestic nuclear employment from roughly 90,000 to over 180,000 jobs.  

## Timeline and construction plans
- Build up to 10 new reactors over the next 15 years.  
- Start construction on two large‑scale reactors by 2035.  
- Have five additional reactors planned or under development by 2040.  
- Ensure at least one reactor outside Ontario is under construction by 2035.  
- Develop a Canadian‑made micro‑reactor by 2035 and deploy it to a remote community in the late 2030s.  
- Proposed small modular reactor (SMR) at Darlington, Ontario, would be the first G7 SMR, up to 300 MW per unit.  

## Economic and job impact
- Projected cost for the reactor program exceeds $100 billion.  
- Funding sources may include the Canadian Infrastructure Bank and the Canada Growth Fund; the strategy does not specify a final financing plan.  
- Expected to double sector employment, creating tens of thousands of new jobs.  

## Export and geopolitical goals
- Promote Canadian CANDU reactors in at least four new international markets by 2040 and engage six‑to‑10 new nuclear entrant markets over 15 years.  
- Use reactor exports to forge long‑term commercial and diplomatic partnerships, supporting broader foreign‑policy objectives.  
- If CANDU sales lag, consider boosting domestic uranium enrichment to supply other reactor types.  

## Funding and cost considerations
- Total estimated cost: > $100 billion.  
- Possible financing through federal infrastructure and growth funds; no detailed payment plan disclosed.  

## Political reactions and challenges
- Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre questioned the plan’s effectiveness, emphasizing the need for tangible results over announcements.  
- Opposition to “anti‑development” laws and calls to shift nuclear impact assessments to the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission.  
- Consultation on regulatory changes extended after pushback from environmental and Indigenous groups.  

## Additional notes
- Prime Minister Mark Carney was not involved in drafting the strategy due to an ethics screen; his holdings in Brookfield (which co‑owns a competing reactor model) are held in a blind trust.  
- The strategy underscores Canada’s aim to reduce reliance on Russian enriched uranium by leveraging CANDU’s natural‑uranium design.
