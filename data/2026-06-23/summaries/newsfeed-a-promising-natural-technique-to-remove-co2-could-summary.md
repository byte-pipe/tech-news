# A promising natural technique to remove CO2 could backfire | New Scientist

# Summary of “A promising natural technique to remove CO₂ could backfire”

## Background
- Large investments have been made in cultivating seaweed (kelp) to capture atmospheric CO₂ as part of carbon‑dioxide removal (CDR) strategies.
- Start‑ups such as Running Tide and Kelp Blue aimed to grow seaweed on floating structures and either sink it to the deep ocean or use it as fertilizer, claiming potential offsets of hundreds of millions of tonnes of CO₂ per year.
- Seaweed absorbs dissolved CO₂ during photosynthesis, but most of its carbon is later released back to the ocean when the biomass is decomposed.

## First Study (University of Bern)
- Modeled cultivation of 20 billion t of seaweed annually within 200 nm of coastlines.
- Findings:
  - Rapid depletion of nitrogen, phosphorus, and iron in surface waters.
  - After 25 years, seaweed growth would decline by ~95 %.
  - Global phytoplankton productivity could drop by up to 8 %, reducing the ocean’s natural carbon sink.
  - In most locations seaweed would *increase* atmospheric CO₂ (≈0.5 t CO₂ emitted per tonne of seaweed carbon grown).
  - Only very limited regions (≈0.05 % of the ocean, e.g., patches off Senegal and southern Australia) could support seaweed without major phytoplankton loss, but these areas are too small to achieve gigatonne‑scale removal.

## Second Study (UK National Oceanography Centre)
- Explored iron fertilisation of seaweed farms to boost growth.
- Findings:
  - Potential removal of up to 40 billion t CO₂ yr⁻¹, but would halve global plankton abundance.
  - Required infrastructure would cover ~14 % of the ocean surface, mainly in nutrient‑rich but stormy Southern Ocean and northern Pacific/Atlantic.
  - Without iron fertilisation, seaweed CDR would be insufficient, potentially adding ~700 million t CO₂ yr⁻¹ to the atmosphere.

## Ecological and Practical Implications
- Large‑scale seaweed cultivation could “backfire” by starving surface ecosystems of essential nutrients, disrupting food webs and fisheries.
- The net climate benefit is highly location‑dependent and limited; most of the ocean cannot support the scale needed for meaningful CO₂ removal.
- Effective CDR must account for trade‑offs with phytoplankton productivity and broader marine ecosystem health.
