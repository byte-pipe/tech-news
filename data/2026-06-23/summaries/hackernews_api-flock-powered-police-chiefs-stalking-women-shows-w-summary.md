# Flock-Powered Police Chiefs Stalking Women Shows Why Warrants Are Needed

## Overview
- Article by IPVM Team (June 22 2026) examines misuse of the Flock license‑plate reader (LPR) system by police chiefs.
- Argues that requiring a warrant before accessing LPR data is essential to prevent stalking and other abuses.

## Holiday Hills Police Chief Case
- Chief (also part‑time officer at Prairie Grove PD) arrested June 18 2026; charged with two counts of official misconduct (Class 3 felony).
- Used Flock LPR and the Illinois State Police LEADS database to track six personal contacts, including three former romantic partners.
- Ran an ex‑girlfriend’s ex‑boyfriend’s plate 140 times (protective‑order petition cites 178 searches, 86 off‑duty).
- Left a threatening voicemail; protective order denied in February 2026; arrest followed four months later.
- Village of Holiday Hills said it was “surprised” by the charges.

## Nationwide Pattern of Abuse
- Institute for Justice identified at least 18 similar cases by mid‑2026, likely an undercount.
- Notable examples:
  - **Braselton, GA** police chief arrested November 2025 after a GBI audit revealed abuse.
  - **Jerome County, ID** sheriff queried his wife’s plate more than 700 times in three months, labeling each search a “test.”
  - **Sedgwick, KS** police chief logged 164 queries on an ex‑girlfriend and 64 on her new boyfriend before resigning.
- Lower‑rank officers also implicated:
  - Milwaukee officer tracked a partner and her ex over 100 times; abuse surfaced via a third‑party website.
  - Costa Mesa, CA officer continued using Flock to locate his mistress after being placed on administrative leave.

## Flock’s Public Statements vs. Reality
- Chief Communications Officer Josh Thomas (company video) claims the system tracks only vehicles, not people.
- Chief Legal Officer Dan Haley (radio interview) said misuse “very rarely… they use it to figure out where an ex‑girlfriend is,” then called that “the most common thing.”
- Haley also noted that license plates are required to “correlate to ownership of that vehicle,” tying each read to a specific individual.
- The juxtaposition of these statements undermines Flock’s denial that the technology enables personal tracking.

## Why Warrants Are Needed
- Police chiefs set policy and supervise officers; misuse at the highest rank shows internal controls are insufficient.
- Romantic jealousy provides a strong motive; LPR offers real‑time vehicle location without prior judicial authorization.
- ACLU, EFF, and Institute for Justice support warrant‑based LPR use, emphasizing the lack of pre‑use oversight in current practice.
- Existing exigent‑circumstances exception already permits warrantless access in genuine emergencies; the proposed warrant requirement targets routine, passive queries of stored LPR data—the category in which all documented stalking cases fall.

## Legal Precedents for Comparable Technologies
- GPS trackers on vehicles require a warrant (United States v. Jones, 2012).
- Historical cell‑phone location data requires a warrant (Carpenter v. United States, 2018).
- Wiretaps require a warrant under Title III of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act (1968).
- Cell‑site simulators are subject to DOJ policy requiring a warrant (policy, not law).
- Flock LPR queries are currently accessible without any judicial authorization.

## Outlook
- Haley acknowledged that courts may eventually require warrants for LPR use, citing a recent “Norfolk decision.”
- Documented abuses by chiefs illustrate why such a requirement is imminent and necessary.
