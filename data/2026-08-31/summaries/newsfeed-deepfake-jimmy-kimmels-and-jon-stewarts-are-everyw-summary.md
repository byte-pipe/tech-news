# Deepfake Jimmy Kimmels and Jon Stewarts are everywhere

## Overview
- AI‑generated deepfakes of late‑night hosts such as Jimmy Kimmel and Jon Stewart are rapidly spreading across social media platforms.  
- These videos are easier to produce than other celebrity deepfakes because hosts typically sit or stand in one place and extensive high‑quality audio archives are publicly available.

## Why Audiences Are Misled
- The clips appear alongside genuine Kimmel and Stewart content, giving them built‑in context and trust.  
- Common tell‑tale signs—odd gestures, scripted‑style monologue, voice overlaid on news footage—are often missed by viewers.  
- Some videos garner hundreds of thousands of views and receive comments that treat them as authentic, e.g., “Thank you Jimmy for speaking the truth.”

## Political and Legal Implications
- Kimmel’s contentious relationship with the Trump administration makes his deepfakes especially risky.  
- Fake anti‑Trump clips could reinforce the administration’s stance during the FCC‑ABC legal battle over First‑Amendment rights.  
- Both Democratic‑leaning and Republican‑leaning hosts are targeted, though left‑leaning deepfakes appear more frequently.

## Technical Ease
- A single image and a short audio sample can be animated into a convincing video, as explained by Dartmouth professor Hany Farid.  
- The format aligns with how many Americans receive news and form opinions through late‑night shows.

## Motivations Behind Creation
- Possible ideological agendas or pure click‑bait for ad revenue.  
- Creators remain largely anonymous and did not respond to NPR’s inquiries.

## Platform Responses
- TikTok, YouTube (Google), and Meta (Instagram/Facebook) state they require AI‑disclosure labels and employ likeness‑detection and moderation tools.  
- Enforcement is inconsistent; several deepfakes remain online without labels for weeks or months.

## Audience Demographics
- Roughly a quarter of Americans still watch late‑night talk shows, with most preferring online clips over linear TV.  
- Viewers are predominantly Democrats, which may explain the higher prevalence of left‑leaning deepfakes.

## Notable Examples
- Kimmel “commenting” on President Trump falling asleep and on an alleged attack on Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El‑Sayed.  
- Jon Stewart deepfake about Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni that has persisted on TikTok for over two months without an AI label.

## Summary
- AI‑generated deepfakes of late‑night hosts pose a distinct disinformation threat due to their ease of creation, trusted contextual framing, and influence on public opinion.  
- Current platform policies are evolving, but the rapid spread of such content challenges media literacy, moderation capabilities, and existing regulatory frameworks.
