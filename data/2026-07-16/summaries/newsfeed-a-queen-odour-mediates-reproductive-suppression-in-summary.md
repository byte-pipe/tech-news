# A queen odour mediates reproductive suppression in a eusocial mammal

## Abstract summary
- Naked mole‑rats exhibit extreme eusociality with a single breeding queen; other females are infertile.  
- The study identifies isopropyl myristate (IPM), a low‑volatility ester, as a queen‑enriched chemical absent in non‑breeders.  
- IPM is detected by peripheral and central olfactory neurons, triggers avoidance in high‑ranking individuals, and alters prolactin and progesterone levels to suppress reproduction.  
- Adding IPM daily to a queenless colony blocks queen succession; removing it induces aggression and competition.  
- IPM is also present in breeding females of other Fukomys species, but at lower concentrations, matching their less pronounced reproductive skew.  
- The findings link chemical reproductive control in mammals to similar mechanisms in eusocial insects.

## Odour‑based social recognition
- Naked mole‑rats spend more time sniffing unfamiliar conspecifics than colony mates, indicating olfactory discrimination of social novelty.  
- Chemical ablation of olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) with methimazole eliminates this discrimination, abolishes attraction to food odour, and temporarily reduces body mass.  
- OSN loss is confirmed by immunohistochemistry showing near‑complete depletion of mature (OMP⁺) and immature (STMN1⁺) neurons within 72 h.  
- Methimazole‑treated animals display markedly reduced aggression toward unfamiliar high‑ranking strangers, demonstrating that olfaction underlies both social recognition and aggressive interactions.

## Identification of the queen‑derived chemical
- Chemical analysis of vaginal secretions reveals that the long‑chain ester isopropyl myristate is highly enriched in queens and peaks during reproductive readiness.  
- IPM is virtually absent from non‑breeding females and males.  
- Gas‑chromatography–mass‑spectrometry and quantitative assays confirm its colony‑specific concentration gradient.

## Olfactory detection and neural response
- Electrophysiological recordings show that both peripheral olfactory epithelium and central olfactory bulb neurons respond selectively to IPM.  
- Behavioral assays demonstrate avoidance of IPM‑treated substrates by high‑ranking individuals, indicating its role as a repellent cue.

## Neuroendocrine effects on non‑breeders
- Exposure of non‑breeding females to IPM reduces circulating prolactin and progesterone, hormones known to support reproductive activation.  
- Hormonal changes correlate with suppressed ovarian activity and maintenance of infertility.

## Manipulation of colony dynamics
- Daily application of synthetic IPM to a queenless colony prevents any female from assuming the queen role, maintaining social stability.  
- Withdrawal of IPM from the same colony triggers aggressive contests and rapid onset of reproductive competition among females.  

## Comparative relevance across Fukomys species
- IPM is detected in breeding females of several related mole‑rat species, but at lower concentrations than in naked mole‑rats.  
- The gradient of IPM levels parallels the degree of reproductive skew across species, suggesting a conserved chemical mechanism modulating eusocial hierarchy.

## Evolutionary implications
- The discovery of a queen‑specific pheromone in a mammal provides a mechanistic link between insect and mammalian eusociality.  
- Chemical reproductive suppression may represent a convergent solution to maintaining cooperative societies with extreme division of labor.
