# The new flywheel for building a predictable LinkedIn inbound engine

Don’t run organic and paid LinkedIn in SILOs. Here’s how to build a compounding flywheel, where both strengthen each other.

Patrick Cumming

Jun 5, 2026

•

5 min read

Hey — Patrick here from Ad Juice!

Welcome to this week’s edition ofThe Squeeze, the newsletter created for B2B marketing operators looking for fresh LinkedIn Ads tactics, proven on real ad accounts.

When I was at KlientBoost, we built one of the most memorable LinkedIn programs in the agency space.

Almost every client I’ve onboarded since starting AdJuice has told me: “I saw what you did at KlientBoost, can you run that strategy for us?”

That led me to very quickly pivoting from a pure LinkedIn Ads agency, to a LinkedIn growth agency that supports organic thought leadership with paid amplification.

The reason our program was so successful is fairly simple: we built the organic and paid strategy together, not separately.

We didn’t use a funnel, we used a flywheel that looked like this:

Create organic LinkedIn content based on data-driven insights → Amplify organic content to tight ICP audiences through LinkedIn Ads → Use in-platform LinkedIn Ads signals to learn which content resonates most with our ICP → Apply those insights to new organic content → Repeat the cycle every month

It worked so well, by my last month there we were spending ~$250 on a qualified booked call and had reduced CAC to ~$1,000. Those are some of the most efficient numbers I’ve seen in my career.

The reality is that the most effective B2Bs in 2026 won’t run those channels separately anymore. They’ll run them as a unified program, where both channels compound the performance of the other through shared insights and goals.

Let’s dive into how to make that strategy work.

## Step 1 - Write for your ICP first

I’m seeing many great marketers starting to apply the new LinkedIn flywheel with great results. But, there’s one problem I see with how many of them approach implementing it. They’ll say: use organic to test what works, then promote what’s already working on organic.

This sounds good in principle, but, if I’d have followed that approach at KlientBoost, we’d have ended up only promoting personal stories and job updates. Those posts typically generate the most engagement, but it’s rarely from ICP prospects or buyers. That content is still nice and adds some variety and personality, but it's the wrong content to promote since it won’t build mental availability or generate pipeline.

Instead, we operated with a different mindset: write all of our organic content for our ICP, then put paid spend behind it, regardless of how well it does in organic feeds.

The hypothesis?

LinkedIn’s algorithm is constantly changing. There’s no guarantee it’s pushing content to ICPs. If we put paid spend behind posts written for our ICP, then use targeting filters to ensure it reaches them, posts that don’t get reach and engagement via organic will still perform well on paid when the right people see them.

Exactly what we predicted would happen, happened.

Posts that didn’t do well on organic performed significantly better on paid. Posts that had performed well on organic, didn’t perform as well on paid.

Here’s a little tip for boosting audience penetration and frequency that’s working for clients right now:

1. Build two campaigns for your thought leader content:Campaign 1: Set to brand awarenessCampaign 2: Set to engagement
2. Campaign 1: Set to brand awareness
3. Campaign 2: Set to engagement
4. Use the exact same ads and audiences
5. Build a retargeting list of people who engage with ads
6. Exclude ad engagers from your awareness campaign

LinkedIn tends to prioritise active engagers, even with awareness objective campaigns. Running this play forces all the active engagers into the same campaign, so the awareness campaign can reach prospects who are less likely to engage, but would still buy from you.

## Step 3 - Learn what resonates

This stage is the main difference between how I approach the flywheel vs many other marketers in the space.

Instead of using reach and engagement on organic content to tell me what’s “working,” I use signals from within LinkedIn Ads that tell me how my exact ICP responds to different pieces of content.

Typically, I’ll use the following metrics as signals to understand what to do more of, and what to do less of:

* Leading indicatorsLanding page clicks: this is still the strongest indicator of intent that isn’t direct pipeline. Anecdotally, I’ve also seen a strong correlation between ads with higher landing page CTRs and more attributed pipeline.Dwell time: this tells us how long readers spend looking at an ad. More dwell time = more mental availability = more pipeline in the long run.
* Landing page clicks: this is still the strongest indicator of intent that isn’t direct pipeline. Anecdotally, I’ve also seen a strong correlation between ads with higher landing page CTRs and more attributed pipeline.
* Dwell time: this tells us how long readers spend looking at an ad. More dwell time = more mental availability = more pipeline in the long run.
* Lagging indicatorsPipeline (Booked Calls, SQLs, Opps): Which posts consistently have more attributed pipeline than others?CAC: Which posts have the lowest in-platform customer acquisition cost (CAC) vs others?
* Pipeline (Booked Calls, SQLs, Opps): Which posts consistently have more attributed pipeline than others?
* CAC: Which posts have the lowest in-platform customer acquisition cost (CAC) vs others?

Now, in-platform metrics aren’t perfect. Attribution still misses some of the impact your posts are having. Landing page clicks and dwell time don’t guarantee an ad is building mental availability. But, there is a strong correlation. These are directional signals to know what your ICP wants more of and what they want less of.

## Step 4 - Apply the insights

Now that you know which organic posts resonate, and which ones don’t, when you put them in front of your exact ICP, you can build out a plan for scaling and evolving your content plan based on those insights.

It’s easy to overcomplicate this stage, with some marketers enjoying building out complex insight tracking spreadsheets to manage the process.

I think that’s OTT and unnecessary. You’re looking for direction, not exact science.

Here’s the main 2 things I’m thinking about as I’m building the content scaling plan:

1. Themes: Are there any pains, gains, or jobs to be done that resonate more than others? Are there any that get no traction? Now I can make the decision to ideate new angles for themes that are resonating and reduce or eliminate themes that aren’t.
2. Formats: Do text-only posts perform better than image posts? Do cheatsheets perform better than carousels? Are there specific hook formats that perform better than others? Are there specific post structures that perform better than others? Are there specific offers that get more engagement than others? These are useful signals in knowing what to scale. If your text-only posts generate the most inbound, for example, you might find you don’t need to spend as much effort on complex carousels that generate less interest.

At this stage, we’ll also move into repeating the process.

Alongside in-platform signals, we’ll pull the most recent discovery call analysis to identify if there are any new emerging trends, pains, gains, or jobs to be done we need to add into the posting rotation.

From there, the cycle repeats itself. With results compounding each month to the next.

## Wrap up

Okay, let’s run through a quick recap of the key takeaways:

* Paid & organic LinkedIn no longer work in SILOs, the most efficient B2Bs run them as a compounding flywheel.
* Don’t use organic reach and engagement alone as the signal for what to promote on LinkedIn Ads. Write for your ICP, then let tight targeting be your guide, it might surprise you to learn posts that bomb on organic, perform well on paid.
* The new flywheel works like this: write organic content for your ICP → amplify on paid → collect performance insights in LinkedIn Ads → scale what resonates and generates pipeline → repeat

Remember: the funnel looks neat and tidy in a slide deck. But it’s not how buyers buy. Use the flywheel to meet buyers where they’re at, not where you want them to be.
