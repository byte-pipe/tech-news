# DoorDash's $17B Acquisition of Instacart Points to a World Without Employees

### It’s not just AI eliminating the idea of jobs. It’s the gig economy.

Today, news broke that food delivery company DoorDash bought its rival Instacart for a reported $17 billion. The deal is slated to close in early 2028, which means the two companies understand it is going to receive significant antitrust scrutiny, even under the Trump administration. According to Reuters this morning, review islikelyto fall under the ambit of the Federal Trade Commission, which separately launched aninquirythis week into an array of companies — including both DoorDash and Instacart — for alleged surveillance pricing. And there’s a reason the acquisition may face tough regulatory hurdles, which I’m going to get into.

But the more important question involved in this merger is what it says about how we understand the nature of work, and how the goal is to unravel a social contract we put together in the late 19th century about our relationship with big business. Both Instacart and DoorDash are leaders in the “gig economy,” both in terms of shaping the legal terms and in encouraging corporate America elsewhere to try and turn their workforce into temporary or gig workers, which is akin to “piece work” during early industrialization.

We’ll start with the merger. Here’sBloomberg:

DoorDash Inc. agreed to buy rival Instacart in a deal that values the company at about $17 billion, the latest sign of consolidation in an increasingly competitive delivery market where Walmart Inc. and Amazon.com Inc. loom large.

For DoorDash, which is already the leading restaurant delivery app in the US, buying Instacart will boost its grocery delivery segment, which co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Tony Xu recently described as the fastest-growing part of the business. The merger would likely help DoorDash tap older users, who tend to use Instacart more to order groceries, analysts from TD Cowen wrote Thursday.

The deal would also better position DoorDash to compete with retail giants like Walmart and Amazon, which have made online grocery orders a bigger priority and rely on their own apps.

This combination would significantly reshape the delivery space, which is a big part of how American consumers interact with business. And it met with vehement and instant political opposition. Here’s the Mayor of New York City:

### What Is DoorDash?

DoorDash is the dominant platform-to-consumer delivery service for restaurants in the United States, with an estimated67% shareof the domestic market by monthly sales. At this point, DoorDash and Uber Eats are really the only two competitors here, comprising about90%of the market, after DoorDashacquiredCaviar in 2019 and Uber EatsacquiredPostmates in 2020. (Uber Eats also made a play for Grubhub in 2019, but abandoned it, and now Grubhub is bottoming out.) And that’s just the domestic restaurant delivery market. DoorDash has also expanded internationally, through itsacquisitionsof Wolt, and last year’s $3.7 billionacquisitionof Deliveroo.

I live in San Francisco where all of these companies started, working on the city council as part of the early regulatory response to insulate restaurants from commissions that werepushing 30%. Suddenly I was receiving emails from two lobbyists instead of four, because the consolidation had actually sapped the market for lobbyists.

The consolidation coincided with the intervening COVID-19 pandemic, which super-charged platform-to-consumer delivery services. The 2020 boom in online delivery made sense, as delivery platforms transformed overnight into essential services, and delivery drivers becamede factofront line workers. And that accelerated adoption of online food delivery has hardly waned. Today, the percentage of Americans who dine out with regularity continues to fall, while the percentage of Americans who regularly use platforms like DoorDash and Instacart has steadilyincreased.

So that’s DoorDash. What about its dance partner, Instacart?

### What Is Instacart?

Instacart is the most dominant third party grocery delivery service in the country, with an estimated share of about57.7%. Instacart’s domination of the third party grocery delivery marketincludesover 2,200 brands and 100,000 grocery stores reaching 98% of homes in the country. And according to their recent SEC quarterlyfiling, they’re active in the restaurant delivery space, too, meaning they are a direct, if nascent, competitor to the entity acquiring them.

In other words, this combination is more than a merger of compliments, with shared back-end operations, it’s also a merger between direct competitors across groceries, restaurants, and delivery drivers.

### The Case for the Merger

Of course I’m skeptical of this merger, but is there a caseforthe combination? Well, Instacart and DoorDash would argue they are little fish in a big pond, and dispute the high market shares. As to Instacart, their substantial market share is in third party grocery delivery services, which they could claim aren’t a real market. What about something like Walmart or Amazon, which offers direct delivery? A lot of people shop at Walmart, and Walmart can crowd Instacart out of the market with free delivery and other promotions. These would be, according Instacart/DoorDash, real competition.

That said, this counter-argument misses a key problem. Restaurants and independent grocers must use third party delivery services, they simply don’t have the scale or brand that Kroger, Amazon, or Walmart have. So at least for them, third party delivery is a real market. And these independents have already beencomplainingabout having to fork over their competitively sensitive information and trade secrets.

The consumer side is another area to examine; both DoorDash and Instacart are under a new microscope forpotentialsurveillance pricing, as of just yesterday. If their data scales the ability to personalize pricing, then we might see the kind of direct evidence for market power that is anathema to a merger.

What makes this merger so interesting is that the harms are more likely to affect suppliers, namely restaurants, drivers and delivery workers, and grocery stories. And in this respect, it is a story of the combined firm’s buyer power, and it is alaborstory. Historically, labor has not really been part of the antitrust legal approach, because labor law and antitrust have existed in separate legal arenas. But as big corporations have taken apart the very notion of the employee, throughmisclassificationand the broader phenomenon of the “fissured workplace,” the distinction between antitrust and labor law is collapsing.

### The Rise and Fall of the Employee

To understand what this merger says about how you and I work, consume, or buy, let’s rewind the clock about 125 years. The way we generally think about work, and the relationship between employers and workers, took shape during the sweeping industrialization of the economy in the late 19th and early 20th Century. Older forms of work were characterized by skilled artisans and family farms. From the 1890s to the 1910s, big employers gained control over production, costs and labor – a factory-based model of production – while workers fought for higher wages, shorter hours, and a voice in the workplace.

Here’s Woodrow Wilson in 1913describingthe change that happened in most people’s memories:

We have come upon an age when we do not do business in the way in which we used to do business,—when we do not carry on any of the operations of manufacture, sale, transportation, or communication as men used to carry them on. There is a sense in which in our day the individual has been submerged. In most parts of our country men work, not for themselves, not as partners in the old way in which they used to work, but generally as employees—in a higher or lower grade—of great corporations. There was a time when corporations played a very minor part in our business affairs, but now they play the chief part, and most men are the servants of corporations.

It would be too simple to characterize the old order as fundamentally independent and egalitarian — there was no shortage of exploitation taking place among farm workers — but generally speaking workers had much more control over their productive labor. The rise of factories led to greater specialization of skills, workplaces became more centralized, and there was a new dependence formed between employers and their workers.

This period also saw the emergence of two bodies of law – antitrust and labor – which developed on mostly separate but parallel tracks, and not without conflict. In 1886, the American Federation of Laborformedto represent some 140,000 skilled tradesmen in their pursuit of better wages and working conditions, and they engaged in strikes and collective bargaining to do so. Meanwhile, lawmakers sought to control corporate power with new laws like the Interstate Commerce Act in 1887, which regulated railroad prices and prohibited discrimination, and the nation’s foundational, enduring antitrust law, the Sherman Act in 1890.

Labor unions and antitrust laws sought to address the abuse of corporate power in different ways. But when Senator John Sherman railed against “a king over the production, transportation, and sale of the necessities of life,” and AFL President Samuel Gompers declared, “You are our employers, not our masters,” it was two sides of the same coin.

But in the early years, enforcers used the Sherman Actagainstorganized labor to restrain labor strikes and boycotts. That friction created a persistent and lasting discord. Even after the Clayton Act of 1914 explicitly exempted the combination of workers from the Sherman Act, courts construed the exemption narrowly and continued toenjoincertain types of boycotts and strikes. Two decades after the Clayton Act, Congress passed the National Labor Relations Act in 1935, amid a depression, militant labor organizing, greater federal intervention in the economy, and collective bargaining emerged as the preferred means of countering employer power and abuses.

In the traditional employer-employee relationship, the fundamental exchange went as follows. Employees received a living wage, or salary, and benefits in return for employer control over the labor of the individual. The conflict between labor and capital, then, was premised on the ability of workers to organize for better pay. This deal was a narrowing of earlier labor goals, such as the Knights of Labor, who wanted sovereignty over production, but it did foster broadly shared wealth.

Since the 1970s, even this narrower deal unraveled, with offshoring, union-busting, immigration policy, and a decline in the minimum wage, among other statutory changes to employee power. Anyone interested in a deeper narrative should go pick upHow Antitrust Failed Workersby Eric Posner, a 2021 book which follows the rise of labor, and its decline beginning in the 1970s. Posner’s broader point was that antitrust was meant originally as a pro-labor doctrine, to prevent concentrations of capital from dominating Americans. Enforcers and judges, however, so perverted the law that we simply created an entirely separate legal regime for industrial labor, forcing large corporations to take responsibility for their employees in a variety of ways.

And Posner argued, as have many over the past decade, that should change. Shortly after publishing it, Posner joined the Biden Antitrust Division, and actually enacted many of the ideas in his book.

Under the Biden administration, enforcers sought to explicitly deal with power in labor markets in a number of ways, seeking tobannon-compete agreements, bringingcriminal chargesagainst employers colluding to wage-fix,blockingthe Penguin/Simon and Schuster on author wage grounds, and successfullychallengingthe Kroger-Albertsons merger on the grounds the combined entity would have more bargaining power over workers and labor unions.

These moves were not just creative, but indicated that the economy has structurally changed such that employers have become big enough to exert market power over wage-setting, even as the definition of the employee had been eroded for decades. Labor law alone, beleaguered by decades of eroded union membership, just could no longer deliver.

### A World Without Employees

And with that, let’s get back to DoorDash’s proposed acquisition of Instacart. Under the hood of this transaction is a new wave of tech- and platform-based gig work, which breaks the traditional employment relationship that has existed for over a century.

Today, many companies use platform-based gig work, where an employer can control every facet of work, while absolving themselves of the obligation to provide a consistent wage and benefits. In theory, workers can choose when they want to work and earn some supplemental income. Inpractice, over 1.1 million Californians, comprising 81% of all app-based gig workers, consider it their main job. And it’s not just delivery and rideshare drivers, everyone fromnursestosoftware engineersare becoming app-based gig workers. By at least one estimate,over halfof the United States workforce could be gig workers by 2027.

The campaign to institutionalize this form of exploitation has been extraordinary. In early 2020, Instacart was foundliablefor worker “misclassification,” in the wake of a California Supreme Court decision, calledDynamex, that established a three-part test for whether an employment relationship was subject to employee protection laws. Later that year, Instacart and DoorDash would end up contributing acombined$83 million to Proposition 22, a California statewide ballot measure that overturned the California Supreme Court and the state legislature’s samecodificationof the three-part test.

Altogether, over$204 millionwas spent by gig companies, including Uber and Lyft, to preserve the independent contractor model. Until this year’s billionaire wealth tax, it was the most expensive political campaign in California history.

The gig-ification of work has had expectedly deleterious effects for worker compensation. A 2022studyfollowing the adoption of Prop 22 found that median take-home earnings were just $6.20 per hour. Because gig workers only get paid for when they are working a job, and not while they are waiting or in transit for a gig, they aren’t paid for their total time, further diminishing what they would make as employees. According to the same 2022 study, the post-Prop 22 wage floor was just $4.10, nearly $10 lower than the state’s minimum wage. California workers receivedlesscompensation than their counterparts in five other metros.

And gig employers, like Lyft and Uber who pull a lion’s share of the weight for their industry counterparts, including DoorDash and Instacart, have threatened capital strikes to keep it that way. In 2024, Uber and Lyftthreatenedto leave Minneapolis over the state’s new minimum wage law, and at that point, I noted the moral question at hand.

### The Real Goal of DoorDash-Instacart

It should be clear at this point that DoorDash and Instacart built their business models on the premise that the traditional obligations to employees could be jettisoned by turning them into gig workers. That extra cost, in the forms of higher wages, liability insurance, health care, and so forth, is profit to the platform as human beings were classified as “independent businesses.”

And now comes the second stage, an important source of scrutiny for regulators reviewing the DoorDash-Instacart deal, which is the roll-up of market power over gig workers, along with the potential extraction from independent restaurants and grocers. The twoplatformscombined“employ” about 9.6 million drivers, but compensation terms vary, and some gig workers engage in “multi-homing” or “multi-apping,” using multiple platforms simultaneously to optimize for better gigs. One analysis of the two platformsrevealsdifferent pay structures and promotional offers between the two platforms, resulting in different overall compensation ($16 on Instacart, $15 on DoorDash) and per-order tips ($5 on Instacart, $3 on DoorDash). There are also special compensation terms unique to grocery shopping, to account forheavy basketsand the time it takes to do the actual shopping. It’s unclear how all of those compensation terms would be consolidated into a single app, but collapsing competition between these two platforms risks reducing the ability of gig workers to “bid” up compensation by pitting utilization of each platform against the other.

These important dynamics aside, the relationship between platforms and workers is a fundamentally new way to understand the social contract. It is not a return to pre-industrial norms, because large corporations didn’t exist then, but they do now. It is something relatively new, having emerged over the past 15 years. Or at least, itcouldbe something new. This path ahead is not pre-determined. And the point of the history lesson on antitrust and labor is that the path forward could and should look like a unity of interests aimed at re-balancing this asymmetric power dynamic.

We’re seeing the signs of it. The new Biden-era antitrust policy on labor, continuedoddlyinnarrow waysby Trump, is a key part of it. Laws have also been introduced in several states and federally not just to prohibit surveillance pricing, but also toprohibitsurveillance wage discrimination. It’s the reflection of a populist form of politics that is appearing at the local level, too, and the Mamdani Administration is a highly visible part of that work. Last month, NYC Deputy Mayor for Economic Justice Julie Su and Department of Consumer and Worker Protection Commission Sam Levine — both former Biden Administration officials — led a$131.5 millionenforcement action against DoorDash. And DoorDash issued its own press release, leading with: “We screwed up.”

There’s another approach that draws on price fixing law, and the interesting wrinkle here is that the push by gig platforms to classify their workers as independent contractors could expose them to liability for fixing the wages of independent competitors. In California, recently-passed Assembly Bill 325prohibitsany use of a common pricing algorithm to coerce the setting of prices on independent businesses. Well, independent contractors are technically independent businesses, a “wage” is the price of labor, and there’s nothing more coercive than a take-or-leave price.

I’ve used the DoorDash-Instacart acquisition to talk about the need for a renewed, dual commitment to antitrust and labor, because this $17 billion proposed acquisition is the next data point in the restructuring of American life. And as gig work expands to other sectors, it’s not hard to see a lot of dead canaries in the coal mine over the past 15 years.
