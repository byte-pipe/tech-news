# LLM Voice – Summary

## Main Argument
- The author feels a growing “LLM‑Voice” in some prose, causing revulsion and an uncanny‑valley reaction.
- Tools that detect AI‑generated text miss the core issue: the human perception of the prose’s tone and authenticity.

## Observations on Recognition
- Recognizing LLM‑Voice is unreliable; language evolves and generational phrasing (e.g., “new salesmotion”) can mimic AI patterns.
- Personal bias (British vs. American usage) also influences detection.

## Recommendations for Writers
- Avoid using LLMs to draft full prose; even with human editing, the AI imprint is hard to erase.
- Using LLMs for outlines may be acceptable, but the final wording should be authored by the writer.
- Declare any AI assistance in acknowledgments for transparency.
- Practice writing by speaking thoughts internally, then transcribing and editing to develop skill and retain personal voice.

## Risks of Over‑Reliance
- Writing serves as a tool for thinking; delegating it to LLMs may also delegate cognition.
- Over‑use could diminish the writer’s ability to clarify and understand complex ideas.

## Exceptions
- **Medical necessity:** Jim Highsmith relies on LLMs to overcome Parkinson’s‑related writing challenges.
- **Translation:** Non‑native speakers may use LLM translation, though the author prefers imperfect human prose.
- **Low‑stakes documents:** Form letters, internal reports, or future documentation intended for LLM consumption can be AI‑generated without concern.

## Notes (Footnotes)
1. Rare cases exist where a fully AI‑drafted piece loses its LLM‑Voice after professional editing.
2. The author’s policy: disclose AI usage; personally, AI is used only as a search tool, not for drafting or structuring.
3. Michael Nygard’s comment: communicating with a process (e.g., automated reports) differs from human‑to‑human communication.
