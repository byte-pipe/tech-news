# Some Great Lakes sturgeon may be 400 years old. Scientists are rethinking how to save them

## Main findings
- New research suggests lake sturgeon in the Great Lakes can live more than 400 years, far exceeding the previous maximum estimate of about 150 years.  
- Scientists used 44 years of capture‑and‑recapture data to model individual growth rates instead of counting fin‑ray growth rings, which become indistinct in very old fish.  
- A notable example: a male caught in 1990 at 117 cm grew only 7 cm over the next 34 years (≈0.21 cm per year), leading to age projections that stretch into centuries.  

## Conservation implications
- Existing recovery plans are roughly a century long, based on the assumption that sturgeon live far shorter lives; the new longevity data may require extending or reshaping these plans.  
- Long lifespans affect decisions about habitat restoration, monitoring frequency, and the timing of release programs.  
- Sturgeon help control invasive quagga mussels and gobies, so their loss has altered the Great Lakes food web.  

## Human impact and recovery efforts
- Overfishing and destruction of spawning habitats eliminated an estimated 99 % of the historic population.  
- Current populations still depend on hatcheries and the release of captive‑reared juveniles to survive.  
- University of Windsor biologist Trevor Pitcher’s team is training members of Georgian Bay and Walpole Island First Nations to raise sturgeon, spreading restoration expertise.  

## Methodology shift
- Traditional age estimation by counting growth rings in fin rays becomes unreliable as growth slows with age.  
- Researchers calculated the amount each fish grew between successive captures and modeled how long it would take to reach adult size, producing century‑scale age estimates.  

## Cultural perspective
- For the Anishinaabe, especially Nipissing First Nation elder Perry McLeod‑Shabogesic, the sturgeon’s extreme longevity makes it a “grandfather fish” and an elder in the natural world.  
- Historically, lakes like Nipissing were teeming with sturgeon; older fishermen described spawning runs so dense that their backs broke the water’s surface.  
- The fish is seen as a being that lives “in between,” embodying a spiritual role beyond ordinary fish.  

## Outlook
- If the 400‑year lifespan is confirmed, conservation timelines may need to extend beyond a single human generation, prompting a fundamental rethink of how to protect and restore this keystone species.  
- Understanding the species’ slow growth and extraordinary longevity is essential for designing effective, long‑term recovery strategies.
