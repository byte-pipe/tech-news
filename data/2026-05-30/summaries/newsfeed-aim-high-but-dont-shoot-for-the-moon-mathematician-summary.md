# Aim high but don't shoot for the moon, mathematicians advise

## Key points
- Mathematicians modelled how people balance ambition and realistic outcomes in uncertain situations.  
- The optimal strategy is to aim above the average reward but stay well below the maximum possible reward.  
- This “moderately high” ambition is mathematically provable as strictly above average and strictly finite.  

## Research approach
- Matt Burgess and colleagues built a statistical model that assigns a satisfaction threshold to each possible outcome.  
- They ran thousands of simulations varying the number of choices, the distribution of good versus bad outcomes, and the effort required for each choice.  
- Results were compared with real‑world data such as university application statistics and US election polls.  

## Findings on biased scenarios
- When most outcomes are mediocre and one outcome is extremely bad (e.g., a rare recession), the model advises being slightly more ambitious than average to avoid the drag of the bad outcome on the mean.  
- When one outcome is extremely good (e.g., a start‑up that either makes $1 billion or nothing), the optimal ambition is slightly less than average, contrary to intuition.  

## Context and limitations
- Individual risk preferences vary; some may prefer stable income over “optimal” but riskier returns.  
- In winner‑takes‑all environments, higher risk‑seeking ambition may be appropriate.  
- The study does not claim a universal prescription, but highlights a mathematically grounded middle ground for ambition.  

## Publication
- The work appears in *Physical Review E* (DOI: 10.1103/dfw8‑vhjk).
