# Code Isn’t Product

## Introduction
- A year ago the author noted AI shifting bottlenecks from coding to helping customers understand, try, and monetize products, and to front‑end discovery of what to build.  
- The current observation is a deeper market confusion between creating code and creating products.

## The 100x Problem
- AI enables shipping roughly 100 times more products or features, boosting engineering throughput.  
- Boards and CEOs see AI as justification for cutting engineering, product, and design teams.  
- Customer attention does **not** scale: prospects have limited bandwidth, buying processes are unchanged, and budgets are static.  
- Positioning becomes critical; the language used to describe a product must match the words actual users use for their problems.  
- Effective discovery—talking directly with customers before code is shipped—is increasingly urgent, yet under‑emphasized, leading to many “dead‑on‑arrival” products.

## Forward‑Deployed Engineer Tell
- Companies are deploying engineers on‑site at enterprise customers to help plan, deploy, and extract value from AI‑enhanced products.  
- This signals a shift toward shipping tools, kits, or platforms rather than finished, problem‑solved products.  
- If a customer needs a full‑time engineer to understand the product’s purpose and fit, the issue lies in product definition, not deployment.  
- Forward‑deployed engineers are costly, hard to hire, and do not scale; they act as a workaround for weak product thinking.

## The Next Bench Syndrome, Revisited
- A rising model promotes solo product engineers who build end‑to‑end solutions without committees, claiming 50 × faster shipping.  
- This works when the engineer’s own workflow, language, and buying behavior match the user’s (“next‑bench” scenario).  
- For broader markets—veterinarians, teachers, firefighters, government buyers—the model fails because user problems, language, success metrics, and procurement processes differ.  
- Over‑generalizing this approach yields technically impressive but commercially weak products.

## What Comes Next
- The next 2‑4 quarters will be painful for product teams: boards push speed, engineering throughput rises, AI IPOs inflate expectations, and short‑term shipping metrics dominate.  
- Most AI‑driven products will fail not due to bad code but because discovery was skipped, positioning was off, pricing was sloppy, deployment barriers were underestimated, and target users were misidentified.  
- The same failure modes have appeared in past technology transitions, only accelerated by AI.  
- When the reckoning arrives, strategic product thinking at the front and strong product marketing at the back will regain value.  
- “Tuition” (the cost of learning these lessons) is about to increase.

## Sound Byte
- AI provides 100 × the code, but it does **not** provide 100 × the customers or revenue.

## Reference
- The term “next‑bench syndrome” was coined by Bill Hewlett at HP, describing how engineers built tools for nearby colleagues, a model that later became a liability in consumer markets.
