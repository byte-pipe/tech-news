# Barbell‑Shaped Product Roles – Rich Mironov

## Classic software product management (1971‑2024)

- Product work was split between a **fuzzy front‑end** (discovery) and **core development** (coding, testing, UX, documentation).  
- Most organisations allocated only ~20 % of effort to discovery, despite evidence that the majority of product failure occurs before any code is written.  
- Core development consumed 60‑100 % of time and was highly visible (roadmaps, tickets, releases).  
- Go‑to‑market activities (targeting, messaging, pricing, sales enablement) received 10‑20 % of a product manager’s time but were essential for revenue; they are often under‑resourced.

## Product management in AI‑first organizations (2027+)

- AI promises to deliver production‑quality code up to 10× faster, shifting the most valuable work to the **ends** of the product lifecycle, forming a “barbell” shape: heavy effort on discovery and go‑to‑market, lighter on core coding.

### The fuzzy front‑end (discovery)

- Pressure will rise to skip discovery because coding becomes cheap and fast.  
- Mironov argues that **speed‑first** is a false promise: buyers are already overloaded with features and pitches.  
- He recommends dedicating **40 %+** of product time to evidence‑based discovery for a few high‑impact revenue bets, discarding the bulk of low‑value backlog.  
- AI can assist discovery (ticket clustering, trend analytics, competitor scanning, interview transcription, success‑metric reminders) but cannot replace human judgment, context, or the ability to spot the truly “sparkling” idea.  
- Emphasises real user interviews (Teresa Torres) and strategic timing of discovery (Büşra Coşkuner).

### Core development

- Engineers still provide system architecture, security, scalability, and intuition; AI accelerates code generation but does not guarantee commercial viability.  
- Product roles should:
  - Keep teams outcome‑focused, questioning gold‑plating and market impact.  
  - Produce context files, briefs, and problem statements that align with the new rapid‑development model.  
  - Raise higher‑level concerns (architecture, supportability, concept drift, model changes) beyond pure code questions.

### Go‑to‑market planning

- Without a clear, simple economic value proposition, revenue stalls.  
- In AI‑first settings, product managers must ensure that messaging, pricing, ROI calculators, and reference materials are ready quickly, as sales and marketing rely on these assets to generate cash flow.  
- The article suggests that product managers cannot simply hand off this work; they must actively shape and validate the market narrative.  

## Key takeaways

- AI will make code production fast, but **discovery and market validation remain the bottlenecks** for revenue.  
- A “barbell” allocation—heavy effort on the fuzzy front‑end and go‑to‑market, lighter on core coding—optimizes product success.  
- AI tools can augment discovery but cannot replace human judgment; focus on high‑impact bets and discard the majority of low‑value ideas.  
- Product managers must evolve to steward outcomes, maintain strategic context, and drive compelling economic messaging in AI‑first organizations.
