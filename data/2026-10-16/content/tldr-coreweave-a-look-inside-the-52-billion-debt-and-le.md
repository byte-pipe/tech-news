### ONE BIG THING

## CoreWeave investor explains how the loss-making AI ‘neocloud’ provider plans to claw its way out of a $52 billion hole

CoreWeave—and the vast amount of debt it has taken on to fund its business—dragged down the entire stock market this week. According to its Q2 earnings release, it had operating lease liabilities of $16.3 billion and total debt of $35.6 billion. The company only made $4.6 billion in revenue for the first six months, and its operating expenses exceeded its sales, leaving it with a net loss of $1.4 billion on the bottom line in the period.

To put it simply, how will a company that makes no money pay back $52 billion in combined debt and lease commitments?

The short answer is that the company has $103.7 billion of “remaining performance obligations” (RPOs), 41% of which is scheduled to be paid to CoreWeave through 2028, its Q2 disclosure states. Those RPOs are “take or pay” agreements that CoreWeave’s customers cannot weasel out of.

Not everyone is a believer. “If CoreWeave is counting on Microsoft to honor its contracts and bail them out of extreme debt and losses I have a bridge in Brooklyn I’d like to sell them,” Francine McKenna, the accountant and investigative journalist who has taught at The Wharton School, toldFortune.

### THE MARKETS

## The bloodletting in stocks is stanched as investors buy the dip and rotate out of tech

After a grueling couple of days for tech stocks—propelled by drama and speculation around CoreWeave’s debt—traders bought the dip in the last 24 hours and stocks rose globally. U.S. futures are up before the open in New York, too.

Much of the gains were in non-tech stocks. While the S&P 500 gained 0.33% yesterday, the tech-heavy Nasdaq was flat, and the equal-weight S&P was up a solid 1.36%. Deutsche Bank’s Jim Reid noted that “378 names in the S&P 500 were higher on the day.” The index remains within 1% of its record high.

"The selling in tech has been stopped in its tracks, while the rotation into other sectors continues. Tech stocks aren't out of the woods yet, but rotation of this kind is just what investors need to see, even if it is a deeply painful experience for tech fans,” Chris Beauchamp, the chief market analyst at trading platform IG, toldFortunein an email.

### WHAT COULD POSSIBLY GO WRONG

## AI's next hurdle isn't debt. It's state legislatures.

The collapse in the price of CoreWeave stock has dragged down the entire tech sector in the last couple of days, and focused investors’ minds on the risks attached to AI. And although the chatter has revolved around the “existential threat” of rogue AI agents, customers who aren’t seeing productivity gains, AI CEOs like Dario Amodei and Sam Altman urging a slowdown in development, and the widening spreads on debt issued by CoreWeave and Oracle, Macquarie’s Thierry Wizman and Gareth Berry point out that there’s another set of hurdles in the system: The number of U.S. states legislating against the development of AI.

### CHART OF THE DAY

## Fast, cheap, and (possibly) out of control

The cost of new technology tends to fall over time as companies become more expert at making it and improving it. But the cost of AI is falling so fast it’s almost charting a vertical drop, per this chart from Deutsche Bank comparing new tech pricing over time.

“The cost of achieving a given level of AI performance has tumbled about 47 percent a quarter, or 13 times a year, since 2023. That is four times faster than DNA sequencing, six times faster than compute, and 18 times faster than lithium batteries,” Deutsche’s Adrian Cox said in a recent note.

### ONE MORE THING

## This GOP billionaire's squirrel buffet is allegedly fueling a rat infestation in Chicago

Chicago officials are weighing legal action against Barre Seid, the 94-year-old industrialist behind the largest known political advocacy donation in U.S. history, over claims that workers he hires to scatter nuts in Lincoln Park are fueling a rat infestation,writesFortune’sCatherina Gioino.

Alderman Timmy Knudsen said he is pushing the Chicago Park District to sue Seid for damage to the park, while Park District officials told the publication they are still weighing their options.

In an Aug. 25 letter to Seid, Knudsen called the matter a final warning and said city staff had received photos and video documenting Seid or his staff putting out food daily for squirrels and other wildlife, according to WGN. The letter said the feeding created a public health hazard because it drew in not only squirrels but also pigeons and rats.
