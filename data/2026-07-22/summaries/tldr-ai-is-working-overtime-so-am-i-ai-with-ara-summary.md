# AI Is Working Overtime. So Am I

## AI fatigue is real and nobody talks about it
- AI features now appear in many everyday apps, creating a sense of “twenty tabs open” in the mind.  
- The phenomenon is observed both at work (pressure to adopt AI quickly) and in personal life (AI embedded in almost every tool).  
- Herbert A. Simon’s 1971 insight—“a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention”—captures the current challenge.

## AI use is growing faster than healthy working habits
- Gallup (Feb 2026): 50 % of U.S. employees use AI at least a few times a year; 28 % a few times a week or more; 13 % daily.  
- Lack of clear AI strategy leads to uncertainty about:
  - Which tasks should be delegated to AI  
  - Approved tools and privacy rules  
  - Required level of output verification  
  - Accountability for AI errors  
  - When to work without AI  
- Pew Research (2026): 52 % of workers worry about future AI use; 33 % feel overwhelmed; 36 % feel hopeful.

## What is AI fatigue?
- A layered set of strains:
  - **Tool overload** – juggling many platforms, dashboards, and features.  
  - **Verification burden** – constant checking of AI output.  
  - **Prompt exhaustion** – repeated re‑phrasing to obtain usable answers.  
  - **Keeping‑up anxiety** – fear of falling behind peers who have mastered a tool.  
- A 2026 review of 22 studies warns that AI fatigue is not only an individual self‑care issue; employers and designers also shape it.

## Why does AI become exhausting?

### 1. The tool becomes another thing to manage
- Workers must decide:
  - Which AI to use for a task?  
  - Which version or model is best?  
  - Is the tool approved and privacy‑compliant?  
  - Do I need a better prompt?  
- The decision‑making layer adds cognitive load instead of simplifying work.

### 2. Fast output produces slow decisions
- Example: a marketer receives 25 AI‑generated headlines in seconds, then must read, de‑duplicate, assess brand risk, verify claims, and select one.  
- The pattern “generate → compare → revise → generate again” multiplies small decisions, often without increasing overall clarity.

### 3. The first draft is fast, but the full task is not
- Upwork survey (2,500 respondents):
  - 96 % of executives expect AI to boost productivity.  
  - 77 % of employees using AI report higher workload.  
  - Reasons: 39 % spend more time reviewing/moderating AI content, 23 % learn new tools, 21 % are asked to do more work because AI makes it possible.

### 4. AI enters an already interrupted workday
- Microsoft telemetry: active users are interrupted by meetings, emails, or chats roughly every two minutes; 60 % of meetings are unscheduled.  
- Adding AI creates another stream of outputs to review, further fragmenting attention.

## The same AI can help one task and slow another
- Effectiveness depends on:
  - Task type and required context  
  - Worker experience  
  - Quality of the AI system  
  - Cost of checking mistakes  
  - Fit with existing workflow  
- When AI helped: study of 5,179 customer‑support agents showed a 14 % increase in issues resolved per hour; newer/less‑skilled agents improved by 34 %.  
- When AI slowed work: 2025 randomized study of experienced open‑source developers found a 19 % increase in time spent, despite expectations of a 24 % speed gain.

## The coworker who inherits the hidden work
- Scenario: an employee uses AI to produce a polished report that omits key complaints and next steps, shifting verification work to the manager.  
- “Workslop” (BetterUp & Stanford Social Media Lab, 2025):
  - 40 % of U.S. desk workers received workslop in the past month.  
  - Each case required nearly two hours to fix.

## AI can save time and still create fatigue
- Even when AI reduces the time to generate content, the downstream tasks of verification, contextualization, and decision‑making often generate comparable or greater effort, sustaining the feeling of fatigue.
