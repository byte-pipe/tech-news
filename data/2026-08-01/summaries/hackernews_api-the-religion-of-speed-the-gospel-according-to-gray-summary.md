# The Religion of Speed – Summary  

## Main Argument  
- “Moving fast” has shifted from a practical need to a moral badge, equating speed with ambition and caution with fear.  
- Real speed exists only when work is well‑understood, constraints are clear, and decisions are clean; most touted speed is merely impatience dressed as progress.  

## Consequences of a Speed‑First Culture  
- Produces fragile systems, opaque processes, untrusted dashboards, and workarounds that become essential yet hidden.  
- The initial rushed effort is recorded as “fast,” while the subsequent cleanup, rework, and learning are labeled with euphemisms that hide the true cost.  
- Leads to burnout, customer loss, and accumulated operational scar tissue that persists because no one revisits the original mistakes.  

## Why Speed Is Attractive  
- Allows people to stay abstract, keep decisions fuzzy, and avoid responsibility for details until they become someone else’s emergency.  
- When emergencies arise, the same speed mantra (“move faster, fix faster”) is invoked as the cure, creating a self‑reinforcing loop.  

## Urgency vs. Haste  
- **Urgency**: appropriate response to genuine time‑sensitive matters.  
- **Haste**: the emotional shortcut of acting without clarity, seeking dopamine from motion rather than progress.  

## Recommended Approach  
- Slow down by making work legible: ask what is being produced, who depends on it, what assumptions exist, and where past failures occurred.  
- Follow the disciplined sequence: **understand first → decide second → execute only after due diligence**.  
- Respect the work enough to do it properly the first time, avoiding unnecessary meetings or prolonged delays that do not add value.  

## Final Insight  
- True productivity does not require constant motion; thoughtful execution yields faster outcomes in the long run.  
- Systems remember shortcuts; reality is patient and will eventually collect the debt incurred by a culture that worships speed over understanding.
