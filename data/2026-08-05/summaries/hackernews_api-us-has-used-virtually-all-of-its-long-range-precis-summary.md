# U.S. has used ‘virtually all’ of its long‑range precision missiles: report  

## Main findings  
- Three sources familiar with U.S. data say the Army’s long‑range surface‑to‑surface missiles – ATACMS and the newer Precision Strike Missiles (PrSM) – have been “virtually all” expended during the five‑month war with Iran.  
- The depletion has not been publicly reported before and raises concerns about the U.S. ability to conduct future long‑range strikes.  

## Missile types and usage  
- **ATACMS** – older Army tactical missile, used heavily in Iran and supplied to Ukraine; being phased out.  
- **PrSM** – newer, longer‑range replacement for ATACMS; stockpiles were already low before the war, but the Army has ordered large numbers for 2027.  
- **Tomahawk cruise missiles** – Navy‑launched; about half of the global supply has been fired since the conflict began.  
- **Patriot interceptors** – anti‑ballistic‑missile system; roughly 65 % of the February‑July stockpile expended.  
- **THAAD interceptors** – ballistic‑missile defense; inventory down at least 38 % from the start of the war.  

## Stockpile status  
- Sources declined to give exact remaining numbers for ATACMS or PrSM.  
- Central Command has nearly exhausted its land‑based missiles but has been able to replenish some from other U.S. depots.  
- Internal government figures circulated in the past week show rapid drawdown of both offensive and defensive munitions.  

## Government and industry responses  
- President Trump asserted the U.S. has “far more munitions than anyone in the world” and that defense firms are producing munitions at record rates.  
- Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell emphasized the overall strength of the U.S. military and its deep arsenal.  
- Lockheed Martin (producer of ATACMS, PrSM, THAAD) and Raytheon (producer of Tomahawk, Patriot) did not immediately comment on the supply claims.  
- Raytheon has a tentative multi‑year agreement with the Pentagon to boost Tomahawk production and other munitions.  

## Implications and concerns  
- Analysts warn that the loss of high‑cost (> $1 million each) long‑range missiles could limit U.S. deterrence against Iran, Russia, and China, especially where air defenses are strong.  
- Military leaders have cautioned the president that dwindling stocks of defensive weapons (Patriot, THAAD) also threaten readiness for other crises.  
- The depletion contributed to Trump’s decision to forego a larger offensive inside Iran, reportedly due to adviser warnings rather than Gulf‑state pressure.  
- No formal congressional authorization for the war with Iran has been sought, intensifying debate over the president’s authority to continue hostilities.
