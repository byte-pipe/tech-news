# Every App Eventually Becomes a Dashboard. Please Make It Stop.

## The lie of simplicity  
- Apps often start with the promise “We’re going to keep this simple.”  
- The initial experience is a single, focused task that feels beautiful and fast.  

## Feature creep leads to dashboards  
- Over time features such as analytics, filters, sidebars, workspaces, roles, notifications, activity feeds, and widgets are added.  
- The accumulation inevitably produces a dashboard, turning a tiny useful app into enterprise‑style software.  

## Nobody asks for dashboards  
- Users rarely request metric cards or donut charts for simple tools.  
- Yet dashboards appear in project managers, writing apps, bookmark managers, even niche tools like plant‑watering apps.  

## Feature creep’s polite progression  
- New features are introduced with gentle rationales (“show recent activity,” “add shortcuts,” “customizable widgets,” “enterprise roles,” “AI assistant,” “marketplace”).  
- The creep sneaks in gradually, eventually consuming resources and adding complexity.  

## Confusing “more” with “better”  
- The ability to add a feature often triggers discussion about implementation rather than questioning its necessity.  
- More features look impressive on pricing pages and give marketing and sales material, but they rarely add real value.  
- Sometimes the best decision is to add nothing at all.  

## Simplicity feels luxurious  
- When software opens quickly, does one thing well, and avoids onboarding hoops, it feels premium.  
- Restraint signals confidence; the product simply works and stays out of the way.  

## Dashboards as a symptom  
- Genuine dashboards are useful for monitoring servers, revenue, logistics, etc.  
- Problematic dashboards appear when a product loses sight of its core purpose, cluttering the UI with cards, charts, and “getting started” prompts for long‑time users.  

## Missing software with edges  
- The author misses tools that know their limits and do not try to become an OS, community hub, knowledge base, or personal assistant.  
- A timer can just be a timer; notes can just be notes, without forcing users into a larger ecosystem.  

## Rethinking “more”  
- Instead of asking “What else can this product do?” the harder question is “What can we remove without making it worse?”  
- Removing feels risky, but true simplicity results from hidden effort and makes a product feel obvious.  

## Call to action  
- Before adding analytics, widgets, workspaces, or productivity cards, ask whether the addition truly improves the product or merely builds another dashboard.  
- Recognize that somewhere a beautiful, single‑purpose app exists, and it may soon be targeted for unnecessary expansion.
