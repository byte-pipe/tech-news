# 15,000 years of solar wind in four hours? A Seattle company gives it a go.

## Background on lunar helium‑3
- The Sun’s nuclear fusion creates helium‑3, some of which escapes as solar wind and implants into the Moon’s regolith because the Moon lacks a magnetic field and atmosphere.  
- Over billions of years the solar wind has delivered only 10–20 ppb of helium‑3, with higher concentrations in titanium‑rich soils, but still far more than on Earth.  
- Helium‑3 is attractive for long‑term fusion energy and for near‑term uses such as ultra‑low‑temperature cooling, medical research, and neutron detection, making it one of the few lunar resources that could be profitably mined and returned to Earth.

## The practical obstacle
- Commercial firms need kilograms of lunar material to test extraction methods, but NASA’s Johnson Space Center holds only ~300 kg of pristine regolith and is unlikely to release large quantities for destructive testing.  
- Existing lunar simulants reproduce geological properties but not the exact helium‑3 chemistry.

## Interlune’s innovative simulant
- Interlune’s planetary‑science team, led by Elizabeth Frank, placed real lunar regolith in a vacuum chamber and bombarded it with ionized helium accelerated to mimic solar‑wind exposure.  
- In four hours they simulated the effect of 15,000 years of solar wind, implanting helium‑3 at realistic depths.  
- Heating the treated regolith (pyrolysis) released helium at the same temperatures observed in Apollo‑returned samples, confirming the simulant’s fidelity.

## Future plans and significance
- The company will use the new simulant to test hardware designed to extract and process helium‑3 on the Moon and intends to sell the material to other lunar‑mining ventures.  
- A prototype mission on a lunar lander is planned for later this decade to validate the extraction system in situ.  
- CEO Rob Meyerson emphasizes the importance of “similitude” – matching lunar gravity, vacuum conditions, and now regolith chemistry – to ensure Earth‑based testing accurately reflects lunar operations.
