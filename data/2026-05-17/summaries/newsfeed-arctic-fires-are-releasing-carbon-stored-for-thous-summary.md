# Arctic fires are releasing carbon stored for thousands of years

## Main findings
- Soil cores from Arctic and boreal regions show that recent wildfires ignite carbon that is up to 5 000 years old.  
- Surface vegetation burns quickly, but it triggers slower smouldering of deep organic material, releasing CO₂ and black carbon.  
- The depth of burn and age of released carbon vary with location:  
  - Northwest Territories, Canada – several centimetres deep, carbon up to 400 years old.  
  - Greenland – average 10 cm depth, carbon up to 560 years old; some sites 15 cm deep, carbon up to 1 000 years old.  
  - Quebec boreal forest – isolated spots where carbon dating back 5 000 years was released.

## Climate implications
- Black carbon absorbs sunlight, warming the atmosphere directly and darkening snow/ice, which accelerates melting.  
- Ancient carbon release adds a source of greenhouse gases that current climate models do not include.  
- As fire regimes shift toward larger, more frequent Arctic fires, the risk of unlocking long‑stored carbon increases.

## Study methodology
- Researchers led by Meri Ruppel (Finnish Meteorological Institute) collected and analysed soil cores from areas affected by recent fires.  
- Radiocarbon dating was used to determine the age of the combusted organic material.  
- Findings were presented at the European Geosciences Union meeting in Vienna.

## Expert commentary
- Sandy Harrison (University of Reading) highlighted the significance of old carbon in high‑latitude soils and warned that changing fire regimes could release substantial amounts of this carbon.  
- The study is described as a preliminary step; further work is needed to quantify the total amount of ancient carbon being emitted.

## Broader context
- Arctic and boreal soils act as major carbon sinks, storing plant remnants as peat over centuries to millennia.  
- Increased fire activity threatens the stability of these sinks, potentially turning them into carbon sources and amplifying global warming.
