# A hidden ocean change near Antarctica could have global consequences

## Main findings
- Since 2016 Antarctic sea‑ice has declined rapidly, coinciding with a sharp increase in salinity within the Antarctic Intermediate Water (AIW) layer (≈2,300–4,000 ft deep) that encircles the continent.  
- The salinity rise reverses decades of freshening and mirrors ocean shifts that normally occur over millennia, but now happens within a few years.  
- AIW spreads northward into the Indian, Pacific and Atlantic oceans, carrying heat, carbon, oxygen and nutrients toward the tropics and across the equator.

## Data and methodology
- Researchers combined six independent datasets covering 2004–2024, primarily from Argo floats that profile temperature, density and salinity at depth.  
- A 2025 study also detected increasing surface salinity linked to the loss of sea‑ice.  

## Sea‑ice trends
- Antarctic sea‑ice peaked in satellite records in 2014, then fell to a record low by 2016 and remained well below the 2014 level.  
- In 2023 the ice extent was about 1 million sq mi below the 1981‑2010 average, roughly four times the size of Texas.  
- Reduced melt‑season freshwater input makes the AIW layer saltier.

## Potential climate impacts
- Changes in AIW density, thickness and salinity affect heat exchange between surface and deep ocean, influencing how the ocean stores and redistributes heat.  
- Within five years, impacts could include increased storm frequency and altered ocean heat loss, as suggested by recent studies.  
- A 2024 scientific review linked Antarctic sea‑ice changes to global atmospheric heat and moisture distribution and variability.  

## Expert commentary
- **Michael Meredith (British Antarctic Survey)**: The Southern Ocean is a pivotal junction for global ocean circulation; sea‑ice loss is already altering deep‑water properties and could have worldwide consequences.  
- **Bishakhdatta Gayen (University of Melbourne)**: More monitoring is needed; the rapid shift may be temporary, but climate models still struggle to reproduce recent sea‑ice declines, indicating a need for improved modeling.  
- The upper Southern Ocean circulation can affect climate on timescales of years to decades, unlike deeper circulation changes that manifest over centuries.

## Outlook
- Continued observation and enhanced climate‑model representation of Antarctic sea‑ice dynamics are essential to anticipate how these oceanic changes will propagate through global climate systems and ecosystems.
