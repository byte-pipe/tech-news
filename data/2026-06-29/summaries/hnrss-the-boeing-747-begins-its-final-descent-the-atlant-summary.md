# The Boeing 747 Begins Its Final Descent – Summary

## I. The Boneyard  
- I arrive at Pinal Airpark near Tucson, Arizona, and see a stark‑white 747 stripped of its wings, doors open to the desert wind, and a rickety airstair – a “corpse in full.”  
- The 747, nicknamed the Whale, Longreach, Sky Cruiser, Mother of All Airliners, and Queen of the Skies, served as the primary vehicle for generations of important journeys.  
- Over the last two decades airlines have retired the 747 in favor of smaller, more efficient jets, turning the aircraft from a symbol of American might, invention, and progress into a reminder of their decline.  
- Jim Petty, manager of the airpark, explains that Pinal is not merely a “boneyard”; some aircraft come for inspection or short‑term storage, while others end their lives there when repair proves impossible.  
- A TWA 747 that has sat for almost 30 years dominates the horizon; Boeing built 1,574 of the type from 1970 to 2023, including the two that still serve as Air Force One.  
- Petty predicts that eventually only one 747 will remain.

## II. Birth of an Icon  
- The 747’s massive size (≈300 metric tons, wingspan covering two‑thirds of a football field) made it difficult to maneuver on the ground but smooth to fly; pilots recall almost imperceptible takeoffs and landings.  
- The project originated from a 1960s military transport request; after Boeing lost the C‑5 Galaxy contract, engineers redirected effort to the largest commercial airliner ever built.  
- Boeing constructed a massive Everett assembly plant (the world’s largest building by volume) at a cost of $200 million (≈$2 billion today), employing about 2,700 engineers.  
- Executives described the gamble as a “sporty game” – a high‑risk bet that could have sunk the company.  
- Thomas Gray, an electrical engineer who helped test the first 747, recalls the 1969 maiden flight and predicts fleets of 747s would line up for takeoff.  
- The 747 could carry over 490 passengers and substantial cargo, far exceeding earlier jets (707, 727, 737).  
- Pan Am’s Juan Trippe ordered 25 aircraft, calling the jet “a great weapon for peace” and envisioning it as a catalyst for human flourishing.  
- The jet helped double annual air travelers from 1969 to 1979 (to 640 million) and combined speed with comfort, outlasting the failed supersonic passenger projects.

## III. Legroom and Caviar (architectural and cultural impact)  
- Architect Norman Foster described the 747 as his favorite 20th‑century building, emphasizing its role as a dwelling rather than just a vehicle.  
- The upper‑deck lounge, created because the flight deck had to sit above the cargo hatch, became the plane’s most iconic interior space.  
- The design’s “hump” allowed nose‑loading of containers, a feature that kept the 747 relevant even after supersonic ambitions faded.  
- The aircraft’s longevity turned it into a cultural touchstone, symbolizing an era of ambitious American engineering and mass travel.
