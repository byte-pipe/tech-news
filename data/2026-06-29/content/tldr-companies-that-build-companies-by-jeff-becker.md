# Companies Building Companies

### A Pareto extreme

Jeff Becker
Jun 22, 2026
6
1
Share

TLDR: A new kind of company has arrived. Companies whose product is starting and running other companies, run by swarms of AI agents.

Have you read about Polsia?

A startup that calls itself “AI Slop” spelled backwards raised $30m at a $250m valuation with one founder and a swarm of AI agents instead of employees.

Half the internet called it the future. The other half called it a scam.

Let me lay out what actually happened.

Subscribe

Polsia spins up and runs a software business for you. Pay $49 a month plus 20% of whatever it makes, and a stack of agents handles the research, the code, the payments, the ads, the support, the sales.

One human. Zero employees.

The founder, Ben Cera, came out of CloudKitchens. He says Polsia is approaching $10 million in annualized revenue five months in, with around 7,600 business customers. And the detail everyone thinks is a lie: he says the AI ran the fundraise itself. Built the data room, briefed the investors, handled diligence. Apparently Ben just showed up for the final calls to sign the term sheets.

I’m actually betting there’s a bit of truth in that, and a whole lot of hyperbole, which as it turns out, is a whole lot like the business model itself.

In Episode 1 of their mini series, Ben makes light of the fact that the product isn’t perfect yet, particularly in light of its name. Its Trustpilot score is a 2.0, with users saying it marks work finished that never shipped. As a user, I can attest to that fact. And plenty of smart people think “zero employees” is just marketing too.

So which is it?

Before we go there, it’s not just one company.

YC just backed a company called Thomas. Not an AI you hire. The AI founder. A virtual human with a face and a voice whose only goal is to make money, who starts and runs his own companies and keeps the profit. The human behind it scaled a freelance shop to $40k a month, then automated himself, and says he mostly signs the legal documents now.

Thomas’s own site is refreshingly blunt about it: you can get rich selling the dream of self‑running companies even if the companies never make a dollar. But, it also promises that’s not the plan.

Behind them is a whole cohort. One YC company builds AI‑run consumer brands that move at the speed of compute, not the speed of people. Another is an AI consulting firm with no humans in the loop.

The category has a shape now. The company whose product is companies.

In recent months, I’ve seen more and more of these.

Founders looking to ride the wave. To outsource the human work to agents. Insurance, medical billing, consulting, tour scheduling, dating, you name it.

That’s not to say the ideas are good or bad, inevitable or tarpits. It’s to say the competition is deep and fierce, and the founder’s relationship to the problem—their obsession, work ethic, ability to create leverage, and their appreciation for first principles and durability now matters more than ever.

We shouldn’t be asking whether Polsia and Thomas are scams or miracles. That’s the wrong perspective. The right one is Shopify.

Millions of people have opened a Shopify store. Almost none ever launch a product, run an ad, or make a single sale. A small slice become real businesses. A sliver of those become huge. Shopify never needed every store to work. It needed the few that did, and they carried the whole platform.

Polsia and Thomas are the same math. Ninety‑five percent of what these platforms spin up will likely be slop. Abandoned drafts, half‑shipped products, a $49 charge someone forgot to cancel. That’s not the bug. That’s the model.

The question was never “do most of them work.” In reality, and maybe by design, they won’t. The real question is whether the 5% that do are real enough and big enough to matter. If one obsessed person and a swarm of agents can produce a handful of real winners, the slop is just the cost of distribution.

Look at it that way and the whole fight dissolves. The skeptics are right that most of it is junk. The believers are right that some of it is real. They’re describing the same power law and fighting over which end to stare at.

You could say the vibecoding sites are the same. You could even argue all of VC is too.

And notice what’s getting cheap. Starting a company.

The paperwork, the landing page, the first build, the cold outreach, the payments integration. It’s all collapsing toward free and instant. Starting has never been less of an edge. The whole game moves to the 5% these platforms can’t touch: the human element, the creativity, the relationship to the ICP and to the problem, the opinion about the workflow and the jobs to be done. The platform does the easy 95% of having a company and making it legible. But it can’t do the 5% that made it potentially “daring” or “legendary.” At least not yet.

As for the funds that wrote the check, whether it’s a winner or footnote, they’re making a platform bet, not a bet that the product is flawless today. Underwrite the 5%, tolerate the 95%. That’s how you bet on a platform. Aggressive. Not crazy.

The company that builds companies is here, and it’s not going anywhere. In fact, it’s the worst it will ever be, which is to say, it’s only going to get better, faster, cheaper, and stronger. Most of what it builds will be noise, the same way most Shopify stores sit without meaningful traffic or revenue. But Shopify still minted a few giants out of a sea of dead storefronts, and I’m betting our future will have more, certainly not less, AI automation in it.

Whether that scares you or not, I don’t know, but you might take solace in the idea that the founders who matter were never the ones who needed the easy part done for them. They’re the ones obsessed enough to make it durable, to never give up, and to take it the distance.

Always watch the 5%. That is always the real story.

See you Monday.
