## Not ridiculous
- **Time commitment** – The accelerated NOLS Wilderness EMT course required 28 consecutive days; I could only attend because of unlimited vacation, supportive management, and the funds for travel.
- **Cultural concerns** – Past negative experiences with first responders and the “fratty” atmosphere, especially for women, made me hesitant.
- **Fit with people** – First‑responder communities can be tight‑knit, but I found allies from diverse backgrounds, including a tech‑industry PM who helped me get the job.
- **Fit with classmates** – I worried the class would be full of teenage adventure junkies; instead, it mixed enthusiastic pre‑meds with peers my age.
- **Focus on day job** – I often used EMS as a distraction when work was rough, but after certification balancing both has been manageable.
- **Ambulance work** – I never imagined riding in an ambulance; EMT knowledge is still valuable for CPR and basic care without needing to staff an ambulance.
- **Scope of practice** – EMTs mainly provide advanced first aid (CPR, AEDs, oxygen) but not the full interventions of paramedics; the training was enjoyable and practical.
- **Finding volunteer work** – Volunteer EMS is easier in smaller cities; a car and willingness to drive a short distance are usually enough.
- **Certificate expiration** – Continuing education is required to keep the NREMT active, but learning for its own sake is still worthwhile.
- **Interest in search & rescue** – While SAR is appealing, effective EMT work on a truck is still essential for that path.
- **Prep costs** – I did cheaper prep (books, CPR, Stop the Bleed, weekend wilderness first aid) before committing to the full course.
- **Expense** – The course cost about $5,000 plus travel; cheaper options exist, but the experience felt worth the investment.
- **No immediate use** – I pursued the credential mainly for personal interest; later I happened to join a crew and found a practical outlet.
