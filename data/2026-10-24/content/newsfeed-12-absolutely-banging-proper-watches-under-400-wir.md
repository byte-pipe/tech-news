The wonderful countertorising pricesat thevery top end of the watch worldis that, frankly, things have never looked better for those seeking propermechanical watcheson a budget. Entry-level timepieces keep getting better and cheaper—and this isn't just from newly established microbrands hoping to make a name for themselves, but from the major players, too.

Recently, I was asked to pick a couple of wallet-friendly watches for WIRED'sGear newsletter, and it sent me down a glorious rabbit hole where I was genuinely surprised by superb timepieceswell belowwhat WIRED has previously set for affordable watches (thesub-$1,000bracket). Far from dropping three figures on a decent automatic, you can now get almost anything you desire from a watch for far less than $500.

To check my math and make sure I wasn't going slightly crazy, I reached out to WIRED's wider circle ofwatch expertswith a devilishly tricky, unenviable challenge: Pick their favorite mechanical watch for less than $400, the sort of price where quartz watches dominate. It was a hard ask, but they delivered. Here's the list—all are exceptional value, and watches we'd be delighted to sport on our wrists.

## Caravelle Sea Hunter

Courtesy of Caravelle

Caravelle has no right to make a mechanical dive watch this attractive and capable for amere $300, but here it is. The secret to its timeless look is that Bulova's budget brand sensibly fashioned this modern Sea Hunter heavily on the diver's design from the late 1960s, while bumping the size up to a more modern 39 mm. Elegant and stylish, it's practically a GADA (go anywhere, do anything) watch. But with 200 meters of waterproofing, a 120-click unidirectional bezel, and a 42-hour power reserve, the Sea Hunter is a compelling sports watch. Throw on a rubber strap for diving, a nato for casual (our pick is Artem), or stick with the three-link steel bracelet for a more classic look.

## Orient Bambino Version 7 38 mm

Courtesy of Orient Bambino

Unquestionably one of the best-value automatic dress watches you can buy today, the$295 Orient Bambino’s timeless 38-mm design with white dial and leather strap has been carefully shrunken down from the original 40.5-mm casing, making this piece incredibly wearable (both in these smaller watch times and when case sizes inevitably get larger again.) You can view the automatic Japanese movement through the exhibition case back, and there's even 30 meters of water resistance—not that you should be swimming with such a watch. Want something less traditional? Go for thepink dialwith a stainless steel bracelet.

Shop.com

## Brew Metric Lite Retro

Courtesy of Brew

This new smaller take on Brew's original Metric Retro is the choice ofMike Christensen, British GQ's lifestyle director and watch editor. Cashing in on the current trend for very small watches, Mike loves that this 30-mm piece is wearable for both men and women. “This li'l Brew number is so retro they decided to call it exactly that,” he says.At just $375, the stainless steel Metric Lite Retro, complete with a matching bracelet, uses an automatic movement visible through the open caseback. It has 50 meters of water resistance, and even sapphire crystal atop the striking dial. Proper retro charm that's forgiving on the bank balance.

Shop.com

## Timex Marlin 1965 Reissue Hand-Wound

Courtesy of Timex

Chris Hall, a watch expert who writes for WIRED, The New York Times, Financial Times, and others, as well as his excellentThe Fourth WheelSubstack, recommends the Timex Marlin 1965 Reissue Hand-Woundat $259. “It was the model that marked Timex’s return to mechanical watches about a decade ago, and unlike so many watches at this level, it isn’t a rip-off of something else: It’s a tribute to one of Timex’s own designs,” Hall says. “It’s small, quirky, and very much its own thing. Never mind the mechanics, getting real personality and credibility at this price is what’s really impressive.” At 34 mm on a leather strap, the design feels pure, and the 30-meter water resistance is a bonus.

Shop.com

## Sea-Gull 1963 “International Edition”

Courtesy of Sea-Gull

Looking a little further afield can often pay off in the watch world. A prime example is the Sea-Gull 1963, a hand-wound mechanical chronograph based on a vintage pilot's timepiece originally built for the Chinese Air Force back in 1961. Many call this watch the gateway drug to watch collecting, but there are a few different versions. Here we've gone for the “International Edition,” which seems to be a budget model of the 1963 Sea-Gull, as it sells alternative iterations for more on its own website. The design is faithful (including the ST1901 movement), the 38-mm size is perfect, and you can't argue with the unfathomably low $269 price.

## Baltic HMS

Courtesy of Baltic

Founded in 2016 by Etienne Malec and debuting on Kickstarter in 2017, this French micro-brand was inspired by the watch collection Malec inherited from his late father, whose family hailed from northern Poland near the Baltic Sea. The brand has quickly become a darling of watch reviewers, so it's no surprise that two of WIRED's watch experts have chosen the 1940s-inspired three-hand HMS (cheating ever so slightly as the HMS clocks in at $430).

Robin Swithinbank, who writes on watches for The New York Times, Financial Times, and Business of Fashion, and hosts theLuxury Society Podcast, has been won over by the 38-mmHMS 002 Silver(above right). “At any price, I like my watches simple, legible, and hardwired with the kind of style that never dies. Baltic absolutely nailed it with this,” he says. Powered by an automatic Miyota 8315 movement with a 60-hour power reserve, it also has 50 meters of water resistance.

Tim Vaux, who writes for both Hodinkee and British GQ about watches, prefers theHMS 003 Salmon(above left). It's smaller than the 002 at 36.5 mm, but still uses the Miyota 8315 movement. “In 2026, there’s no need to look at the household names anymore. The HMS 003 has one of the most classically configured dials you’ll find at any price,” Vaux says. “Walk into a room wearing this and nods of approval will come from all angles. With the Salmon dial, you can’t go wrong.”

Baltic

## Bulova A-11 Hack Watch

Courtesy of Bulova

As Caravelle's big brother, Bulova has appeared in ourbargain watch roundupsbefore. Still, this time, Felix Scholz, editor-in-chief of Revolution Australia and cohost ofOT: The Podcast, chose the A-11 Hack Watch, on sale forjust $357. “Military watches hold a special place in the hearts of watch enthusiasts,” Scholz says. “And while they might not be rare or intrinsically valuable, the hacking watches produced by numerous American makers during the Second World War are among the greatest.”

Legible and functional, Scholz says such pieces are a true slice of history, and that Bulova is one of the historic manufacturers of this type. “The A-11 Hack captures the spirit, but the less martial blue dial feels contemporary, and doesn't make you look like you're LARPing as a reenactor.”

Shop.com

## Citizen Tsuyosa

Courtesy of Citizen

Pickings are admittedly slim for genuine automatic GADA (go anywhere, do anything) watches under $400, but take a look at the$396 Citizen Tsuyosawith its classic black dial and integrated stainless steel bracelet. It features a 40-mm case, 50 meters of water resistance, sapphire crystal, 42-hour power reserve, and a 3 o’clock date window that will see you from beach to boardroom. If black is too serious, Citizen offers severaldial hues(gold and greenis a nice combo).

WIRED's top pick, however, is the new, more wearable37-mm rangeconveniently on sale right now at just under our $400 target. If you venture above $600, park Citizen and go for theLorier FalconorTraska Commuter 36instead.

Shop.com

## Vostok Amphibia

Courtesy of Vostok Amphibia

WIRED watch expertLaura McCreddie-Doakhas seen our Chinese chrono option and upped the ante with this Russian dive watch. Vostok traces back to the Soviet Union'sChistopol Watch Factory, a supplier to the USSR Ministry of Defense during World War II. This Amphibia 420269 (there are many, manyother models) has a 2416B automatic movement that offers a 31-hour power reserve. Bafflinglyjust $151on a steel bracelet, it's rated for 200 meters of water resistance, thanks to its unique, pressure-tightening case design. “This watch needs no other justification for inclusion on this list other than it was worn by Bill Murray as Jacques Cousteau-alike Steve Zissou in Wes Anderson’sThe Life Aquatic,” says McCreddie-Doak.

## Seiko 5 Sports

Seiko 5 SRPE71.

Courtesy of Seiko

Finally, we come to the almost inevitable inclusion of theSeiko 5 Sportsautomatic. The familiar and affordable Seiko 5 Sports line began in 1968 as a tougher, sportier evolution of the 1963 Seiko Sportsmatic 5. The lineup is still going strong with dozens of variants plus a back catalog of thousands. That explains why, for the second time on this list, two of our experts chose the 5 Sports.

The Seiko 5 Sports SNSX.

Courtesy of Seiko

WIRED watch writer andauthor Josh Simssays the Seiko 5 Sports “is the MPV of watches: dependable, rugged, adaptable, as happy in the city as out of it. For my money—and this really is a lot of watch for, relatively, not a lot of money—it also has the cool of not being Swiss. Seiko isn’t just a giant of Japanese watchmaking; it’s a giant of watchmaking that deserves more r-e-s-p-e-c-t, and remains as relevant as ever.” Sims has gone for the SNSX Series that startsat $385.

Holly Ross, as executive editor ofVanity Fair’sOn Timewatch supplement, has gone for the Seiko 5 SRPE71 (pictured above), a model that's not new but still available if you look. “What is not to love about this chic, timeless, durable, and mechanical Seiko 5? And fora neat $320, it makes it even more desirable.”

Seiko
