The best workoutheadphones disappear when you start moving. They’re locked in without readjustments and comfortable enough that the music feels like it’s coming from inside your head. Your workout headphones have a tougher job than your everyday pair. They need to survive sweat, being thrown into a gym bag, and the occasional drop. They also need to sound good enough to keep you motivated until the finish line.

The WIRED Reviews team has tested hundreds ofheadphones and earbuds, and I’ve narrowed down the models that best balance sound quality, comfort, and durability. We put these picks through workouts like weightlifting and indoor swimming, as well as outdoor activities like trail runs, hikes, and cycling, to evaluate which headphones enhanced the experience and which ones got in the way. This roundup includes a mix of headphones and earbuds, wired and wireless, from the top pick, theBeats Powerbeats Pro 2 ($250), to the bone-conductionShokz OpenRun Pro 2 ($180).

For more recommendations, check out our guides to theBest Wireless Earbuds, theBest Open Earbuds, and theBest Bone Conduction Headphones.

Beats by Dre

Beats were the first sport earbuds to tap into Apple’s ecosystem, but thePowerbeats Pro 2work nearly as well with Android, making them a great pick for any phone. The redesigned nickel-titanium ear hooks stayed in place through hours of testing, and former WIRED reviewerRyan Waniatasaid they’re the most comfortable hooked earbuds he’s worn. The noise canceling is a major upgrade, outperforming the BeatsStudio Buds+and holding its own against premium rivals likeSony’s WF-1000XM5andTechnics’ AZ80. The Powerbeats Pro 2 also finally add a natural-sounding transparency mode, along with punchy sound, up to 10 hours of battery life (eight with ANC enabled), and convenient push-button controls.

Apple users get extras like iCloud device switching, Find My functionality, and hands-free Siri, while Android users still benefit from the Beats app, heart rate monitoring, and one-touch pairing. I wish the PowerBeats Pro 2 had a higher IP rating than IPX4, but they’re still built to handle sweat and light rain.

Bose

If you prefer over-ear headphones for your workouts, the Bose QuietComfort Ultra 2 are hard to beat. The plush fit is comfortable enough for long listening sessions, and the impressive noise canceling blocks out any distractions at the gym. The sound is clear and engaging, with plenty of punch, plus a transparency mode that feels natural. Bose also added useful upgrades like USB-C lossless audio, improved ambient controls, and longer battery life.

The ear cups do trap heat and sweat, but I have yet to find a pair of over-ear headphones that don't encourage sweat during intense workouts. Nonetheless, they are a premium pick for anyone who wants workout headphones to double as everyday headphones.

## Honorable Mentions

Photograph: Adrienne So

BlueAnt Pump X for $100:The BlueAnt Pump X has an incredible standout feature: cooling gel ear pads that you can keep in the fridge and swap out for the regular ear pads, so you don't get too sweaty. The single-button operation is intuitive, and ANC is effective. Battery life was also startlingly long—I wore them for an hour or two a day for several weeks before the battery got to 50 percent. The regular ear pads are soft and comfortable, and they're also water-resistant, which is unusual in over-ear gym headphones. There's also a special X Mode that pumps up the bass; I could feel distinct vibrations with my bass standard, Beyoncé's “Partition.” The price is also reasonable. —Adrienne So

Skullcandy Crusher 540 Active for $210:The BlueAnt headphones are just a better buy in all regards, but I enjoyed these a lot. The noise canceling doesn't work very well, but these headphones are attractive, and the bass is powerful. —Adrienne So

Shokz OpenFit Pro for $250:TheShokz OpenFit Pro’s sound quality is excellent; the buds are beautiful and comfortable; and I appreciate the physical buttons. That said, the open-ear noise-reduction system merely blunts outside noise rather than blocking it, so I don't understand the value here. —Adrienne So

Soundcore AeroFit 2 Pro for $180:TheAeroFit 2 Prophysically switches modes with its adjustable ear hooks to transform from open earbuds to noise-canceling ones. Unfortunately, I found the case to be too big, and the noise cancellation wasn’t so effective. Still, it's an interesting concept, and I do like being able to toggle the angle on the ear hooks for a more secure fit. —Adrienne So

Photograph: Adrienne So

Oakley Meta Vanguard for $499:Not only are they Oakley sunglasses with Prizm lenses for enhancing contrast and keeping you from tumbling head over heels on the trail, but they also have a 12-MPaction camerain the bridge of the nose, two over-ear speakers with a five-mic array for calls, and they integrate with Garmin to work as a (not very useful)fitness tracker. That's OK, because these are the best for moving at speed. They are 6 decibels louder than theOakley Meta HSTNs, and you can tell. They're the only open headphones where I don't have to turn the volume all the way up to hear podcasts when going over 20 mph. —Adrienne So

Photograph: Adrienne So

Chamelo Music Shield for $260:Chamelo's audio sunglasses have electro-chromic lenses; you can change how dark they get with a finger slider on the arm. However, the music isn't very loud, the battery life is much worse than the Vanguard, and they're relatively expensive. —Adrienne So

JLab Epic Sport ANC 3 for $100:This is the upgraded version of the Go Air Sport above, with hybrid dual drivers for better sound, a higher IP rating, better battery life, and active noise canceling. You need a fully sealed fit for ANC to be effective, which these don't have; I can still hear people at the gym when I'm lifting weights. Still, in every other way, these headphones meet their promises, and they do feel secure. —Adrienne So

H2O Audio Tri 2 Pro Multi-Sport for $220:These are a huge improvement from the first iteration of the brand's waterproof headphones, thanks to a more secure fit, better buttons, and a smoother silicone finish. However, they're pricier than the Shokz, and they use a proprietary charger instead of USB-C. —Adrienne So

Nwm Go for $99:Stylish Japanese brand Nwm recently launched these open-ear bone conduction headphones that purport to reduce sound leakage. It works—they soundfineand are incredibly light; they also use a proprietary charger. —Adrienne So

Photograph: Adrienne So

Nothing Open Earbuds for $99:These are some of the slimmest buds I've tested, and they fit well under layers of hoods, helmets, and hats. —Adrienne So

JLab JBuds Mini for $40:Fortiny earbuds, these sound pretty decent. They’re IP55, so they’re sweatproof and dustproof, and because they fit so well, the noise canceling is effective. The biggest drawback is that they’re not great for phone calls. They also only get around five hours of playtime without the case and 20 hours with it.

Suunto Sonic for $79:If you want to try a neckband-style headphone like the Shokz above, but for cheaper, WIRED contributor Michael Sawh says these are lightweight with a balanced sound profile. —Adrienne So

Speck Gemtones Sport for $70:These are cheap and fit well. The buttons are a little too sensitive, and the sound is noticeably fuzzier than most of our other picks, but they're not terrible. —Adrienne So

## Avoid These Headphones and Earbuds

Raycon Bone Conduction Headphones for $100:These headphones were so uncomfortable, pressing on my ear canal where they buzzed the flesh of my eardrums and not my bones. It was unbearable. —Adrienne So

Skullcandy Method 360 ANC for $130:In testing, I found the earbuds and case to be huge. The noise canceling also doesn’t work at all. —Adrienne So

Anker Soundcore C40i for $120:These open-ear buds fell out before I'd run a block down my street. I put them in my pocket and didn't wear them again for the rest of the run. —Adrienne So

1More Fit Open for $55:These earbuds sound OK, but the buttons are so sensitive that I couldn't run for more than five minutes without a song skipping or the music turning off. —Adrienne So
