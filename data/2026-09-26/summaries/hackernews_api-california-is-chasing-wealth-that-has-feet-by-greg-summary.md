# California Is Chasing Wealth That Has Feet

## Main argument
- California’s new billionaire wealth tax is unlikely to work because the targeted wealth is mobile, while the state’s real wealth—its land—cannot move.
- A modest land‑value tax would raise the same revenue without the problems of wealth flight.

## Size of California’s land wealth
- The Center for Land Economics estimates the total value of California’s land at **$8.14 trillion**, based on a parcel‑by‑parcel analysis cross‑checked with federal housing data and sales trends.
- This figure is about eight times larger than the total billionaire wealth the state could realistically tax.
- Los Angeles County alone holds more land value than the entire billionaire base the wealth tax targets; the Bay Area is close behind.

## Why the billionaire wealth tax will fall short
- The tax assumes a $2 trillion billionaire base, but recent residency changes have removed roughly half of that amount.
  - Six major billionaires (Page, Brin, Thiel, Hankey, Kalanick, Spielberg) left before the Jan 1 2026 cutoff, taking about $540 billion.
  - Mark Zuckerberg (≈$220 billion) also left and is expected to challenge the retroactive application.
- An over‑estimate of $200 billion in the proposal’s own model further shrinks the taxable base.
- To still generate $20 billion, the rate would need to rise from 1 % to 1.6–1.9 %, which would incentivize the remaining billionaires to leave as well.

## Land‑value tax as an alternative
- A **0.25 %** land‑value tax on the $8.14 trillion base would produce the same $20 billion annually.
- A 1 % rate would cover California’s entire $87 billion health and human services budget in perpetuity.
- The tax applies only to land value, not improvements, so it cannot be avoided by moving or passed on to renters.
- It captures public‑created value (e.g., transit‑induced appreciation) and returns it to taxpayers.
- Because land value is highly concentrated, the burden falls on prime coastal and downtown parcels, minimally affecting average working families.

## Historical context: Proposition 13
- Since 1978, Prop 13 capped property taxes and froze assessed values until a sale, resulting in assessments at only 44–60 % of market value.
- The state therefore collects on roughly half of its real‑estate base, forcing reliance on high income taxes.
- The billionaire wealth tax is a desperate add‑on that does not address the underlying property‑tax distortion.

## Comparison with other states
- Florida is moving to eliminate its property tax and replace it with a higher sales tax, shifting from a non‑distorting tax to one that taxes everyday purchases.
- California is adding a new tax on mobile wealth while its property‑tax system remains broken.
- Both states overlook the stable, large tax base that land provides.

## Conclusion
- The most reliable, abundant, and immobile source of revenue for California is the land itself.
- A modest land‑value tax would generate needed funds, target wealth fairly, and avoid the pitfalls of chasing mobile billionaires.
