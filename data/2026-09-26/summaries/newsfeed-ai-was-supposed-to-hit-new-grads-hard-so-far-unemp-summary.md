# AI was supposed to hit new grads hard. So far, unemployment data says otherwise.

## Background
- Earlier Stanford study suggested entry‑level jobs in “AI‑impacted” occupations were lagging behind other fields.  
- CESifo (Munich) released a working paper titled *The Early Impacts of AI on Employment Among Recent College Graduates* to test whether recent graduates are facing higher unemployment because of AI.  
- Researchers Robert Fairlie and Jane Wu focused on graduates because hiring reductions would appear before layoffs of experienced workers.  

## Key Findings
- Summer 2026 unemployment for recent college graduates (ages 22‑25) was **7.3 %**, within the historical range of 6.3 % (2022) to 7.8 % (2024).  
- Including those “wanting a job” but not actively looking did not change the picture.  
- Statistical comparisons with non‑college peers (same age) and older college graduates (30‑49) showed no significant differences in unemployment trends from 2022‑2026.  
- Breakdown by “AI exposure” (based on a 2023 AI‑readiness study) also revealed no statistically significant impact.  

## Comparison with Stanford Study
- Stanford analysis used payroll data from ADP, covering job supply but not necessarily demand.  
- CESifo used Census Current Population Survey microdata, which captures both supply and demand through unemployment rates.  
- The differing data sources may explain the opposite conclusions.  

## Caveats & Future Outlook
- The 2026 data is a “first test” and does not guarantee future trends.  
- AI adoption is accelerating: recent Census surveys show many firms replacing large numbers of tasks with AI, and AI spending per employee has risen sharply.  
- Researchers warn that graduates from 2027 onward could face higher displacement if AI intensity continues to grow.  
- Ongoing data collection will be needed to detect emerging effects.
