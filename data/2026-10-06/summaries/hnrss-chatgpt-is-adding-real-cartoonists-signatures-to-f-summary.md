# ChatGPT is adding real cartoonists’ signatures to fake New Yorker cartoons

## Background
- A AI‑generated cartoon depicting Dolly Parton and Tim Curry went viral after the entertainers’ deaths, receiving tens of thousands of likes across social platforms.  
- The image was posted by a fan who asked ChatGPT to create “a New Yorker‑style cartoon.”  
- The cartoon displayed the pen name **BLOPER**, belonging to New Yorker cartoonist Brendan Loper, although Loper did not create or sign it.

## Impact on Cartoonists
- Loper began receiving messages, emails, and DMs from strangers asking if the image was his work, describing the experience as “very surreal.”  
- Earlier in May, Loper was warned that his signature had appeared in a ChatGPT output; later, similar examples surfaced on Reddit.  
- Other New Yorker contributors whose signatures have been reproduced include Harry Bliss, Emily Flake, Joe Dator, Pat Byrnes, Peter Vey, Jason Adam Katzenstein, George Booth, Liza Donnelly, Ellis Rosen, Saul Steinberg, and Warren Miller.  
- Artists describe the signatures as “certificates of authenticity” and view the unauthorized use as a violation of their personal and brand identity.

## OpenAI’s Response
- OpenAI’s statement emphasized a focus on tools that empower human creativity and thanked the community for flagging bugs.  
- After being notified, ChatGPT began displaying a guardrail warning (“This prompt may violate our guardrails concerning similarity to third‑party content”) when asked for New Yorker‑style cartoons, but it still occasionally adds real cartoonists’ signatures.  
- Tests showed the model can produce cartoons with the magazine’s visual style, though punchlines often miss the mark.

## Legal and Licensing Context
- In 2024, Condé Nast (owner of The New Yorker) signed a multi‑year licensing deal with OpenAI, but the terms do not grant permission to train models on the magazine’s cartoons.  
- The New Yorker’s standard cartoonist contracts do not allow licensing for AI training; cartoonists retain copyright and are paid only if their work is selected for publication.  
- OpenAI declined to explain how its models might have ingested signatures, leaving the possibility that they were scraped from publicly available sources.

## Conclusion
- ChatGPT is capable of generating New Yorker‑style cartoons and attaching the authentic signatures of real contributors without consent or compensation.  
- This practice raises concerns about intellectual‑property rights, personal attribution, and the adequacy of existing licensing agreements between publishers and AI developers.  
- Ongoing monitoring and clearer legal frameworks are needed to protect creators’ identities and ensure responsible AI use.
